THE THIELE MACHINE — FULL EDITORIAL AUDIT OF PROOF-TOOL EMPHASIS
Manuscript by Devon Thiele. Review proposals only. No manuscript changes applied.

The first ten suggestions caught the most visible framing. The same pattern also appears
in the personal account, chapter transitions, the explanation of proof checking, the
implementation chapter and the appendices. This pass follows it through the entire book.

There are 58 grouped findings containing 74 exact replacements.
The original ten proposals are included unchanged and identified by their previous numbers.
Most replacements are short cuts or changes of emphasis. The first-person changes, two
heading changes and shortened name-history paragraph are explicitly marked VOICE CHOICE.
They are proposals for your judgment, just like the rest; no one has applied them.

Editorial principle: the argument carries the claim. The formalization is a companion
that can expose gaps in a specified derivation. Tool acceptance does not determine the
importance of the work, choose its definitions, validate its interpretation or confer
novelty. Conversely, having no separate formal counterpart does not refute an argument.

Scope and method: the complete 23,288-line manuscript was searched, from preamble through
the last bibliography entry. The review inventoried 319 tool/foundation-related paragraphs
and screened 294 additional candidate passages for the same framing without tool names.
Relevant passages were read in context, with follow-up reads around ambiguous statements.
The section coverage and dispositions are recorded in coverage.txt and passage-dispositions.tsv.
This is a manuscript-wide editorial audit of this issue, including nearby accuracy and
consistency problems. It is not a fresh verification of every mathematical derivation,
every historical citation or every claim of originality in the 282,043-word TeX source.

WHAT THE FULL PASS ADDS

1. Remove shared-checker endorsements from historical comparisons.
2. Stop treating compilation as the central accomplishment or the identity of the author.
3. Remove the repeated “and it is checked” refrain where the argument and source already follow.
4. Correct claims of certainty, infallibility, automatic totality and independence that the
   described tools or tests do not establish.
5. Make the checking chapter serve the argument; shorten the tool-name digression if you
   agree with that voice choice.
6. Let the appendices document the work without making source syntax the arbiter of all prose.
7. Correct stale descriptions where the book and its current companion sources disagree.

WHAT SHOULD STAY

“Gap-catcher. Not the proof. The proof is the argument.” Keep that paragraph exactly.
Keep theorem statements, proofs, hypotheses, mathematical definitions, formal source blocks,
source identifiers, genuine limitations, reproducibility commands, and attribution of prior work.
Keep the distinction between the machine's CHECK instruction and the external proof checker.
Keep the real-number assumptions and their per-result accounting; deleting them would hide
dependencies rather than improve the emphasis. Keep the honest distinction between runs that
were tested and constructions whose behavior is proved but which have never been run.
Keep the classification of contributions as known mathematics, synthesis, implementation or
mechanization where that is the actual contribution. Changing “mechanized” to “new” would
misrepresent the work. This audit recommends no such change.
Keep the historical discussions when they explain the machine or an argument. The particular
name-history cut below is a judgment about focus, not a ban on historical digressions.
Gallina does not occur by name in the current manuscript.

E16. The checker’s proof outside the machine

Location: The logic / The diagonal / What this does to the book

Type: Accuracy and emphasis

WHY: This paragraph says the proof cannot be moved inside and invokes Gödel and Löb as the reason. Later the book explicitly says keeping it outside is a design choice and that these theorems do not forbid checking proofs about checkers. Bring the earlier passage into agreement with that narrower statement.

Source line 9628

CURRENT:

Push on that and it gives, because a receipt is only as good as whoever wrote it. Its trust rests on the checker meaning what it says, and that gets settled once, from outside the machine. I don't think it can be moved inside, and Gödel's second theorem is my reason. The theorem says a consistent system strong enough for arithmetic can't prove its own consistency, and a checker vouching for itself would be doing something close to that. That last step is my reading. The theorem itself stops at consistency. So I didn't get rid of the receipt somewhere. I moved it out of the machine and handed it to whoever reads the record. Try to move that proof inside the system, so it can certify its own checker or a successor's, and you hit exactly the wall Eliezer Yudkowsky and Marcello Herreshoff named the Löbian obstacle in 2013, writing about agents that want to trust their own rewrites. As I understand their draft report, they came at it from the far side, an agent wanting to trust a successor's proofs the way it trusts its own. Same arrow, pointed forward, same wall.

PROPOSED:

Push on that and it gives, because a receipt is only as good as whoever wrote it. Its trust rests on the checker meaning what it says, and that gets settled once, from outside the machine. That's where I chose to stop this machine's chain of reasons. Gödel's second theorem does not forbid a machine from checking a proof about a checker. It limits a sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatized theory's ability to prove its own consistency, if the theory is consistent. Eliezer Yudkowsky and Marcello Herreshoff's 2013 draft on agents that trust their own rewrites describes a related problem, the Löbian obstacle: how an agent can justify trust in a successor's proofs. The relation is worth examining, but it does not prove that my design is forced. So I didn't get rid of the receipt somewhere. I moved it out of the machine and handed it to whoever reads the record.

E36. The name-and-renaming digression

Location: Checking it, and its edge / What Coq is, and why

Source line 16093

CURRENT:

The name is a joke three ways at once, and it's worth knowing before you say it out loud in English. Coq is French for rooster. It's also a nod to Thierry Coquand, a French computer scientist and mathematician, at the University of Gothenburg as of 2026, who worked out the logic it started from with Gérard Huet, a French computer scientist at INRIA, and to that logic's initials, CoC, for the Calculus of Constructions; and there's a French habit of naming research tools after animals. The English slang reading did what you'd guess. By the development team's own account, some people, women students especially, ended up never learning the tool because of its name. So in March 2025 it got a new one, the Rocq Prover, after Rocquencourt, the Inria site where it was first built, and after the Roc, the giant bird of legend, so the bird stayed. Everything in this book was checked with Coq 8.18, from before the rename, so that's the name I use. The rooster changes nothing about what got checked.

PROPOSED:

The companion formalization uses Coq 8.18. The project was renamed the Rocq Prover in March 2025; this book uses the older name to identify the version its sources were checked with.

E38. What an accepted derivation establishes

Location: Checking it, and its edge / What Coq is, and why / Types as claims

Type: Accuracy and emphasis

WHY: Compilation can succeed with an admitted statement, and a vacuous claim is not literally meaningless. Explain acceptance, assumptions and the translation problem accurately, without making either compilation or its rejection the definition of truth.

Source line 16135

CURRENT:

The compiler checks whether the types line up. If your proof has a hole, it rejects the proof, unless you write “trust me” (the keyword is Admitted), and then it marks the theorem as unproved. But here's the part that matters: the compiler checks that the proof matches the statement. It does not check that the statement says what you meant. Hand it a statement that means nothing and it'll prove it for you, three characters, no complaint. So a proof being accepted is not the same as the thing being true. It means this proof matches this statement, and whether that's the right statement is on me. The machine can't tell. The point said a short version of this and I'm saying it again because every section since has leaned on it: the checked names in this book are a promise that the proof is there, and the sentences around them are my claim about what the statement says, and the second can be wrong while the first is fine, which is why so many sections of this book stop to say exactly what a statement's hypotheses are.

PROPOSED:

The checker tests a proposed derivation against its statement, under the definitions and assumptions it uses. Admitted skips the derivation and turns the statement into an assumption; the file can still compile. That is why a successful build alone does not show that every claimed result has been proved. Even a complete derivation can settle the wrong question. A statement can be true for an empty reason, such as a condition nothing satisfies. Whether it expresses what I meant remains my responsibility. The point said a short version of this: the source names identify formal statements and their derivations, and the sentences around them are my account of what those statements establish. Comparing the two means reading the definitions, the premises and the conclusion. The checker does not do that reading for us.
