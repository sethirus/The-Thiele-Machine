1. reset_full_state
Reason: Separate the two-state reset, whole-state collision, actual input distribution and physical heat at the first explanation.

BEFORE
Making a record on a blank place is one operation. Landauer's 1961 paper names another, and it's
the one this book ends up charging. Take one bit whose value you don't know and set it to one,
whatever it held. Landauer's name for that is ``restore to one,'' and it's the reset he
described, two possible situations made into one, the kind of step he said has to give off
heat. Now raise a flag that stays up, on a machine that might be holding the flag down or might
be holding it up already. Down goes to up, up stays up, and the two become one. That's restore
to one. So the step the one-bit toll charges is Landauer's restore to one, and Landauer priced
it, in heat, as a reset. What I can claim is the narrower thing: which premises about a machine (a finite memory, a flag that stays
up) make the certifying step a reset, so that Landauer applies to it. That's the next section,
and Section~\ref{sec:landauer} comes back to it with the numbers.

AFTER
Making a record on a blank place is one operation. Landauer's 1961 paper names
another: set a bit to one whatever it held. If that bit is the whole state, down
goes to up, up stays up, and two possible states become one. Landauer called this
``restore to one.'' On an uncertain input it removes information; a heat bound
then needs the thermodynamic premises.

A larger machine has more state than its flag. Two starts can both finish with the
flag up while remaining different elsewhere. The next section proves that, on a
finite machine whose reading is permanent, any instruction that raises it must
merge some full states. It does not say the newly certified state is one of that
pair. Put one chair outside the certified room and two inside. Send the outsider
to the first inside chair, and both insiders to the second. The flag rises for the
outsider, but the two insiders are the states that merge. These arrows describe
alternative starts, not three things happening in one run.

That is the distinction the one-bit picture hides. The instruction has a collision
somewhere; the actual distribution of starting states determines whether that
collision removes uncertainty. A known starting state loses none under the entropy
premise, even when its flag rises (Theorem~\ref{thm:known-state}). The ledger can
still charge the instruction under merge pricing. A ledger charge, information
loss and a device's heat are three quantities to keep apart.

5. pricing_strength_before
Reason: Correct prose implying equivalence between pairwise merge pricing and full entropy/halving pricing.

BEFORE
So there are three roads to the toll: merge pricing, the halving price, and entropy pricing. They
don't compete. They're one premise written three ways, and that's checked too. Ask of a move the
most bits it can remove from any spread at all, which is Landauer's principle taken in its worst
case. That most is exactly the base-two logarithm of the move's worst pile-up: no spread loses
more, and the even spread on the biggest pile loses exactly that much. A cost in whole marks that
is at least that logarithm is a cost $c$ with no pile bigger than $2^c$, and that's the halving
price. Merge pricing is the same premise asked about two states at a time.

AFTER
There are three roads to the toll, with two different strengths. Entropy pricing
and the halving price are equivalent: the most bits a deterministic move can remove
is the base-two logarithm of its largest pile of starts sharing a destination.
Merge pricing asks only for at least one mark whenever any pair collides. It is
the two-state restriction of the stronger conditions.

Try eight equally likely cards all sent to one box. The loss is three bits. A
charge of one meets merge pricing but fails entropy pricing and the halving price,
which require at least three. All three conditions give the permanent-record toll;
only the stronger two give the full squeeze bound.

23. entitlement_check_payment
Reason: Separate missing verification from missing payment; neither alone establishes the other.

BEFORE
Without the third part, the object may be sitting right there and the claim may be true, and
the run still hasn't \emph{earned} it. That's the cups: the coin is under cup one; the reason is
disconnected from it; nobody lifted the cup. Put it on Section~\ref{sec:toll}'s sheet of paper, the one with the
NO box, the YES box and the tally line, and grow the toy by one thing: the state is now the box
you're in plus whatever is written on the sheet beside it. Next to the boxes, write a note:
``coin under cup one, checked.'' Writing the note is a WORK move, and WORK costs nothing. The note is an object, it's
on the paper, and it might even be true. What it doesn't have is a crossing on the record: no
move took the reading from NO to YES when the note went down, and there's no mark under the
line for it. The note is raw data, forged in one write, and the five parts allow a system that
lets you write one, because nothing in them says what an ordinary move may put in
the state. A note put down by the move that crosses has a receipt, and the receipt is the
event. One limit on that word ``receipt'': the crossing records that something was paid for at
that moment. What the crossing move actually looked at before it crossed is up to whatever
the move does, and the five parts don't say.

AFTER
Keep the second and third parts separate. A note saying ``coin under cup one, checked''
can be written without anyone lifting a cup. That gives an object, but no checked relation.
Now actually lift the cup, find the coin and write the result. Suppose this is a free move:
the note is on the sheet, the check really happened, but the NO box stays NO and the tally
line stays blank. This time the object and the checked relation are both there. What is
missing is the paid event. The run isn't entitled in this definition's sense; that doesn't
make the result of the check false.

The reverse distinction matters too. A move from NO to YES earns a mark on the tally line,
but the bare toll says nothing about what that move looked at. Payment alone supplies
neither a check nor its correctness. The later machine will bind those acts together in its
step rule. The five parts of a certification system do not do that by themselves.

27. weighted_entropy_scope
Reason: State positive logarithm argument, normalization and attainability condition.

BEFORE
Take any spread of non-negative weights over the states, uniform or not. (1) If every landing pile
has at most $N$ states, the conditional entropy of the state given its landing spot is at most the
total weight times $\log_2 N$ bits. (2) So on a machine with finitely many states and the halving price, the average
information a move destroys is at most its cost in bits, times the weight. (3) The bound is reached:
the even spread on a pile of $2^c$ states loses exactly $c$ bits.

AFTER
Take any spread of non-negative weights over the states, uniform or not. (1) If every landing pile
has at most $N$ states, with $N\ge1$, the weighted conditional entropy of the state given its landing spot is at most the
total weight times $\log_2 N$ bits. (2) So on a machine with finitely many states and the halving price, that weighted
information loss is at most its cost in bits times the total weight. For a probability distribution,
the total weight is one and the average loss is at most the cost. (3) If a landing pile has $2^c$ states,
the uniform probability distribution on it loses exactly $c$ bits and attains that bound.

41. time_symmetric_physics
Reason: Primary paper equations 28–30 give input-weighted low-error work scaling, with time-reversal assumptions; avoid exact universal heat claim.

BEFORE
The generalized principle can charge a known state nothing because it lets the step be driven by
a protocol that changes in time: lower this barrier, tilt that well, raise the barrier again,
each at the right moment. Something outside the step keeps that time, an outside clock or an
outside hand, and the premise doesn't bill it. Take the hand away and the bill changes. Paul
Riechers, Alexander Boyd, Gregory Wimsatt and James Crutchfield (2020) analysed what happens
when the control is \emph{time-symmetric}, the same played forwards or backwards, which covers a clocked pulse and any device
whose rates never change. On their analysis, every move that doesn't come back to
where it started when you apply it twice, which they call \emph{nonreciprocal}, costs at least
$k_B T \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ on top of the change in free energy, to run with error at most
$\varepsilon$. A flip that stays up is always nonreciprocal: apply it twice from the no-state
and you're still at yes. So, if they're right, under that kind of
control the known-state stamp isn't free after all, and the more reliable you want it, the more it costs. Their class is bigger than mine,
though. Erasure is nonreciprocal, and so is a counter of three or more values that steps forward
and wraps around, which merges nothing, so their bound is about what the
controls are allowed to do, records or no records. So read Theorem~\ref{thm:known-state} with its control written in:
Landauer's premise sets no positive heat floor for a known state when the step can be driven by a protocol timed
from outside it. The same goes for Theorem~\ref{thm:forced-iff-merge}. On a machine whose move names can be decided equal or different,
the moves every
merge-pricing schedule must charge are exactly the merging moves, and that's true against merge
pricing; measured against time-symmetric control, the forced moves would be, on this argument
and unchecked, the nonreciprocal
ones, the wrapping counter included. Which moves the physics forces a price on depends on what
the controls can do.

AFTER
The entropy-loss bound supplies no positive floor for a known input. Whether a device
can approach that floor also depends on its controls: lower this barrier, tilt that well,
raise the barrier again. Those operations have a timing and a physical implementation.
The entropy calculation alone does not price either.

Paul Riechers, Alexander Boyd, Gregory Wimsatt and James Crutchfield (2020) analyse
metastable memories under \emph{time-symmetric} control: the prescribed control is the
same played forwards or backwards, with the relevant time-reversal conventions. A
symmetric clock pulse can meet that restriction; an arbitrary clocked pulse need not.
For memory states unchanged by time reversal, their low-error result distinguishes
inputs $m$ for which applying the desired logical map $C$ twice fails to return to $m$.
They call those transitions \emph{nonreciprocal}. With equal local-equilibrium free
energies, the leading minimum-work term as the error allowance $\varepsilon$ tends to
zero is proportional to
\[
k_B T\ln(1/\varepsilon)\,
\Pr\bigl(C(C(m))\ne m\bigr).
\]
The probability is over the initial memory states. This is a low-error scaling with
physical premises, not an exact finite-error heat charge on every execution.

A permanent flip from no to yes is nonreciprocal at that no-state: a second application
cannot return it to no. A known input at that state can therefore face the extra work
cost in their restricted control setting, even though its initial logical entropy is zero.
Their class also includes a three-state cycle, which merges nothing. The distinction is
about the permitted controls as well as the logical map. It neither changes
Theorem~\ref{thm:known-state}'s entropy calculation nor identifies the book's abstract
merge price with a universal physical bill.

48. absolute_irreversibility_scope
Reason: A deterministic logical map and matching logarithm do not specify thermodynamic reverse histories or absolute-irreversibility parameter.

BEFORE
Physicists have a name for a step with no way back, and that logarithm has turned up in their
work before. Y\^uto Murashita, Ken Funo and Masahito Ueda (2014) studied processes where some
histories of the reversed process have no forward history at all. They call that
\emph{absolute irreversibility}, and they derived that the entropy
such a process produces averages at least $-\ln(1-\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the chance of those orphaned reverse
histories. Their first example is a gas let out of a box that held a fraction $l$ of the room,
where the bound comes to $\ln(1/l)$. Now picture the even spread of my flip played backwards:
the chance sitting on the $m$ yes-states spreading back out over all $m+k$, a gas let out of a
box holding $m/(m+k)$ of the room. Their bound for that is $\ln\frac{m+k}{m}$, the same
logarithm as the heat floor, in units of $k_B T$. So is my squeeze a special case of their
bound? No, and the reason is worth a minute, because the match is a good trap. The even spread
gives a real chance to every state the flip starts from, so no forward history is missing: the
flip itself isn't absolutely irreversible at all, and their $\lambda$ for it is zero. The heat
floor is ordinary Landauer heat, the bill for squeezing a spread over $m+k$ states into $m$ of
them, the same kind of bill as any reset into a smaller set. The two logarithms match because a
squeeze's Landauer heat equals the entropy produced by the free expansion that undoes it, and
that free expansion, the flip played backwards, is the process their bound is about. A mirror.
Neither result descends from the other. Their idea does reach the flip, just somewhere else: on a device
whose rates never change, raising the flag from a state it knows, which turns up at the end of this
section. Gonzalo Manzano, G\"ulce Karde\c{s}, \'Edgar Rold\'an and David Wolpert (2024) carried
absolute irreversibility over to computations, with one-way transitions and machines that stop
at random times, worked on finite automata, and the same $-\ln(1-\lambda)$ term sits in their
second-law bounds.

AFTER
A related logarithm appears in a different physical question. Y\^uto Murashita,
Ken Funo and Masahito Ueda (2014) study processes whose reverse histories can include
ones with no forward counterpart. They call this \emph{absolute irreversibility}.
If those reverse histories have total probability $\lambda$, their bound on mean
entropy production contains $-\ln(1-\lambda)$. In their free-expansion example, gas
initially confined to a fraction $l$ of a box gives $\ln(1/l)$.

Setting $l=m/(m+k)$ gives the same logarithm as the squeeze bound. That numerical
match does not identify two physical processes. Our arrows specify neither a
thermodynamic reverse experiment nor its path probabilities, so they do not determine
$\lambda$. We cannot infer absolute irreversibility, its absence, or a heat equality
from the state count alone. Gonzalo Manzano, G\"ulce Karde\c{s}, \'Edgar Rold\'an and
David Wolpert (2024) study such trajectory-level questions for computations with
one-way transitions and random stopping times. Their physical premises are additional
to the counting done here.

And one limited piece of the chances-and-time-spans version, set in continuous time and checked

because it shows exactly where the known-state escape stops. Take a device with no outside hand
at all: a little random process hopping among finitely many states at rates that never change,
with the usual physical conditions (every hop has a reverse hop, and the rates obey local
detailed balance), able to get from any state to any other. Left to run, it settles into a

chances of up and down stand in the ratio of the mean stretch lengths, because the crossings up
and the crossings down balance, so $-\ln\pi(\mathrm{no}) = \ln\bigl(1 + T_{\mathrm{up}}/T_{\mathrm{down}}\bigr)$.
