THE THIELE MACHINE
Devon Thiele


THE POINT

Take anything that can be more than one way and isn't yet any of them for
good: a bead that could sit in either of two dips, a square of paper with
nothing on it, a question nobody has closed. Now let it settle one way, and
keep it there. Something is the case now that wasn't a moment ago. That is a
distinction that stays, and it is what this is about. Nothing becomes
something for free: under a rule that gives every state exactly one next
state, keeping something settled takes a merge somewhere in the run, two
states landing on one, or an endless supply of fresh states.

Structure is made of exactly this. The smallest distinction there is sorts the
ways a thing could be in two and holds one side: a bead in the left dip and
not the right, a mark on the page and not off it. A grouping, an order, a
relation a later move leans on, a fact somebody found out and wrote down: each
is a distinction that stays, paid for the same way as the bead in its dip.
Insight, in the sense I can price, is a kept distinction. The toll requires a
charge when it's raised; merely remaining up requires no further charge.
Repeating a priced move still pays that move's price. That is what the other
name for the rule, No Free Insight, means.

Established is the sharpest case. Two computers can end with the same answer,
and only one of them checked it. The difference between them is a distinction
one of them never kept. A record that a claim was checked and closed is a
distinction that stays, with a check in front of it, and it's paid for the
same way. The point is the distinction that stays, and what keeping it takes.


WHAT A KEPT ANSWER REQUIRES

Give a thing one rule for its next tick, and require a next tick everywhere.
It starts unsettled, then enters a collection of states where the answer is
held. From any state in that collection, the next tick must stay in the
collection too.

Suppose there are K such holding states. Include the unsettled entrant and you
have K+1 sources whose next states fit in K places. Two arrows must land
together. That is the merge.

What if the rule never sends different sources to the same destination? Then
the holding run cannot repeat. From two equal visits, walk one step back on
each: their predecessors must agree, since different predecessors never land
together. Walk back until the earlier visit reaches the unsettled start. The
later visit is still holding the answer. They cannot be the same state. So
every later holding tick needs another state.

For a finite interval, K holding states permit at most K transitions from
distinct sources without a collision, starting at the unsettled state. This
maximum is attainable. Draw

  u -> h_1 -> h_2 -> ... -> h_K -> h_K.

The first K sources have different successors. Include h_K as a source and its
arrow meets the arrow from its predecessor. With K>1 this happens after entry:
the merge need not involve the state that just came in.

That is counting, and it needs no price and no physics. The definition is
where the price goes.


THE DEFINITION

A Thiele machine is a thing, the directions it can move in, and a price on
each direction. A direction turns the thing into the next thing. Look at the
thing and you can read off how much is settled in it. A direction that makes a
distinction stay is never free. From where the thing starts, some run of
directions makes one stay, and no direction undoes it.

That is the whole definition. What follows gives its consequences and
instances, the counting that motivates it, and the argument for choosing it.

Now the same thing with names. S is the set of states and I the set of moves.
The step takes a state and a move to the next state. Each move i has a
nonnegative whole-number price c(i), the same wherever it is taken. A reading
r sends each state to a place in an ordered set: for any two places you can
ask whether one sits at or below the other. A move makes a distinction stay
when it carries the reading somewhere not at or below where it was. The one
rule is the toll: such a move costs at least one. That much is a priced record
system.

A Thiele machine is a priced record system whose reading no move ever lowers,
and that some run, from a start you designate, raises. A reading that never
falls is what I call a record. Nothing else is assumed of the order, and
nothing says what the record is about. A ledger that only climbs, and a window
onto what an outside observer sees, come in when a result needs them.

The smallest record has two positions, no and yes. Then the reading is a
yes-or-no question about the state, making a distinction stay is turning no
into yes, and the toll says that move costs at least one. Most of what follows
is stated on this smallest case, because it is the one you can point at: a
lamp beside a box, a stamp on its side, a flag that goes up. The flag is not
the record. It is the smallest question you can ask of one. The proofs state
the general case first and derive the flag as its two-point instance.


THE SMALLEST MACHINE

The smallest Thiele machine has two states, no and yes, and one move that
costs one. The move sends no to yes and yes to yes. The reading is the state.
It can rise, it never falls, and the rise is priced. Nothing smaller meets the
definition: one state has a constant reading, and on two states read as
themselves every member has this very move, since a move on two states that
merges nothing either changes nothing or toggles, and a toggle turns yes back
to no.

Notice what is already inside it: two states land on one. The merge that
keeping takes is forced at the smallest size there is. A fuse, a bead that
drops into a dip, a stamp on a box, are this machine.

The Thiele Machine is that definition. It is a machine the way Turing's
machine is one: mathematics you can reason about, not a device, not an
instruction set and not a document. I named it after myself, on purpose. I
wanted to call it the meta machine, and in 2021 a company took that word, so I
went one level up and put my own name on it, half a joke and half a stake in
the ground. Turing got a machine; here's mine. What follows is what I work out
from it.


PUT THE PRICE BESIDE THE ARROWS

The toll on the smallest record is

  no -> yes implies price >= 1.

A run that starts at no and ends at yes contains a first crossing. That move
costs at least one, and the other prices are nonnegative. Add them and the run
costs at least one. This is the universal floor. It holds for every Thiele
machine, and for anything at all that obeys the toll. The toll on its own is a
low bar: a clock that charges one for every move obeys it whatever reading you
give it. That's why the toll isn't the whole definition.

Read a machine through a reading its moves never raise and the reading is
flat: the toll asks nothing of it, because nothing is kept. A clock read by
where its hand points is no Thiele machine: the hand comes back around, so
that reading falls. Read through anything its moves never raise, a clock is
flat. So is a reversible process. So is ordinary computation, read as it
stands. A flat reading is not a Thiele machine, whatever start you name. Flat
is a property of the reading, not of the machine.

The definition has to name the reading its toll belongs to. Slide a tile to
the right without paying and ask whether it is in the place it just reached:
the answer was no and is now yes, and if the toll charged every question, no
move could be free. And a free move cannot touch a reading that stays. It
cannot raise it, because a raise costs at least one, and it cannot lower it,
because a yes stays yes. It must leave it alone.

The price stays on the move. It does not disappear when you press the same
button with the reading already up, and on finitely many states the least
price list is the plain one: one on every move that can raise the reading from
somewhere, zero on every move that never does. Nothing smaller meets the toll.
That is a minimum in the definition's units; measuring a device's heat
requires a physical account of the device.

A ledger adds up the prices of the moves that ran. It does not count merges,
fresh states or heat, and its total need not equal the number of raises. An
injective run can visit fresh states at every step while raising its reading
only once. The toll sets a lower bound on the charge.

A stronger pricing rule can connect the bill to the holding graph. If every
instruction that merges distinct full states costs at least one, a permanent
raise into a finite held region pays by the counting argument above. If each
landing pile accepts at most 2^p distinct sources, N starting alternatives and
q surviving alternatives satisfy N <= 2^p q for one step. Across steps with
prices p_0,...,p_(T-1), multiply these inequalities:

  N <= 2^(p_0 + ... + p_(T-1)) q_T.

Write the pricing premise beside the bound. A natural-number ledger is not a
temperature or a heat measurement. A thermal conclusion needs the device,
input preparation and physical law stated too.


THE BOX AND THE PICTURE

Put two boxes on the table. The tiles on top spell the same answer. One box
has a stamp on its side and the other does not. Hang the camera above them.
The pictures agree.

Now ask which box was stamped. Look harder at the tiles. Count them. Make the
picture bigger. None of that puts the side of the box into the picture. The
difference was there when the picture was taken. The picture left it out.

Gather every full state that gives the same picture. The tiles agree, but the
stamps and whatever else the camera misses can differ. That collection is what
I call the fibre above the picture.

The letters give short names for what just happened. S names all the full
states, B names their pictures, and pi names the act of taking the picture.
Writing pi: S -> B says where that act starts and where it lands. Every
picture in B comes from a full state; that is what "onto" means. For one
picture b, its fibre contains every state whose picture is b.

A flag gives one yes-or-no reading of a full state. A hundred states can all
read yes. The fibre can hold differences in checked claims, certified
editions, old receipts, or anything else the chosen picture omits. There is no
universal finite bound on its size. If all choices of which of n stages had an
event are independently possible and exactly recoverable above one final
picture, they require 2^n full states there. Which distinctions must actually
be there is a question about the task and what must remain recoverable.


THE HORIZONTAL MOVES AND THE VERTICAL MOVES

Start with an ordinary working move. It changes the tiles by a rule that
consults only the tiles and the instruction you gave it. Whatever stamps are
on the side, the same tiles and the same instruction give the same next tiles.
The move also leaves the side record alone. That is the horizontal rule I
want.

Now shorten it. Call the full move F_i, its tile rule h_i, and the record
reading r. Starting from a full state s, either move the box and then
photograph it, or photograph it and apply the tile rule. The pictures must
agree:

  pi(F_i(s)) = h_i(pi(s)).

Keeping the record says r(F_i(s)) = r(s). These horizontal moves have zero
price on this ledger.

A vertical establishment move does the other job. The tiles stay put while the
record can rise. Call that move F_v. Taking the photograph before or after
gives the same picture:

  pi(F_v(s)) = pi(s).

The record never goes backwards: r(s) <= r(F_v(s)). For a yes-or-no flag, that
means a down flag may rise and an up flag stays up. The toll charges at least
one unit when it rises. A machine split this way, with some vertical move that
raises the record from a start I designate, is a sliced Thiele machine.

Put a dial with four positions beside a slider with three stops. ROTATE moves
the dial one position around for free and leaves the slider alone. ADVANCE
moves the slider toward its last stop, leaves the dial alone and costs one. At
the last stop the reading is yes; at the other two it is no. ADVANCE pays when
it first reaches yes and still costs one when pressed at the last stop. The
price belongs to the button. Look only at the dial: ROTATE moves the picture
and ADVANCE leaves it alone.

Take two boxes with the same tiles and different records. Give them the same
sequence of horizontal instructions. The first instruction sees the same
tiles, so it produces the same next tiles. Neither record changes. The second
instruction again sees matching tiles, and gives matching tiles. Repeat to the
end. The entire film agrees while the records remain different. Handing that
film to someone cannot tell them which record was there. The view cannot see
the record or the ledger, and no function of the view recovers either. An
account that prices a raise exactly cannot compute the price from a window
that shows a raising step and a quiet step the same way.

That gives the shadow. Look at a Thiele machine through a window that drops
its record and what's left is an ordinary machine, computing away, blind to
what was kept. It runs the other way too: any machine whose states I can tell
apart, with a run that ends somewhere other than where it started, is what
some Thiele machine looks like through such a window. The proof builds one: a
bit beside the state that goes up the first time the run reaches a chosen
state and never comes down, with that move priced. The ordinary machine is the
shadow, not a member. The ordinary computer is one such shadow. The record
adds no computing power; what it adds is distinctions within a fibre, which
the window cannot see.


A RECORD THAT MEANS SOMETHING

Write an answer on a whiteboard. Under it write CHECKED = YES. Somebody who
did no check can write the same two lines. The writing alone cannot tell me
which happened.

A variable called checked has the same weakness if any allowed program can set
it. To make an accepting record mean a check occurred, the machine must close
the unchecked route to acceptance. Suppose a checked and an unchecked history
could end at the same full state. The verdict at that state would have to
accept one history and reject the other. There is only one state there to
read. It cannot do both.

This is why representing a record and enforcing its meaning are separate jobs.
The representation keeps a distinction. The instruction rules govern how a
program can reach it. An implementation has to preserve both. Paying to raise
a record does not, by itself, establish what that record means.


LOOK BEFORE THE VALUE DISAPPEARS

Suppose one job used sound rebar and another used bad rebar. Pour the concrete
so that the finished surfaces look exactly alike. Give the inspector only
those surfaces. Whatever verdict the inspector gives one, the same evidence
gets the same verdict on the other. Looking harder cannot restore what the
surface omitted. Finding a sound arrangement that could have been underneath
proves a possibility; it does not show what was actually there.

If the question is known before the pour, inspect while the rebar is visible
and keep the answer. For a succession of required checks, start a flag at YES.
A passing check leaves it alone. A failing check turns it to NO, and nothing
in the remaining checks turns it back up. After one check it tells you whether
that check passed. After the next it stays YES only if both passed. Carry the
same rule to the end and it tells you whether all of them passed.

Late questions ask for something else. Suppose the inspector may arrive at the
end, name any stage, and ask any yes-or-no question about the value there. Two
different histories disagree at some stage. Ask whether that stage held the
value from the first history: one answer is yes and the other is no. A summary
that answers every such question cannot identify those histories.

If each of k stages can hold any of M values, the first stage gives M choices,
the second multiplies that by M, and each further stage does the same. There
are M^k ordered histories, and every one needs a different summary. Keeping
the ordered history itself meets that count and answers the questions.


WHAT FOLLOWS FROM IT

It is one story, in four parts, and each part says what it rests on.

1. The definition is more general than any mechanism. It assumes no tape, no
head and no counters. The universal floor holds for everything that obeys the
toll, and the theorems with teeth come when you add one next state for every
state (part 2) or a window (part 3). What I would defend as mine is not any
one of the counting facts below, which are old. It is the arrangement: one law
with three columns and no fourth, a fresh state, a merge, or a loop that makes
nothing new, stated alike for deterministic, noisy and quantum steps, and a
toll on a reading that stays, which charges for keeping whichever column pays
for it.

2. Keeping requires a merge or fresh states. This is counting under a total,
deterministic next-state rule and a reading held after entry; it uses no
physics. Before the first revisit each step is fresh: the first column. The
first revisit returns to the start or merges two distinct sources, the third
column or the second; later steps repeat. A run that raises a reading and
keeps it up cannot take the third column, because its start read no and every
later state reads yes, so a return to the start would bring the reading down.
That leaves the merge or endlessly many distinct states. A machine with
endlessly many states can avoid the merge on paper by paying in fresh states.
A machine with finitely many states cannot: when only finitely many states
read yes, a holding run must eventually merge. Whether a real device has
finitely many distinguishable states is a physical assumption, and I name it
as one. When a coarse description of a machine merges, the fine machine merges
too, unless its step leaves the described states or there are endlessly many
of them. If every merging instruction costs at least one, the toll on a finite
machine follows from the merge. A heat bound needs one more premise, a named
physical one, and I keep it apart from the counting.

3. The ordinary machine and the full machine, in both directions. In a sliced
machine, the view pi(S) = B with its horizontal moves is an ordinary
deterministic machine, and it cannot see the record or the ledger. That needs
a raise to exist, and nothing else: no premise about what the view computes
and none about the control. In the other direction, every machine with a run
that leaves its start, read as it stands, is the shadow of a Thiele machine
that keeps one bit above it.

4. What is argued and what is proved. The counting, the floor, the window
results and the flat reading results are theorems under the hypotheses they
state. The mathematical claims have their hypotheses and arguments on the
page. The pointer thesis is a thesis, not a theorem. It proposes certification
as the event worth recording, because certified records get copied and relied
on by parties who did not produce them. Separately, and unnumbered, I argue
that the record and its price are part of computation itself and not something
added afterwards, and I state one thesis about physical processes: that every
physical process which computes with final answers has an exact description as
a machine run by one rule, with a next state for every state, on which the
answer is a reading no run ever turns off. No proof settles that, because a
physical process is not a mathematical object. Neither is presented as a
theorem.


WHAT I FOUND, WHAT I ASSUMED AND WHAT I CHOSE

Most of the time I had the hunch first and found the names later. Over and
over, somebody had been here before me. Sometimes that made my day. Sometimes
it pissed me off. Here is the line between what nothing let me choose, what I
had to assume, and what I did choose.

Found, which counting forces on any machine. Run a machine that gives each
state exactly one next state. It takes fresh states until it first comes back
to one it has used; that return lands on its start or is a merge, two states
with one next state; and after it the run only repeats steps it already took.
There is no fourth thing a step can do. A run reveals at most one pair of
distinct visited states with the same successor; later laps can repeat that
transition. So a reading that goes from no to yes and never comes back is paid
for in a merge or in fresh states, and when only finitely many states read
yes, the move that raises it merges. Over any machine at all, a record that
the computation drives and that never comes down is a latch on one event. That
is counting, and the pigeonhole in it is older than the computer: Dedekind's
finiteness, Zermelo's recurrence objection to Boltzmann, Landauer and Bennett
on merging, and the floor Tarjan's potential method puts under a run.

Assumed, what the found list is taken to stand on in the world. Landauer's
principle: a merge of states the machine really could have been in has a floor
under its heat, however slowly it is done. That is the premise from
thermodynamics, named and never proved here; the monograph names its critics.
A real device can tell apart only finitely many states in a bounded place, so
its fresh states run out; nobody has measured where that ceiling sits. And the
clean start: a raised record means the event happened only because the record
was down at the start, and that start is put in by hand. It is a past
hypothesis in miniature, Boltzmann's idea under David Albert's name.

Chosen, what the model adds, each of which could have gone another way. To put
the account inside the step, as part of what a step is. The reading is part of
the definition, and a reading that some run raises and none lowers is what
makes a system a member. A computing part that cannot change the record is a
further restriction chosen for the machines that need it; the toll on its own
requires neither it nor permanence. A rule preserved by every permitted step
holds of every program built from those steps. A log maintained by one program
needs that program's correctness argument. The toll itself is a choice: a
whole unit, at least one, on the move that climbs. So are which readings
matter, which event gets a record, what price list it pays, and, on any
particular machine, where its flat part ends and its record begins.


THE WIDER ARGUMENT

A growing ordered record has threshold readings: ask whether a particular
level has been reached, and once the answer becomes yes it stays yes. I follow
those latches, study the recursion theorem with its input conventions, and
make cost-framework comparisons under their stated rules.

The count in part 2 does not need the step to be deterministic. A noisy step
on finitely many states that keeps the even spread cannot raise a reading that
stays, and one that can raise it strictly concentrates the spread. A unitary
step on a finite-dimensional space that keeps a subspace keeps its complement
too, so no such step raises a reading that stays. Each is the same obstruction
in its own setting, and endless room is the only way past it in each.

The protocol examples, including RFC 9162, concern specified parts of those
systems. An observer model says what a particular window reveals. The pointer
thesis is a thesis about how that picture connects to the world. Its proposed
interpretation and the mathematical implications of a chosen model have
different jobs, and I state both.


HOW TO READ THE ARGUMENT

Start with the bead in its dip. Follow the holding count, put the price beside
the arrows, and see the merge already inside the smallest machine. Then add
the camera: follow a move through the full state, look at what reaches the
window, and ask how many recoverable answers have to fit above one picture.
Each extra recoverable answer needs room in the full state.
