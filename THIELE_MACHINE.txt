THE THIELE MACHINE
A distillation: state, accounting, and what the proofs establish

Devon Thiele -- September 2026, v3.2.2

I didn't invent a machine. I found one.

That is my conviction. The Thiele Machine is how I put it on the table: definitions, executable rules, proofs, and a machine somebody else can run. The conviction is mine; the proof contracts are public.

A computation gives you an answer. I want to know what it established, which distinctions it preserved, and what it cost to establish them. Certification is one point I could pin down. The argument reaches beyond that point, but the proofs apply to the events, observations, and machines their statements actually describe.

The machine carries structural state and a cost ledger. Its rules charge particular events. Its observation theorems show when a view of that state loses information relevant to a query. If two states look the same through that view but require different answers, no function of the view alone can answer correctly on both.

That is a statement about a specified view. It does not prove that every computer loses this information. Ordinary memory can encode the full state, and a conventional transition rule can maintain the same accounting discipline. The comparison must name the state, permitted transitions, observation, and trusted implementation.

There is a second distinction throughout this account. Charging for a certification event does not establish that an arbitrary assertion is true. Generic CERTIFY sets a flag and charges; it checks no proposition. Other checks have their own soundness contracts. Calling the flag "certified" does not supply one.

I built this to be examined. What follows separates the construction, its proved consequences, and the interpretation I am asking people to consider.


1. SUBSTRATE

A computation is a sequence of steps. Each step takes a state and an instruction and produces the next state. To describe this precisely, name six things:

  S — states: whatever the machine remembers between steps.
  I — instructions: the actions the machine can perform.
  step — the step rule: given a state s and an instruction i, it produces the next state, written step(s, i).
  c — a cost function: every instruction has a non-negative integer cost, written c(i).
  P — a designated Boolean predicate on states: P(s) is 0 or 1. The VM has a certification field as one instance. In another system P may be computed from other state, such as the parity of a counter. A dedicated storage field is not required.
  mu — a cost ledger: each state carries a non-negative integer that starts at 0, and every step adds the cost of the instruction it ran, so mu(step(s, i)) = mu(s) + c(i). So mu is the running total of everything c has charged so far. Call this law conservation: cost is never lost and never conjured, and the ledger is exactly the sum of what was charged.

A substrate is the tuple (S, I, step, c, P), with every state carrying the ledger mu that the step rule maintains by conservation. Five pieces of structure, and one bookkeeping field that just adds them up.

These choices make the accounting explicit. A bare transition-system definition need not designate P, c, or mu. A particular conventional machine can nevertheless encode the state and maintain the chosen invariant. Turing equivalence addresses computable functions; it does not decide which accounting policy a model should specify.

The regression proof `counter_certification_system` uses a natural-number state, its parity as P, and unit step cost. `receipt_encoding_roundtrip` shows that ordinary list memory can retain a ledger and a certification value without loss. Both are in `coq/tests/ClaimBoundaryRegression.v`.


2. THE AXIOM

Call this rule A2:

  For every state s and every instruction i, if P(s) = 0 and P(step(s, i)) = 1, then c(i) >= 1.

In one sentence: any single step that flips certification from "no" to "yes" must cost at least 1. Equivalently, since mu just totals c: a step that flips certification on raises the ledger mu by at least 1. A2 constrains c on that one kind of step; conservation then carries the charge into the ledger, the way it carries every step's c.

A substrate that satisfies A2 meets this certification-cost policy. A substrate where some instruction can flip P from 0 to 1 at cost 0 permits free certification.


3. THEOREM: NO FREE INSIGHT

Claim. In any substrate satisfying A2, any run that starts uncertified (P = 0) and ends certified (P = 1) has total cost at least 1; that is, its ledger mu ends at least 1 above where it started.

Proof. The run's total cost is exactly mu at the end minus mu at the start, because by conservation each step added its own c to the ledger and nothing else touched it. So I only have to show that sum is at least 1. P starts at 0 and ends at 1, and P only ever reads 0 or 1, so it cannot get from 0 to 1 without some single step carrying it across: there is a step where P goes 0 -> 1. By A2 that step cost at least 1. Every other step cost at least 0, because costs are non-negative. A sum of non-negative numbers with one term at least 1 is at least 1.

The theorem holds for any substrate satisfying A2. It does not depend on what the states look like, what the instructions are, or what the step rule does. A2, conservation, and non-negative costs are enough.


4. REPRESENTATION AND ENFORCEMENT

A2 is a property of a step rule together with a designated predicate and cost function. Changing P or c can change whether A2 holds even when the underlying transitions stay the same. Naming those choices is necessary to state the property precisely.

It does not follow that a conventional machine cannot enforce A2. Encode the state, including P and mu, in ordinary memory, and choose transitions that implement the same certified-state updates and cost schedule. Those transitions can preserve A2. Establishing that a concrete implementation obeys them is an implementation proof or a trust assumption, just as it is for a realization of this VM.

A different transition rule that violates the policy is a different implementation. Its existence does not show that the first rule fails to enforce the policy. Nor does making P and mu separate fields by itself prevent an implementation from updating them incorrectly.

The observation question is separate. Define a projection that keeps memory, registers, and program counter while dropping the designated certification field and ledger. If two reachable states have the same projection and different certification values, certification cannot be recovered from that projection on every reachable state. That theorem concerns the chosen projection, not every possible memory encoding.

The more general observation theorem says exactly what is needed: a query is recoverable only if it is constant on each class of states with the same observation. With explicit representatives of those classes, the condition also constructs a decoder. A richer observation may satisfy this condition where a poorer one fails.


5. THE BUGGY SIMULATOR

Suppose a purported implementation changes its designated P from 0 to 1 while adding zero to its designated ledger. If it is supposed to obey A2 and ledger conservation, that transition is incorrect.

An A2-respecting abstract machine has no such transition among its permitted steps. This is a consequence of its specification. To claim that hardware enforces the same exclusion, one must establish that hardware execution follows the specification under the stated representation and implementation assumptions.

A conventional machine can implement those permitted steps too. It can also simulate descriptions of machines that violate A2 without violating its own accounting: the simulated flag is then data, not necessarily the host's designated predicate P. Confusing these two levels creates a false impossibility claim.

The practical question is which updates an implementation permits, and what evidence connects those updates to the claimed policy. The presence of a field or a type signature alone does not settle it.


6. LIFTING, PROJECTION, AND VERIFICATION

A classical transition system can be lifted into an accounting model: retain its state and transitions, let P remain false, and charge zero. A2 then holds vacuously. This embeds the original computation without adding computable functions. The VM supplies more operations as a particular realization of the broader construction.

Projection can lose information. The VM's chosen strict projection forgets the ledger, certification, and graph. Its separation proofs supply colliding witnesses with different answers to particular queries. This proves failure of a decoder on the whole stated domain; it does not imply that every observed state has multiple preimages.

Separate P and mu fields are convenient, but not a universal minimal-representation theorem. A different encoding can pack them into one value; in some systems one may be a function of other state. The query and admissible state space determine what must be retained.

A2 gives a lower bound, not a unique schedule. Charges greater than one are allowed, and steps without certification flips may also cost something. Exact unit pricing follows only after adding the separate requirement of no overcharge relative to certification-flip count. Several designated unit event floors can share one charge; independent coordinates alone do not require separate charges to add.

The verifier impossibility has a similarly precise scope. Suppose a transcript identifies two executions, one satisfying a mu-sensitive claim and the other not. Completeness requires acceptance on the first; strong soundness forbids acceptance when the second is still possible. No verifier of that transcript alone can meet both requirements.

The repository constructs three ways to provide sufficient additional evidence under their respective premises: access to the full state, a commitment under a hardness hypothesis, and an interactive response contract. The hardness construction has its stated weaker soundness guarantee. The constructions do not establish that these three labels exhaust all protocols, or that ordinary computing cannot retain the needed information. The collision premise must hold for the particular transcript under discussion.


7. WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT CLAIM

The construction makes a chosen accounting discipline explicit. The No Free Insight theorem lifts its local positive-charge law to a run. The observation results identify exactly when an interface loses the information needed to answer a query. These are mathematical results about the specified models.

They do not establish a new class of computable functions, an inability of conventional machines to enforce invariants, or a universal physical price for every structural change. The concrete ISA permits some structural changes at zero ledger cost.

The physical interpretation needs its own argument. In particular, `full_vm_f1_premises_incompatible` proves that the full ISA's zero-cost jumps contradict the stated pair of universal collapse-price and cost-calibration premises. A program counter collapsing to one does not by itself establish physical erasure: retaining the old state in a larger history can make each fixed-instruction step injective.

The hardware model's trace bridge follows actual fetched instructions, program counter, fuel, and state under `WFDrivenRun`. Generated RTL keeps the compiler/backend trust boundary named in the pipeline manifest. Synthesis, simulation, and FPGA bitstream generation supply additional evidence; they do not turn that boundary into a Coq proof.

The following sections state further results with their own premises. A2 alone does not supply all of those premises.


8. STRUCTURAL UNDECIDABILITY

Some substrates carry a recursion theorem and a non-trivial behavioral predicate on programs. Those admit the result below. The recursion theorem is Kleene's: any way of transforming programs that the substrate can itself carry out has a fixed point: a program whose behavior equals its own image under the transformation. This is an additional hypothesis; A2 alone does not provide a recursion theorem.

Fix such a predicate. The formal interface calls it `AdmitsShortcut`. Its interpretation must be supplied by the instance; the name alone does not identify it with Section 10's quantitative witness package. The argument works for any predicate satisfying the stated behavioral invariance and nontriviality conditions. Concretely it needs two things: the predicate respects extensional equivalence (programs with the same run-behavior get the same value), and the substrate fixes two canonical programs, one the predicate accepts and one it rejects.

Theorem. No internally representable decider correctly decides this predicate for arbitrary programs. Internally representable means the substrate can run the decider as one of its own programs.

Proof. Suppose D is such a decider. Build the flip transformer f: on input program p, run D(p); if it answers yes, output the canonical program the predicate rejects; if no, output the one it accepts. For the recursion theorem to apply, f must itself be one of the substrate's own programs. It is, on one mild assumption worth naming: that the substrate's programs are closed under "run a program, then branch to one of two fixed programs." This closure is a separate premise, not a consequence of merely being able to run D. Granting that, f is representable, the recursion theorem's side-condition, and the theorem hands back a fixed-point program q whose run-behavior equals that of f(q). Case-split on D(q). If D says q admits a shortcut, then by construction f(q) is the program that does not, and q behaves like f(q), so by extensionality q does not. D was wrong. If D says q does not, the same step makes q one that does. Either answer is wrong, so no such D exists.

This is the Kleene/Rice diagonal pattern expressed through an abstract program interface. The theorem is conditional on that interface's recursion, representability, closure, extensionality, and nontriviality premises. The VM instance retains its recursion-theorem premise; the repository also supplies an unconditional diagonal instance on its separate natural-number substrate.

The proof's not invoking a Turing-machine simulator does not establish non-reducibility to halting or Turing-degree incomparability. Such claims would require their own definitions and proofs. Nor should the undecidable predicate on arbitrary programs be confused with reading a designated boolean from a fully supplied concrete state.

The diagonal can hold on a model with mu always zero and A2 vacuous. Its contradiction uses the recursion theorem and extensionality, not a positive-cost argument. That is the content of this result.


9. COST LEDGER OBSERVABILITY

The VM supplies two reachable computations with the same specified classical observation and different ledgers. Consequently no function of that observation recovers the ledger on every reachable VM state.

Start at `init_state` with mu = 0 and certification false. One trace runs CERTIFY with delta = 0: its charge is 1 and it sets the flag. The other runs PNEW on an empty region with delta = 0: its charge is 0 and it changes the graph. Both advance the program counter by one and leave memory and registers unchanged. The projection retaining only those three components therefore agrees, while the ledgers differ.

The argument needs this instruction set, initial state, cost schedule, and observation. A2 supplies a positive floor for the certification flip, but it alone does not supply a free PNEW instruction or matching observations. This is not a separation theorem for every A2 system.

Allowing zero-cost certification in a modified schedule can remove this particular witness's cost gap. Merely dropping A2 as a hypothesis does not change the existing VM schedule or make every ledger separation disappear. A richer observation that retains mu distinguishes the two executions directly.

This is an information-loss result: the specified view cannot answer a query on which its colliding states disagree. It does not say that the ledger is unrepresentable in ordinary memory, or that paying the charge proves an arbitrary proposition.


10. QUANTITATIVE COST LOWER BOUND

Now a number to go with it. Section 9 showed the cost ledger separates substrate states a classical eye cannot tell apart. This one puts a floor under that cost, for one well-marked class of shortcut: on a sound structural shortcut the ledger mu rises by at least the drop in ceiling-log of the feasible list's length. It is not a bound on every decision trace.

Witness class. A "sound structural shortcut" (`SoundStructuralShortcut`) is an explicit package of receipts a shortcut must hand over to count here: a finite prior feasible list Omega (the candidates still in play before the shortcut runs); a strictly narrower posterior list Omega' (the ones still in play after); an observation function that tells at least one eliminated state from every surviving one; a certified posterior predicate; a BINARY (yes/no) decision tree; a posterior-representative reduction with a fiber bound; and a guarantee that the trace realizes the tree. That last receipt is the load-bearing one. It means precisely that the trace runs at least as many cert-setter instructions as the tree is deep. The members of this class are not "any decision procedure"; they are the ones that hand over the binary tree and that depth-many-cert-setters guarantee as explicit witnesses.

Theorem. For any sound structural shortcut with prior list Omega and nonempty posterior list Omega', the substrate trace that realizes the shortcut satisfies
  log_2^up |Omega| - log_2^up |Omega'| <= Delta mu,
where log_2^up n is the ceiling of log_2 n, |Omega| is a list length (duplicates counted), and Delta mu is the change in the ledger across the trace. The intuition for log_2^up n is the number of yes/no questions it takes to single out one of n things. That is only the intuition. It is not a count of questions the VM asked, and it is not Shannon's entropy of a distribution.

Proof sketch. Write L for the number of leaves of the tree T and d for its depth. Three facts compose.
First, a cover: |Omega| <= L * |Omega'|. Sort the prior entries by which posterior representative they land on. That makes |Omega'| groups, and the supplied fiber bound caps every group at L entries.
Second, take ceiling-log, using two of its properties. It is monotone, so the cover's <= carries through: log_2^up|Omega| <= log_2^up(L * |Omega'|). And it is sub-additive over a product: log_2^up(L * |Omega'|) <= log_2^up L + log_2^up|Omega'|. Put together, log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L. Because T is binary, it has at most 2^d leaves, so log_2^up L <= d.
Third, the cost. The realized-by-the-trace receipt is exactly the statement that the trace runs at least d cert-setters. In the VM every cert-setter costs at least 1, and by conservation the ledger is the sum of step costs, so Delta mu >= (cert-setters the trace runs) >= d.
Chain the three: log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L <= d <= Delta mu.

What this theorem does not claim. It does not claim that an arbitrary single trace is forced to pay log_2(n) for an n-way decision. A trace that does not hand over the realized-tree receipt carries no depth-many-cert-setters guarantee, so the chain has nothing to hang from: it might run a single cert-setter and the bound says nothing. The theorem applies exactly to traces that supply the binary tree, the fiber bound, and the guarantee that they ran at least depth-many cert-setters. An unconditional Shannon-style bound on every deterministic single trace is a different and stronger thing, and it is left open.

What this theorem does claim. When the narrowing is carried by a binary tree the trace realizes, the ledger mu pays at least the drop in ceiling-log of the feasible list, log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'|. The direction is the safe one: mu is lower-bounded, never claimed equal to anything, and never given physical units. The left side can even be 0 when the list got shorter but no ceiling-log dropped (4 down to 3 drops none). So the statement never overclaims the narrowing: it says the trace cannot pay less in mu than this floor, and the tree's depth, which the trace's cert-setters dominate, is what carries the floor up to mu. Compression is bounded below by paid certified observation.

What closes the chain. The last step needs every cert-setter to cost at least 1. That is the VM's cost floor, the concrete form of the charge A2 asks for. Without it, cert-setter steps could cost zero, their count would no longer lower-bound Delta mu, and the chain breaks at the last step. The earlier steps come from the supplied tree and fiber bound, not from any cost rule.


11. COMPARING TWO COST MODELS

The VM's payload-sensitive schedule charges `flen * 8 + S(delta)` for LASSERT. The formula-length contract relates `flen * 8` to the formula's encoded bit length B, giving a B + 1 floor for the stated successful-certification case. Meanwhile, an abstract instruction counter records one executed instruction. This compares two chosen metrics; it is not a speedup, a runtime lower bound for all implementations, or a consequence of A2 alone.

The B + 1 floor requires the formula-length and additive-pricing contracts of the result being used. A2 says that a false-to-true certification transition costs at least one. If another requirement says the same step costs at least B, the two requirements together imply `cost >= max(B, 1)`, not `cost >= B + 1`. For B >= 1, charging B satisfies both individual floors. Additivity needs a further premise or a chosen schedule.

The VM's schedule and the payload-pricing contracts should therefore be named explicitly. They do not measure physical information erased merely because mu increases. Nor does counting one abstract LASSERT prove that a conventional RAM can check an arbitrary B-bit assertion in one physical or primitive machine step.

To compare execution efficiency, fix a shared task, representation, primitive instruction set, and cost metric. The between-metric comparison by itself establishes no such efficiency separation.


12. HOW TO CHECK THE CLAIMS

Read the definitions before the theorem names. A checked implication can be correct while its prose describes a larger class of systems than its premises allow.

The principal checks are:

  1. For the accounting theorem, inspect A2, non-negative costs, and ledger conservation. Then check the trace argument in Section 3. Whether a particular event should carry that price is a modeling question separate from the implication.
  2. For observation and verifier results, identify the observation, query, state domain, and colliding witnesses. Check the claims on precisely that domain. An encoding that retains more information is a legitimate comparison, not something to exclude by wording.
  3. For implementation claims, check permitted transitions and the abstraction relation. Include WFDrivenRun preconditions and the named compiler/backend boundary when discussing generated RTL.
  4. For the diagonal, inspect the recursion and representability premises. Distinguish a predicate on arbitrary programs from a Boolean query on a supplied state. Do not infer non-reducibility from the absence of a reduction in this proof.
  5. For Section 10's quantitative bound, inspect the explicit tree, posterior-representative/fiber premise, and depth/payment condition. Those hypotheses are part of the result; the bound is not asserted for every deterministic decision trace.
  6. For a payload-cost claim, identify the actual schedule and the premise requiring additive charges. Keep that metric separate from runtime and physical dissipation.

The v3.2.2 documentation describes these boundaries without changing the kernel theorems. The assumption receipt covers 12,335 statements: 5,520 closed under the global context and 6,815 depending on standard-library assumptions. The receipt reports no project-local axiom findings. A theorem's explicit hypotheses still matter even when it is closed under the global context.

Start with `README.md` and the proof contracts it links. Run `make verify`, `make verify-claims`, and `make verify-research` in the documented build environment. These checks provide reproducible evidence about their defined scope; they do not certify every interpretation in an essay.

I want the argument to survive being read closely. If a definition misses the phenomenon I intended, or a sentence exceeds its theorem, that is a correction to make, even when the proof compiles.
