THE THIELE MACHINE
A distillation: state, accounting, and what the proofs establish

Devon Thiele -- September 2026, v3.2.2

I didn't invent a machine. I found one.

That is my conviction. The Thiele Machine is how I put it on the table: definitions, executable rules, proofs, and a machine somebody else can run. The conviction is mine. The proofs are public, and anyone can check them.

A computation gives you an answer. I want to know what it had to establish to get there, which distinctions it kept along the way, and what keeping them cost. Certification is the one place I could pin that down hard. The idea reaches further than that one place. The proofs don't. They cover the events, views, and machines their statements name, and nothing past them.

Here is the picture. The machine carries two things an ordinary description leaves out: structure, and a ledger of what that structure cost. Now look at the machine through a narrower window, one that only shows memory, registers, and where the program is. Two states can look identical through that window and still be different underneath. Ask a question whose answer depends on the difference, and nothing looking only through the window can answer it right every time. Not with any amount of cleverness. The information isn't in the window.

That's a fact about the window. It isn't a fact about computers. Ordinary memory can hold the whole state, ledger included, and an ordinary machine can follow the same accounting rule step for step. The blindness is in the view. The hardware isn't blind. So every comparison in here names four things: the state, the steps allowed, the window, and what you're trusting the implementation to do.

One more line I keep drawing. Paying for certification doesn't make anything true. The generic CERTIFY instruction flips a flag and charges for it. It checks nothing. Other instructions do check things, and each of those has its own soundness proof. Calling the flag "certified" doesn't give it one.

I built this to be examined. Below, what I built and what the proofs show about it stay on one side. What I'm asking you to consider stays on the other.


1. SUBSTRATE

A computation is a line of steps. Each step takes the state the machine is in and one instruction, and lands on the next state. To talk about that precisely you need six names:

  S      states: whatever the machine remembers between steps.
  I      instructions: the moves it can make.
  step   the step rule: given a state s and an instruction i, it gives the next state, step(s, i).
  c      a cost for each instruction: a whole number, never negative, written c(i).
  P      a yes/no reading on states: P(s) is 0 or 1. In the VM it's the certification field. In another system P could be worked out from other state, like whether a counter is even or odd. It doesn't need its own storage slot.
  mu     the ledger: a whole number every state carries. It starts at 0, and each step adds the cost of the instruction it ran: mu(step(s, i)) = mu(s) + c(i). Nothing else touches it. Call that conservation. The ledger is exactly the sum of what got charged.

A substrate is (S, I, step, c, P), with mu riding along on every state. Five pieces of structure, and one field that just adds them up.

Picking these out doesn't make the machine special. It makes the accounting visible. A bare transition system doesn't have to name a P, a c, or a mu. An ordinary machine can still carry all three in memory and keep the same rule. Turing equivalence is about which functions you can compute. It doesn't pick an accounting policy for you.

Two small proofs make that concrete. `counter_certification_system` uses a counter as the state, its parity as P, and a cost of 1 per step. `receipt_encoding_roundtrip` shows ordinary list memory holding a ledger and a certification value with nothing lost. Both live in `coq/tests/ClaimBoundaryRegression.v`.


2. THE AXIOM

One rule. I call it A2:

  For every state s and every instruction i, if P(s) = 0 and P(step(s, i)) = 1, then c(i) >= 1.

Said plainly: any single step that flips P from no to yes costs at least 1. Since mu is just the running total of c, that flip also pushes the ledger up by at least 1. A2 only touches that one kind of step. Conservation carries the charge into the ledger the same way it carries every other step's cost.

A substrate where A2 holds keeps this pricing rule. A substrate where some instruction flips P from 0 to 1 for free lets certification happen for nothing.


3. THEOREM: NO FREE INSIGHT

Picture a run that starts with P at 0 and ends with P at 1. P can't cross that gap without some step carrying it over. Find that step. A2 says it paid. Everything else in the run paid zero or more. So the whole run paid at least 1.

That's the theorem. The formal version:

Claim. In any substrate satisfying A2, any run that starts uncertified (P = 0) and ends certified (P = 1) has total cost at least 1. Its ledger mu ends at least 1 higher than it started.

Proof. By conservation, the run's total cost is mu at the end minus mu at the start. P only ever reads 0 or 1, so some single step takes it from 0 to 1. By A2 that step cost at least 1. Every other step cost at least 0. A sum of non-negative numbers with one term at least 1 is at least 1.

It holds for any substrate satisfying A2. It doesn't care what the states look like, what the instructions are, or what the step rule does. A2, conservation, and non-negative costs are all it uses.


4. REPRESENTATION AND ENFORCEMENT

A2 isn't a property of a machine on its own. It's a property of a step rule together with the P and the c you point at. Keep the same transitions, point at a different P or charge a different c, and A2 can stop holding. So I name those choices every time.

An ordinary machine can enforce A2. Put P and mu in ordinary memory, and write transitions that update them the way the rule says. Those transitions keep A2. Showing that a particular implementation really follows them takes an implementation proof, or you trust it. That's true of this VM too.

Somebody can always write a different rule that breaks A2. That's a different implementation. It says nothing against the first one. And giving P and mu their own fields doesn't stop an implementation from updating them wrong.

Seeing is a separate question from enforcing. Take a window that shows memory, registers, and the program counter, and hides the certification field and the ledger. If two reachable states look the same through that window and one is certified while the other isn't, nothing reading only the window can tell you which is which on every reachable state. That's about this window. It isn't about every way of storing the state.

The general version says exactly when a window is good enough. Group together the states that look the same through it. A question can be answered from the window only if every state in a group gives the same answer. Hand over one sample state from each group and that same condition builds the answering function. A wider window can pass where a narrower one fails.


5. THE BUGGY SIMULATOR

Say an implementation flips its P from 0 to 1 and adds nothing to its ledger. If it was supposed to keep A2 and conservation, that step is a bug.

A machine built to A2 has no such step among the ones it allows. That follows from its specification. Saying hardware has no such step is a bigger claim. It takes showing the hardware actually follows the specification, under whatever representation and implementation assumptions are in play.

An ordinary machine can allow exactly those steps too. It can also simulate a machine that breaks A2 without breaking A2 itself. The simulated flag is just data to the host. It isn't necessarily the host's own P. Mix those two levels up and you get an impossibility claim that isn't true.

So the real question is always the same. Which updates does the implementation allow, and what evidence ties them to the rule? Having a field for it, or a type that mentions it, doesn't settle that.


6. LIFTING, PROJECTION, AND VERIFICATION

Going up is free. Take any ordinary transition system, keep its states and steps exactly as they are, leave P at 0 forever, and charge nothing. A2 holds, because nothing ever flips. The original computation sits inside the accounting model untouched, and no new computable functions appear. The VM adds more instructions on top. It's one way of building the idea out. There are others.

Going down loses things. The VM's strict window forgets the ledger, the certification flag, and the graph. The separation proofs hand over pairs of states that collide in that window and need different answers. That proves no decoder works across the whole domain. It doesn't mean every state you observe has more than one state behind it.

Giving P and mu separate fields is convenient. It isn't a theorem about the smallest possible representation. Another encoding can pack them into one value, and in some systems one of them is already a function of other state. What has to be kept depends on the question you're asking and which states are allowed.

A2 sets a floor. It doesn't set a price list. Charging more than 1 is allowed, and steps that flip nothing can still cost something. Exact unit pricing only comes out once you add a second rule: never charge more than the number of certification flips. And several unit floors can share one charge. Two independent things being true at once doesn't force their costs to add.

The verifier result is just as exact about its reach. Suppose a transcript can't tell apart two runs, one where a mu-dependent claim holds and one where it doesn't. Completeness says accept the first. Strong soundness says don't accept while the second is still possible. Nothing reading that transcript alone can do both.

The repository builds three ways out, each giving the verifier the extra evidence it needs under its own premises: see the full state, check a commitment under a hardness assumption, or ask the prover a question it has to answer. The hardness route comes with the weaker soundness guarantee its theorem states. None of this shows those three are the only ways out, or that ordinary computing can't keep the information around. And the collision has to actually happen for the transcript you're talking about.


7. WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT CLAIM

What it does: it makes one accounting discipline explicit and follows it all the way through. No Free Insight takes the local rule, where one step pays, and stretches it over a whole run. The observation results say exactly when a window loses what a question needs. These are mathematical results about the models they name.

What it doesn't: it doesn't add new computable functions. It doesn't say ordinary machines can't enforce invariants. It doesn't put a physical price on every change to structure. The VM itself lets some structural changes happen at zero cost.

Anything physical needs its own argument. `full_vm_f1_premises_incompatible` proves that the full instruction set's free jumps can't live alongside the stated pair of universal collapse-price and cost-calibration premises. A program counter collapsing to one place doesn't prove anything was physically erased, either. Keep the old state in a longer history and no two states land on the same next state for a fixed instruction.

The hardware model follows the instructions actually fetched, the program counter, the fuel, and the state, under `WFDrivenRun`. Generated RTL still sits behind the compiler and backend trust boundary named in the pipeline manifest. Synthesis, simulation, and the FPGA bitstream add evidence. They don't turn that boundary into a Coq proof.

The sections below state more results, each with its own premises. A2 alone doesn't supply them.


8. STRUCTURAL UNDECIDABILITY

This is the old diagonal trick, pointed at structure. Suppose something could look at any program and tell you yes or no about it. Build a program that asks that decider about itself and then does the opposite. Whatever the decider says about that program is wrong.

For the trick to work the substrate has to carry some extra machinery, and A2 doesn't give you any of it.

First, a recursion theorem. That's Kleene's: any way of transforming programs that the substrate can itself run has a fixed point, a program that behaves exactly like its own transformed image.

Second, a predicate on programs. The formal interface calls it `AdmitsShortcut`. What it means has to be supplied by the instance. The name alone doesn't tie it to Section 10's quantitative witnesses. It needs two properties. Programs that behave the same get the same answer. And the substrate fixes two canonical programs, one the predicate accepts and one it rejects.

Theorem. No decider the substrate can run as one of its own programs gets this predicate right on every program.

Proof. Suppose D is such a decider. Build f: given a program p, run D(p). If D says yes, output the canonical program the predicate rejects. If no, output the one it accepts. For the recursion theorem to apply, f has to be one of the substrate's own programs. That takes one more premise, and I name it: the substrate's programs are closed under "run a program, then branch to one of two fixed programs." Being able to run D doesn't give you that for free. Grant it, and the recursion theorem hands back a program q that behaves exactly like f(q). Now look at D(q). If D says q admits a shortcut, then f(q) is the program that doesn't, and q behaves like f(q), so q doesn't. D was wrong. If D says q doesn't, the same step makes q one that does. Either answer is wrong, so D can't exist.

That's the Kleene/Rice pattern run through an abstract program interface. It holds under that interface's recursion, representability, closure, same-behavior, and nontriviality premises. The VM instance still carries its recursion-theorem premise. The repository also has an unconditional instance on its separate natural-number substrate.

The proof doesn't go through a Turing-machine simulator. That doesn't show the predicate is unrelated to halting, or that it sits at a different Turing degree. Those would need their own definitions and proofs. And deciding a predicate over arbitrary programs is a different job from reading one yes/no field out of a state someone hands you.

The diagonal even works in a model where mu is always zero and A2 never has anything to charge. The contradiction comes from recursion and same-behavior. Cost plays no part. That's the whole point of this result.


9. COST LEDGER OBSERVABILITY

Two runs look identical through the window and leave different bills.

Start at `init_state`: mu = 0, certification off. Run one does CERTIFY with delta = 0. It costs 1 and turns the flag on. Run two does PNEW on an empty region with delta = 0. It costs 0 and changes the graph. Both move the program counter forward by one and leave memory and registers alone. A window that only shows those three things sees the same picture both times. The ledgers read 1 and 0.

So no function of that window recovers the ledger on every reachable VM state.

It takes this instruction set, this starting state, this cost schedule, and this window. A2 gives the certification flip its floor. It doesn't give you a free PNEW or matching windows. This isn't a separation theorem for every A2 system.

Change the schedule to allow free certification and this particular pair stops differing in cost. Just dropping A2 as an assumption doesn't change the VM's schedule, though, and it doesn't make every ledger separation go away. A wider window that shows mu tells the two runs apart at a glance.

It's an information-loss result. The window can't answer a question its colliding states disagree on. It doesn't say the ledger can't be stored in ordinary memory, and it doesn't say that paying proves anything.


10. QUANTITATIVE COST LOWER BOUND

Now a number. Picture a pile of candidates, everything still in play. A shortcut narrows the pile. If it narrows it by asking yes/no questions laid out as a tree, and the run pays for every level of that tree, then the ledger pays at least the number of halvings it takes to get from the big pile down to the small one.

That's the idea. The formal version only covers shortcuts that hand over their receipts.

Witness class. A "sound structural shortcut" (`SoundStructuralShortcut`) is a package the shortcut has to hand over:
  - a finite prior list Omega, the candidates in play before it runs;
  - a strictly shorter posterior list Omega', the ones left after;
  - an observation that separates at least one eliminated candidate from every survivor;
  - a certified posterior predicate;
  - a binary (yes/no) decision tree;
  - a map from prior entries to posterior representatives, with a bound on how many land on each;
  - a guarantee that the run realizes the tree.
The last one carries the weight. It says the run executes at least as many cert-setter instructions as the tree is deep. This class isn't "any decision procedure". It's the procedures that hand over the tree and that guarantee.

Theorem. For any sound structural shortcut with prior list Omega and nonempty posterior list Omega', the run that realizes it satisfies
  log_2^up |Omega| - log_2^up |Omega'| <= Delta mu,
where log_2^up n is log base 2 of n rounded up, |Omega| is a list length with duplicates counted, and Delta mu is how much the ledger rose over the run. The feel of log_2^up n is "how many yes/no questions it takes to pick one thing out of n". That's only the feel. It isn't a count of questions the VM asked, and it isn't Shannon entropy.

Proof sketch. Call the tree T, its leaf count L, and its depth d. Three pieces click together.
  First, a cover: |Omega| <= L * |Omega'|. Group the prior entries by which posterior representative they map to. That makes |Omega'| groups, and the supplied bound caps each group at L.
  Second, take log_2^up of both sides. It never goes down as its input grows, so the inequality survives: log_2^up|Omega| <= log_2^up(L * |Omega'|). And the rounded log of a product is at most the sum of the rounded logs: log_2^up(L * |Omega'|) <= log_2^up L + log_2^up|Omega'|. So log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L. A binary tree of depth d has at most 2^d leaves, so log_2^up L <= d.
  Third, the cost. The realizes-the-tree guarantee says the run executes at least d cert-setters. In the VM every cert-setter costs at least 1, and conservation makes the ledger the sum of step costs. So Delta mu >= d.
Chained: log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L <= d <= Delta mu.

What it doesn't claim. It doesn't say an arbitrary run has to pay log_2(n) for an n-way decision. A run that doesn't hand over the realized-tree receipt has no depth guarantee, so the chain has nothing to hang from. It might run one cert-setter and the bound says nothing about it. An unconditional Shannon-style bound on every deterministic single run would be a different and stronger result. It's open.

What it does claim. When a binary tree the run realizes carries the narrowing, mu pays at least the drop in log_2^up of the list length. The direction is the safe one. mu gets a floor. It's never set equal to anything and never given physical units. The floor can even be 0 when the list got shorter but the rounded log didn't move (4 down to 3). So it never overstates the narrowing. The run can't pay less than this, and the tree's depth, which the run's cert-setters cover, is what lifts the floor up to mu. Compression has a floor, and paid, certified observation sets it.

What holds the chain together. The last link needs every cert-setter to cost at least 1. That's the VM's cost floor, the concrete version of what A2 asks for. Take it away and cert-setters could cost zero, their count would stop bounding Delta mu, and the chain snaps at the end. The first two links come from the supplied tree and bound. No cost rule touches them.


11. COMPARING TWO COST MODELS

Two meters can watch the same instruction and read different numbers. The VM's payload-sensitive schedule charges LASSERT `flen * 8 + S(delta)`. The formula-length contract ties `flen * 8` to the formula's encoded bit length B, which gives a B + 1 floor in the stated successful-certification case. An abstract instruction counter watching the same step reads 1. That's a comparison between two chosen meters. It isn't a speedup, a runtime lower bound for every implementation, or something A2 gives you on its own.

The B + 1 floor needs the formula-length and additive-pricing contracts of the result you're using. A2 says a false-to-true certification step costs at least 1. If some other rule says the same step costs at least B, the two together give `cost >= max(B, 1)`. They don't give `cost >= B + 1`. For B >= 1, charging B satisfies both. Getting them to add takes another premise, or a schedule chosen that way.

So name the VM's schedule and the payload-pricing contracts out loud. A rising mu doesn't measure physical information erased. And counting one abstract LASSERT doesn't show an ordinary RAM can check an arbitrary B-bit assertion in one physical or primitive step.

To compare speed you have to fix the same task, the same representation, the same primitive instructions, and the same cost meter on both sides. Comparing two different meters doesn't give you a speed difference.


12. HOW TO CHECK THE CLAIMS

Read the definitions before the theorem names. An implication can check out in Coq while the sentence describing it reaches past what its premises allow. That gap is where I'd look.

Where to push:

  1. The accounting theorem. Look at A2, non-negative costs, and conservation, then walk the run argument in Section 3. Whether a particular event deserves that price is a modeling question. The implication doesn't answer it.
  2. Observation and verifier results. Pin down the window, the question, the states allowed, and the colliding pair. Check the claim on exactly that. An encoding that keeps more is a fair comparison. Don't let wording rule it out.
  3. Implementation claims. Look at which steps are allowed and at the abstraction relation. For generated RTL, include the WFDrivenRun preconditions and the named compiler/backend boundary.
  4. The diagonal. Look at the recursion and representability premises. Keep a predicate over arbitrary programs apart from a yes/no read of a state you've been handed. Don't read "this proof didn't go through halting" as "this can't be reduced to halting".
  5. Section 10's bound. Look at the tree, the representative/fiber premise, and the depth-and-payment condition. They're part of the result. The bound isn't claimed for every deterministic decision run.
  6. Payload-cost claims. Find the actual schedule and the premise that makes charges add. Keep that meter apart from runtime and from physical heat.

In v3.2.2 the documentation draws these lines without changing any kernel theorem. The assumption receipt covers 12,817 statements: 5,526 closed under the global context and 7,291 depending on standard-library assumptions. It reports no project-local axioms. A theorem's own hypotheses still matter, even when it's closed under the global context.

Start with `README.md` and the proof contracts it links. Run `make verify`, `make verify-claims`, and `make verify-research` in the documented build environment. They give you reproducible evidence about what they check. They don't certify every reading of an essay.

I want this to hold up when someone reads it closely. If a definition misses what I was reaching for, or a sentence says more than its theorem, I want it fixed, even when the proof compiles.
