THE THIELE MACHINE
Devon Thiele


WHAT THIS IS

I think in shapes, so I'll start with one.

Take a thing. Anything. A coin on a table, a light switch, a page in a
notebook. Right now it's one way. It could be another.

Now do something to it. Flip the coin, hit the switch, write on the page.
That's a move: a direction that takes the thing from how it is to how it is
next.

Flip here means turn it over by hand. When something else picks what happens,
chance with a die, your hand in a bucket of numbers, a person at a button, a
message coming in, call each way it can go its own move, with exactly one next
thing each. Whatever does the picking sits outside the machine, and chapter
twenty-two takes on what that changes.

Here's a computer you can run with a pencil. Write 3 on a scrap of paper. The
move is: erase the number and write the next one up. Do it twice. 3, 4, 5. You
just added 2 and 3. Nothing happened there but a thing, a move, and the next
thing. Hold on to that picture; whether it covers every computer is something
I'll have to earn, and I will, later, with you watching.

Here's the part I couldn't let go of. Some moves just shuffle. Flip the coin
and you can flip it back, and nothing is different for good. But some moves
make a difference that stays. Write a word in pen. Blow a fuse. Check an
answer and mark it checked. After that move something is the case that wasn't
before, and it stays the case.

I figured there had to be a cost in that step. Not in the shuffling. In the
step where something goes from not settled to settled and stays there. That
was a hunch, so test it on the pencil computer.

Tell it when to stop: count up, and when you reach 5, stop. Ask one question
of each number: is it done? 3, no. 4, no. 5, yes. And once it says yes it
keeps saying yes, because the machine has stopped.

A machine that has stopped is still somewhere on the next tick. It stays where
it is: 5 goes to 5. Draw the arrows. 4 goes to 5. 5 goes to 5. Two different
starting places, one landing place. Standing on 5, you can't tell whether you
just got there or have been there all along.

Could you dodge that? Try letting the stopped machine rock back and forth
instead of sitting still: 5-tick, 5-tock, 5-tick, both of them saying done.
Draw the arrows. 4 goes to 5-tick. 5-tock goes to 5-tick. Two arrows land on
5-tick, the spot where you came in. Rocking only moved the place where they
meet. The one way out is to never land anywhere twice: make every tick write
something new, a tally mark, the time, anything, so the paper never repeats.
That works as long as there's paper. On a finite sheet you run out, and two
arrows land on one spot again.

So whenever this machine settles its answer and keeps it, something gives.
Either two states become one, or it uses up fresh paper on every tick for as
long as it runs. That part isn't a hunch. You drew it. You drew it for one
small counter; the book proves it for any machine that goes from one state to
a next, and shows you where.

What I do with it is a choice, and I'll say so: I charge for the move where
the answer goes from not done to done. Charging for it is up to me. That
something has to give once it makes that move isn't. The two arrows don't
always meet on that very move. Give done two spots, 5a and 5b: 4 goes to 5a,
5a goes to 5b, and 5b stays put. Now the arrows from 5a and 5b meet on 5b, a
tick after the answer settled. But after the move from not done to done,
there's no avoiding it: two arrows meet somewhere, or the paper never repeats.

Two more things have to be pinned down before the rule means anything, and you
can see both with what's already on the table.

First, take the coin again. Flip it, flip it back, forever, free. Ask whether
it's settled. It never is. A rule that says "a move that settles something has
to cost" never asks the coin for anything, because the coin never settles
anything. So the rule alone would let a coin that does nothing count. The
machine has to actually settle something, at least once.

Second, suppose a later move could pay you back. The move that settles charges
you one, the next move hands you one back, and the whole run comes out free.
So prices can be nothing or something, never a refund.

One last thing before the whole definition. Settled doesn't have to be just
yes or no. Take a checklist with three boxes. None ticked, one, two, all
three: each tick settles it a little more, and ticks don't come off. "Done or
not" is the smallest case, a checklist with one box. So when I say "more
settled" from here on, picture the checklist.

So here's the Thiele Machine, all of it. A thing. Moves that take it to the
next thing. A way to tell how settled the thing is, where settled never goes
back to unsettled. Some run, from a start you pick, that actually settles it
more than it was. A price on every move, nothing or more, never a refund. And
one rule: a move that settles something more than it was costs something.

That's the whole definition. The rest of the book works out what follows from
it, one step at a time, and tries to break it.

Now look back at the pencil computer with a stop in it. That's Turing's
machine, in miniature. Turing's machine, from his 1936 paper, is a tape and a
table of rules: from where it is, the table says where it goes next. A thing
and moves. It already has something that settles and stays settled: whether it
has stopped, the same question you asked of 3, 4 and 5. What Turing never put
in is a price on the move that stops it. Put one in, and a Turing machine that
ever stops fits every line above. So this isn't a rival to Turing's machine,
and it computes nothing his can't. His machine is one kind of Thiele machine,
with the price left out.

Now try this. Give two people the same messy list of numbers. The first sorts
it, then goes back down it checking that every number is no bigger than the
next. The second copies a sorted list off somebody else's sheet. Now look at
the two sheets. Same numbers, same order. Look as hard as you like: nothing on
either sheet tells you which one was checked. The difference was real, and
neither sheet kept it. It's the same thing you saw standing on 5: you can see
where you are, and not how you got there.

So put a box on each sheet that says checked. A plain Thiele machine charges
for ticking the box, but nothing in the rules above stops the copier from
paying and ticking it anyway. Paying for the tick doesn't make it true. So add
one more condition: the box can only be ticked right after a check of that
very list passes. Now the copier's tick is refused, and a ticked box tells you
a check of that list passed. I call a box like that established. It's a
condition you put on the machine. The bare definition doesn't hand it to you.

One more picture to carry through the book. Write "checked" on Turing's tape,
and nothing in his model stops the next rule from rubbing it out, or from
printing it without the check; a particular table might leave it alone, but
the model doesn't make it hold. Cover a Thiele machine's record with your hand
and set the price of settling to zero, and what's left is a bare machine that
steps: every one of them, Turing's included, is a Thiele machine with a
blindfold on.

It's called the Thiele Machine. The afterword says why.


WHAT THE BOOK SHOWS

Each of these is argued on the page, with the reader, under the conditions its
chapter states.

- Keeping takes a merge or fresh paper. Every step of a machine with one next
  state lands somewhere new, makes two arrows meet, or goes round a loop. On
  finitely many states, settling something for good makes two arrows meet.
  Chapters 1 and 2.

- The toll isn't only a preference. With finitely many states, settled staying
  settled, and Bennett's rule (a move where two arrows meet costs at least
  one), the toll follows. Take any one of the three away and a machine walks
  through. Chapter 3.

- The smallest one. Two boxes and one move that sends both to YES. Every
  machine with a move is what's left of a Thiele machine once you cover a
  coin. Chapter 4.

- Looking isn't keeping. A look can be free; it uses a blank page that can be
  handed back. Keeping what you saw is what pays. Chapter 6.

- A window can't price what it hides. A window with a clash can't price
  exactly, and the book finds the least it has to overcharge. Chapters 7 and
  15.

- A box over any machine. A box that answers only to the machine and never
  comes down is a cup turned by one event. Chapter 14.

- Four doors. A machine that reads its box to skip work it already paid for
  pays in fresh editions, a box that comes down, room for every value checked,
  or wrong answers, and under two stated conditions there's no fifth. Chapter
  16.

- What a machine can't decide about itself. No procedure inside the machine
  decides whether its own shortcuts are sound, under the conditions stated.
  Chapter 17.

- Heat, given Landauer. If Landauer's principle holds, a merge of states the
  machine could really have been in gives off at least a set amount of heat;
  on a state that's already known it sets no floor at all. The principle is an
  assumption with its critics named, never something the book proves. Chapter
  20.

- How much room keeping takes. Exact counts of the states a machine needs to
  hold off a merge. Chapter 21.

Chapter 22 sorts every claim in the book into shown, chosen, assumed, cited,
thesis, or not claimed. The theses are labelled as theses, among them the
pointer thesis (Chapter 19) and the Thiele thesis (Chapter 22).
