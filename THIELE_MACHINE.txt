THE THIELE MACHINE
A distillation: state, accounting, and what the proofs establish

Devon Thiele -- September 2026, v3.2.1


I didn't invent a machine. I found one.

That is my conviction. The Thiele Machine is how I put it on the
table: definitions, executable rules, proofs, and a machine somebody
else can run. The conviction is mine; the proof contracts are public.

A computation gives you an answer. I want to know what it established,
which distinctions it preserved, and what it cost to establish them.
Certification is one point I could pin down. The argument reaches
beyond that point, but the proofs apply to the events, observations,
and machines their statements actually describe.

The machine carries structural state and a cost ledger. Its rules
charge particular events. Its observation theorems show when a view
of that state loses information relevant to a query. If two states
look the same through that view but require different answers, no
function of the view alone can answer correctly on both.

That is a statement about a specified view. It does not prove that
every computer loses this information. Ordinary memory can encode
the full state, and a conventional transition rule can maintain the
same accounting discipline. The comparison must name the state,
permitted transitions, observation, and trusted implementation.

There is a second distinction throughout this account. Charging for
a certification event does not establish that an arbitrary assertion
is true. Generic CERTIFY sets a flag and charges; it checks no
proposition. Other checks have their own soundness contracts. Calling
the flag "certified" does not supply one.

I built this to be examined. What follows separates the construction,
its proved consequences, and the interpretation I am asking people
to consider.


1. SUBSTRATE

A computation is a sequence of steps. Each step takes a state and an
instruction and produces the next state. To describe this precisely,
name six things:

  S    states. Whatever the machine remembers between steps.
  I    instructions. The actions the machine can perform.
  step a step rule. Given a state s and an instruction i, the rule
       produces the next state, written step(s, i).
  c    a cost function. Every instruction has a non-negative integer
       cost, written c(i).
  P    a designated boolean predicate on states: P(s) is 0 or 1.
       The VM has a certification field as one instance. In another
       system P may be computed from other state, such as parity of
       a counter. A dedicated storage field is not required.
  mu   a cost ledger. Each state carries this too: a non-negative
       integer. It starts at 0, and every step adds the cost of the
       instruction it ran: mu(step(s, i)) = mu(s) + c(i). So mu is the
       running total of everything c has charged so far. Call this law
       conservation: cost is never lost and never conjured, and the
       ledger is exactly the sum of what was charged.

A substrate is the tuple (S, I, step, c, P), with every state carrying
the ledger mu that the step rule maintains by conservation. Five
pieces of structure, and one bookkeeping field that just adds them up.

These choices make the accounting explicit. A bare transition-system
definition need not designate P, c, or mu. A particular conventional
machine can nevertheless encode the state and maintain the chosen
invariant. Turing equivalence addresses computable functions; it does
not decide which accounting policy a model should specify.

The regression proof counter_certification_system uses a natural-number
state, its parity as P, and unit step cost. receipt_encoding_roundtrip
shows that ordinary list memory can retain a ledger and a certification
value without loss. Both are in coq/tests/ClaimBoundaryRegression.v.


2. THE AXIOM

Call this rule A2:

  For every state s and every instruction i,
  if P(s) = 0 and P(step(s, i)) = 1, then c(i) >= 1.

In one sentence: any single step that flips certification from "no" to
"yes" must cost at least 1. Equivalently, since mu just totals c: a
step that flips certification on raises the ledger mu by at least 1.
A2 constrains c on that one kind of step; conservation then carries
the charge into the ledger, the way it carries every step's c.

A substrate that satisfies A2 meets this certification-cost policy. A
substrate where some instruction can flip P from 0 to 1 at cost 0
permits free certification.


3. THEOREM: NO FREE INSIGHT

Claim. In any substrate satisfying A2, any run that starts uncertified
(P = 0) and ends certified (P = 1) has total cost at least 1 -- that
is, its ledger mu ends at least 1 above where it started.

Proof. The run's total cost is exactly mu at the end minus mu at the
start, because by conservation each step added its own c to the ledger
and nothing else touched it. So I only have to show that sum is at
least 1. P starts at 0 and ends at 1, and P only ever reads 0 or 1, so
it cannot get from 0 to 1 without some single step carrying it across:
there is a step where P goes 0 -> 1. By A2 that step cost at least 1.
Every other step cost at least 0, because costs are non-negative. A
sum of non-negative numbers with one term at least 1 is at least 1.

The theorem holds for ANY substrate satisfying A2. It does not depend
on what the states look like, what the instructions are, or what the
step rule does. A2, conservation, and non-negative costs are enough.


4. REPRESENTATION AND ENFORCEMENT

A2 is a property of a step rule together with a designated predicate
and cost function. Changing P or c can change whether A2 holds even
when the underlying transitions stay the same. Naming those choices
is necessary to state the property precisely.

It does not follow that a conventional machine cannot enforce it.
Encode the state, including P and mu, in ordinary memory. Choose
transitions that implement the same certified-state updates and cost
schedule. Those transitions can preserve A2. Establishing that a
concrete implementation obeys them is an implementation proof or a
trust assumption, just as it is for a realization of this VM.

A different transition rule that violates the policy is a different
implementation. Its existence does not show that the first rule fails
to enforce the policy. Nor does making P and mu separate fields by
itself prevent an implementation from updating them incorrectly.

The observation question is separate. Define a projection that keeps
memory, registers, and program counter while dropping the designated
certification field and ledger. If two reachable states have the same
projection and different certification values, certification cannot be
recovered from that projection on every reachable state. That theorem
concerns the chosen projection, not every possible memory encoding.

The more general observation theorem says exactly what is needed:
a query is recoverable only if it is constant on each class of states
with the same observation. With explicit representatives of those
classes, the condition also constructs a decoder. A richer observation
may satisfy this condition where a poorer one fails.


5. THE BUGGY SIMULATOR

Suppose a purported implementation changes its designated P from 0
to 1 while adding zero to its designated ledger. If it is supposed to
obey A2 and ledger conservation, that transition is incorrect.

An A2-respecting abstract machine has no such transition among its
permitted steps. This is a consequence of its specification. To claim
that hardware enforces the same exclusion, one must establish that
hardware execution follows the specification under the stated
representation and implementation assumptions.

A conventional machine can implement those permitted steps too. It
can also simulate descriptions of machines that violate A2 without
violating its own accounting: the simulated flag is then data, not
necessarily the host's designated predicate P. Confusing these two
levels creates a false impossibility claim.

The practical question is which updates an implementation permits,
and what evidence connects those updates to the claimed policy. The
presence of a field or a type signature alone does not settle it.


6. LIFTING, PROJECTION, AND VERIFICATION

A classical transition system can be lifted into an accounting model:
retain its state and transitions, let P remain false, and charge zero.
A2 then holds vacuously. This embeds the original computation without
adding computable functions. The VM supplies more operations as a
particular realization of the broader construction.

Projection can lose information. The VM's chosen strict projection
forgets the ledger, certification, and graph. Its separation proofs
supply colliding witnesses with different answers to particular
queries. This proves failure of a decoder on the whole stated domain;
it does not imply that every observed state has multiple preimages.

Separate P and mu fields are convenient, but not a universal minimal
representation theorem. A different encoding can pack them into one
value; in some systems one may be a function of other state. The
query and admissible state space determine what must be retained.

A2 gives a lower bound, not a unique schedule. Charges greater than
one are allowed, and steps without certification flips may also cost
something. Exact unit pricing follows only after adding the separate
requirement of no overcharge relative to certification-flip count.
Several designated unit event floors can share one charge; independent
coordinates alone do not require separate charges to add.

The verifier impossibility has a similarly precise scope. Suppose a
transcript identifies two executions, one satisfying a mu-sensitive
claim and the other not. Completeness requires acceptance on the first;
strong soundness forbids acceptance when the second is still possible.
No verifier of that transcript alone can meet both requirements.

The repository constructs three ways to provide sufficient additional
evidence under their respective premises: access to the full state,
a commitment under a hardness hypothesis, and an interactive response
contract. The hardness construction has its stated weaker soundness
guarantee. The constructions do not establish that these three labels
exhaust all protocols, or that ordinary computing cannot retain the
needed information. The collision premise must hold for the particular
transcript under discussion.


7. WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT CLAIM

The construction makes a chosen accounting discipline explicit. The
No Free Insight theorem lifts its local positive-charge law to a run.
The observation results identify exactly when an interface loses the
information needed to answer a query. These are mathematical results
about the specified models.

They do not establish a new class of computable functions, an inability
of conventional machines to enforce invariants, or a universal physical
price for every structural change. The concrete ISA permits some
structural changes at zero ledger cost.

The physical interpretation needs its own argument. In particular,
full_vm_f1_premises_incompatible proves that the full ISA's zero-cost
jumps contradict the stated pair of universal collapse-price and
cost-calibration premises. A program counter collapsing to one does
not by itself establish physical erasure: retaining the old state in
a larger history can make each fixed-instruction step injective.

The hardware model's trace bridge follows actual fetched instructions,
program counter, fuel, and state under WFDrivenRun. Generated RTL keeps
the compiler/backend trust boundary named in the pipeline manifest.
Synthesis, simulation, and FPGA bitstream generation supply additional
evidence; they do not turn that boundary into a Coq proof.

The following sections state further results with their own premises.
A2 alone does not supply all of those premises.


8. STRUCTURAL UNDECIDABILITY

A2-respecting substrates form a category. Within that category,
those carrying a recursion theorem and a non-trivial behavioral
predicate on programs admit the result below. The recursion theorem
is Kleene's: any way of transforming programs that the substrate can
itself carry out has a fixed point -- a program whose behavior equals
its own image under the transformation. This is an additional
hypothesis; A2 alone does not provide a recursion theorem.

Fix such a predicate. The formal interface calls it AdmitsShortcut.
Its interpretation must be supplied by the instance; the name alone
does not identify it with Section 10's quantitative witness package.
The argument works for any predicate satisfying the stated behavioral
invariance and nontriviality conditions. Concretely it needs two
things: the predicate respects
extensional equivalence (programs with the same run-behavior get the
same value), and the substrate fixes two canonical programs, one the
predicate accepts and one it rejects.

Theorem. No internally representable decider -- a decider the
substrate can run as one of its own programs -- correctly decides
this predicate for arbitrary programs.

Proof. Suppose D is such a decider. Build the flip transformer f: on
input program p, run D(p); if it answers yes, output the canonical
program the predicate rejects; if no, output the one it accepts. For
the recursion theorem to apply, f must itself be one of the
substrate's own programs -- and it is, on one mild assumption worth
naming: that the substrate's programs are closed under "run a program,
then branch to one of two fixed programs." This closure is a separate
premise, not a consequence of merely being able to run D. Granting
that, f is representable, the recursion theorem's side-condition, and
the theorem hands back a fixed-point program q whose run-behavior
equals that of f(q). Case-split on D(q). If D says q admits a shortcut, then by
construction f(q) is the program that does not, and q behaves like
f(q), so by extensionality q does not -- D was wrong. If D says q does
not, the same step makes q one that does. Either answer is wrong, so
no such D exists.

This is the Kleene/Rice diagonal pattern expressed through an abstract
program interface. The theorem is conditional on that interface's
recursion, representability, closure, extensionality, and nontriviality
premises. The VM instance retains its recursion-theorem premise; the
repository also supplies an unconditional diagonal instance on its
separate natural-number substrate.

The proof's not invoking a Turing-machine simulator does not establish
non-reducibility to halting or Turing-degree incomparability. Such
claims would require their own definitions and proofs. Nor should the
undecidable predicate on arbitrary programs be confused with reading a
designated boolean from a fully supplied concrete state.

The diagonal can hold on a model with mu always zero and A2 vacuous.
Its contradiction uses the recursion theorem and extensionality, not
a positive-cost argument. That is the content of this result.


9. COST LEDGER OBSERVABILITY

The VM supplies two reachable computations with the same specified
classical observation and different ledgers. Consequently no function
of that observation recovers the ledger on every reachable VM state.

Start at init_state with mu = 0 and certification false. One trace
runs CERTIFY with delta = 0: its charge is 1 and it sets the flag.
The other runs PNEW on an empty region with delta = 0: its charge is
0 and it changes the graph. Both advance the program counter by one
and leave memory and registers unchanged. The projection retaining
only those three components therefore agrees, while the ledgers differ.

The argument needs this instruction set, initial state, cost schedule,
and observation. A2 supplies a positive floor for the certification
flip, but it alone does not supply a free PNEW instruction or matching
observations. It is not a separation theorem for every A2 system.

Allowing zero-cost certification in a modified schedule can remove
this particular witness's cost gap. Merely dropping A2 as a hypothesis
does not change the existing VM schedule or make every ledger
separation disappear. A richer observation that retains mu distinguishes
the two executions directly.

This is an information-loss result: the specified view cannot answer
a query on which its colliding states disagree. It does not say that
the ledger is unrepresentable in ordinary memory, or that paying the
charge proves an arbitrary proposition.


10. QUANTITATIVE COST LOWER BOUND

Now a number to go with it. Section 9 showed the cost ledger
separates substrate states a classical eye cannot tell apart. This
one puts a floor under that cost, for one well-marked class of
shortcut: the ledger mu on any sound structural shortcut rises by at
least the number of yes/no questions the shortcut's narrowing answers
-- the drop in ceiling-log of the feasible set's size. (I call that
drop the narrowing's entropy, meaning log-of-cardinality, not
Shannon's entropy of a distribution.)

Witness class. A "sound structural shortcut" is an explicit package
of receipts a shortcut must hand over to count as honest here: a
finite prior feasible set Omega (the candidates still in play before
the shortcut runs); a strictly smaller posterior set Omega' (a subset
of Omega, the ones still in play after); an observation function that tells at
least one eliminated state from every surviving one; a certified
posterior predicate; a BINARY (yes/no) decision tree; a posterior-
representative reduction that sorts each prior element into one leaf
of that tree; and -- the load-bearing receipt -- a guarantee that the
trace realizes the tree, which means precisely that the trace fires
at least as many cert-flips as the tree is deep. The members of this
class are not "any decision procedure"; they are the ones that hand
over the binary tree and that depth-many-cert-flips guarantee as
explicit witnesses.

Theorem (entropy-bounded mu-ledger). For any sound structural
shortcut with prior set Omega and nonempty posterior set Omega'
(a subset of Omega), the substrate trace that realizes the shortcut
satisfies
  log_2^up |Omega| - log_2^up |Omega'| <= Delta mu,
where log_2^up n is the ceiling of log_2 n -- the count of yes/no
questions needed to single out one element of an n-element set -- and
Delta mu is the change in the ledger across the trace.

Proof sketch. Write L for the number of leaves of the tree T and d
for its depth. Three facts compose.
First, a cover: |Omega| <= L * |Omega'|. The posterior-representative
reduction sorts each prior element into one leaf and one posterior
representative, and no two prior elements share both -- so it injects
Omega into (leaves of T) x Omega', and a set injecting into a product
has size at most that product: |Omega| <= L * |Omega'|.
Second, take ceiling-log, using two of its properties. It is monotone,
so the cover's <= carries through: log_2^up|Omega| <= log_2^up(L *
|Omega'|). And it is sub-additive over a product (the yes/no questions
to pin a pair are at most those for each part, summed): log_2^up(L *
|Omega'|) <= log_2^up L + log_2^up|Omega'|. Put together,
log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L. Because T is binary,
it has at most 2^d leaves, so log_2^up L <= d.
Third, the cost. The realized-by-the-trace receipt is exactly the
statement that the trace fires at least d cert-flips. By A2 each
cert-flip costs at least 1, and by conservation the ledger is the sum
of step costs, so Delta mu >= (cert-flips the trace fires) >= d.
Chain the three: log_2^up|Omega| - log_2^up|Omega'| <= log_2^up L <=
d <= Delta mu.

What this theorem does not claim. It does not claim that an arbitrary
single trace is forced to pay log_2(n) for an n-way decision. A trace
that does not hand over the realized-tree receipt carries no
depth-many-cert-flips guarantee, so the chain has nothing to hang
from: it might fire a single cert-flip and the bound says nothing.
The theorem applies exactly to traces that supply the binary tree and
the guarantee that they fired at least depth-many cert-flips. An
unconditional Shannon-style bound on every deterministic single trace
is a different and stronger thing, and it is left open.

What this theorem does claim. When the narrowing is carried by a
binary tree the trace realizes, the ledger mu pays at least the drop
in ceiling-log of the feasible set, log_2^up|Omega| -
log_2^up|Omega'|. The direction is the safe one: mu is lower-bounded,
never claimed equal to anything. And the left side is itself only a
floor on the questions answered -- it is at most the number of yes/no
tests the narrowing takes, and it can even be 0 when a test was
answered but no ceiling-log dropped (4 down to 3 drops none). So the
statement never overclaims the narrowing: it says the trace cannot
pay less in mu than this floor, and the tree's depth, which the
trace's cert-flips dominate, is what carries the floor up to mu.
Compression is bounded below by paid certified observation.

Why A2 closes the chain. Without A2 the cert-flip cost floor
disappears: cert-flip steps could cost zero, the cert-flip
count would no longer lower-bound Delta mu, and the chain of
inequalities breaks at the last step. A2 is what closes the chain
between tree depth and mu-ledger change.


11. COMPARING TWO COST MODELS

The VM's payload-sensitive schedule charges flen * 8 + S(delta)
for LASSERT. The formula-length contract relates flen * 8 to the
formula's encoded bit length B, giving a B + 1 floor for the stated
successful-certification case. Meanwhile, an abstract
instruction counter records one executed instruction. This compares
two chosen metrics. It is not a speedup, a runtime lower bound for all
implementations, or a consequence of A2 alone.

The B + 1 floor requires the formula-length and additive pricing
contracts of the result being used. A2 says that a false-to-true
certification transition costs at least one. If another requirement
says the same step costs at least B, the two requirements together
imply cost >= max(B, 1), not cost >= B + 1. For B >= 1, charging B
satisfies both individual floors. Additivity needs a further premise
or a chosen schedule.

The VM's schedule and the payload-pricing contracts should therefore
be named explicitly. They do not measure physical information erased
merely because mu increases. Nor does counting one abstract LASSERT
prove that a conventional RAM can check an arbitrary B-bit assertion
in one physical or primitive machine step.

To compare execution efficiency, fix a shared task, representation,
primitive instruction set, and cost metric. The between-metric
comparison by itself establishes no such efficiency separation.


12. HOW TO CHECK THE CLAIMS

Read the definitions before the theorem names. A checked implication
can be correct while its prose describes a larger class of systems
than its premises allow.

The principal checks are:

  1. For the accounting theorem, inspect A2, nonnegative costs, and
     ledger conservation. Then check the trace argument in Section 3.
     Whether a particular event should carry that price is a modeling
     question separate from the implication.

  2. For observation and verifier results, identify the observation,
     query, state domain, and colliding witnesses. Check the claims on
     precisely that domain. An encoding that retains more information
     is a legitimate comparison, not something to exclude by wording.

  3. For implementation claims, check permitted transitions and the
     abstraction relation. Include WFDrivenRun preconditions and the
     named compiler/backend boundary when discussing generated RTL.

  4. For the diagonal, inspect the recursion and representability
     premises. Distinguish a predicate on arbitrary programs from a
     boolean query on a supplied state. Do not infer non-reducibility
     from the absence of a reduction in this proof.

  5. For Section 10's quantitative bound, inspect the explicit tree,
     posterior-representative injection, and depth-many-cert-flips
     receipt. Those hypotheses are part of the result; the bound is
     not asserted for every deterministic decision trace.

  6. For a payload-cost claim, identify the actual schedule and the
     premise requiring additive charges. Keep that metric separate
     from runtime and physical dissipation.

The v3.2.1 documentation correction changes these descriptions, not
the kernel theorems. The assumption receipt still covers 4,026
statements: 2,979 closed under the global context, 1,047 depending on
standard-library axioms, and none on project-local or third-party
axioms. A theorem's explicit hypotheses still matter even when it is
closed under the global context.

Start with README.md and the proof contracts it links. Run make verify,
make verify-claims, and make verify-research in the documented build
environment. These checks provide reproducible evidence about their
defined scope; they do not certify every interpretation in an essay.

I want the argument to survive being read closely. If a definition
misses the phenomenon I intended, or a sentence exceeds its theorem,
that is a correction to make, even when the proof compiles.
