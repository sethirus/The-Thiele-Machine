THE THIELE MACHINE
Devon Thiele

THE POINT

Take anything that can be more than one way and isn't yet any of them for
good: a bead that could sit in either of two dips, a square of paper with
nothing on it, a question nobody has closed. Now let it settle one way, and
keep it there. Something is the case now that wasn't a moment ago. That is
a distinction that stays, and it is what this is about. Nothing becomes
something for free: under a rule that gives every state exactly one next
state, keeping something settled takes a merge somewhere in the run, two
states landing on one, or an endless supply of fresh states.

Structure is made of exactly this. The smallest distinction there is sorts
the ways a thing could be in two and holds one side: a bead in the left dip
and not the right, a mark on the page and not off it. A
grouping, an order, a relation a later move leans on, a fact somebody
found out and wrote down: each is a distinction that stays, paid for the same way as the bead in its dip. Insight, in the sense I can price, is a kept
distinction. The toll charges the moment it's raised; holding it up
afterward adds nothing. That is what the other name for the rule, No Free
Insight, means.

Established is the sharpest case. Two computers can end with the same
answer, and only one of them checked it. The difference between them is a
distinction one of them never kept. A record that a claim was checked and
closed is a distinction that stays, with a check in front of it, and it's
paid for the same way. The two boxes below are that case. It's the sharpest case
of what keeping takes, and it isn't the point. The point is the distinction that stays.


THE BOX AND THE PICTURE

Put two boxes on the table. The tiles on top spell the same answer. One box
has a stamp on its side and the other does not. Hang the camera above them.
The pictures agree.

Now ask which box was stamped. Look harder at the tiles. Count them. Make
the picture bigger. None of that puts the side of the box into the picture.
The difference was there when the picture was taken. The picture left it out.

I want to put that difference into the machine's description, follow the
operations that establish it, and count what keeping it takes. Computation,
record and accounting belong in the same drawing so I can see which arrows
touch which parts.

Gather every full state that gives the same picture. The tiles agree, but
the stamps and whatever else the camera misses can differ. That collection
is what I call the fibre above the picture.

The letters give short names for what just happened. S names all the full
states, B names their pictures, and pi names the act of taking the picture.
Writing pi: S -> B says where that act starts and where it lands. Every
picture in B comes from a full state; that is what "onto" means. For one
picture b, its fibre contains every state whose picture is b.

A flag gives one yes-or-no reading of a full state. A hundred states can all
read yes. The fibre can hold differences in checked claims, certified
editions, old receipts, or anything else the chosen picture omits. There is
no universal finite bound on its size. Which distinctions must actually be
there is a question about the task and what must remain recoverable.


THE DEFINITION

A Thiele machine is a thing, the directions it can move in, and a price on
each direction. A direction turns the thing into the next thing. A direction
that makes a distinction stay is never free.

That is the whole definition. A machine whose directions never make a
distinction stay is the flat case, and the rule has nothing to say to it: a
clock is one, a reversible process is one, and so is ordinary computation.
Tear off the price column, forget which distinctions stayed, and what is
left is a Turing machine. What follows is the account of what that forgetting throws away.

Now the same thing with names. S is the set of states and I the set of
moves. The step takes a state and a move to the next state. Each move i has
a nonnegative whole-number price c(i), the same wherever it is taken. The
record is a reading of the state into an ordered set: for any two records
you can ask whether one sits at or below the other. A move makes a
distinction stay when it carries the record somewhere not at or below where
it was. The one rule is the toll: such a move costs at least one. Nothing
else is assumed of the order, and nothing says what the record is about. A
ledger that only climbs, and a window onto what an outside observer sees,
come in when a result needs them.

The smallest record has two positions, no and yes. Then the record is a
yes-or-no reading of the state, making a distinction stay is turning no into
yes, and the toll says that move costs at least one. Most of what follows is stated on this smallest case, because it is the one you can point at: a lamp
beside a box, a stamp on its side, a flag that goes up. The flag is not the
record. It is the smallest question you can ask of one. The proofs state
the general case first and derive the flag as its two-point instance.

The Thiele Machine is that definition. It is a machine the way Turing's
machine is one: mathematics you can reason about, not a device, not an
instruction set and not a document. I named it after myself, on purpose. I
wanted to call it the meta machine, and in 2021 a company took that word,
so I went one level up and put my own name on it, half a joke and half a
stake in the ground. Turing got a machine; here's mine. What follows is
what I work out from it.

Every machine whose moves never climb the order is a Thiele machine in the
flat case, where the toll never charges: a clock, a reversible process, any
deterministic machine with its record standing still. The ordinary computer
is one member of that case, and only one. The computer is a shadow of the
machine, not the floor it is built on.

To ask what a computation hides, add a window. A sliced Thiele machine is a
Thiele machine on the two-position record, with an onto view pi: S -> B,
what an outside observer sees,
and its moves split in two. The tabletop B is the flat part, what the
window shows. Horizontal moves cost nothing, move the view by a
rule that uses only the view, and leave the reading alone. Vertical moves
leave the view alone and can raise the reading, never lower it. The toll
still holds.

Put a dial with four positions beside a slider with three stops. ROTATE
moves the dial one position around for free and leaves the slider alone.
ADVANCE moves the slider toward its last stop, leaves the dial alone and
costs one. At the last stop the reading is yes; at the other two it is no.
ADVANCE pays when it first reaches yes and still costs one when pressed
at the last stop. The price belongs to the button. Look only at the dial:
ROTATE moves the picture and ADVANCE leaves it alone. Those are the
horizontal and vertical moves, before any checking enters the story.

On the two-position record the toll asks nothing of the reading beyond the
price of turning it on. Permanence is what the sliced rules add. A ledger
is additional structure, not part of the definition.

The definition has to name the reading its toll belongs to. Slide a tile
to the right without paying and ask whether it is in the place it just
reached: the answer was no and is now yes, and if the toll charged every
question, no move could be free. And a free move cannot touch a reading
that stays. It cannot raise it, because a raise costs at least one, and it
cannot lower it, because a yes stays yes. It must leave it alone.

The price stays on the move. It does not disappear when you press the same
button with the reading already up, and the least price list is the plain
one: one on every move that can raise the reading from somewhere, zero on
every move that never does. Nothing smaller meets the toll. That is a
minimum in the definition's units; measuring a device's heat requires a
physical account of the device.


WHAT FOLLOWS FROM IT

It is one story, in four parts, and each part says what it rests on.

1. The definition is more general than any mechanism. It assumes no tape, no
head and no counters. Any machine whose moves never climb is in it as the
flat case; give one of them a record that an event of its own rule raises,
and charge the move that raises it, and it has left the flat case without
changing a step of what it did before. A run that starts at no and ends at
yes costs at least one. That universal floor holds for every machine, and
the bar is low on purpose: a clock that charges one for every move meets
it. The theorems with teeth come when you add one next state for every
state (part 2) or a window (part 3). What I would defend as mine is not any
one of the lemmas below, which are old. It is the arrangement: one law with
three columns and no fourth, a fresh state, a merge, or a loop that makes
nothing new, stated alike for deterministic, noisy and quantum steps, and
a toll on a reading that stays, which charges for keeping whichever column
pays for it.

2. Keeping requires a merge or fresh states. This is counting under a total,
deterministic next-state rule and a reading held after entry; it uses no
physics. Before the first revisit each step is fresh: the first column. The
first revisit returns to the start or merges two distinct sources, the third
column or the second; later steps repeat. A run that raises a reading and
keeps it up cannot take the third column, because its start read no and
every later state reads yes, so a return to the start would bring the
reading down. That leaves the merge or endlessly many distinct states. A machine with endlessly many states
can avoid the merge on paper by paying in fresh states. A machine with
finitely many states cannot: when only finitely many states read yes, a
holding run must eventually merge. The instruction that raises and preserves
the reading is non-injective, but the collision need not involve the entry
state or its destination. Whether a real device has finitely many
distinguishable states is a physical assumption, and I name it as one. When a coarse description of a machine
merges, the fine machine merges too, unless its step leaves the described
states or there are endlessly many of them. The toll is where the model
writes a price on that step, and the price is a choice: if every merging
instruction costs at least one, the toll on a finite machine follows from the
merge. A heat bound needs one more premise, a named physical one, and I keep it apart from the counting.

3. The ordinary machine and the full machine, in both directions.
In a sliced machine, the view pi(S) = B with its horizontal moves is a
flat machine, an ordinary deterministic one. Every deterministic machine
is such a view: it is the shadow of the machine that keeps one bit above it. The view cannot
see the record, because the free moves never touch it and the picture follows
the computation step for step.
That needs a raise to exist, and nothing else: no premise about what the
view computes and none about the control.
The view cannot see the record or the ledger, and no function of the view
recovers either: two runs can end on the same picture with the record up on
one and down on the other, and the ledger apart by the price of the raise.
An account that prices a raise exactly cannot compute the price from a
window that shows a raising step and a quiet step the same way.
If all choices of which of n editions were certified are independently
possible and exactly recoverable above one final picture, they require
2^n full states there.
A small six-instruction machine and a universal program on it show these
definitions can be met. They are examples and not the subject.

4. What is argued and what is proved. The counting, the floor, the window
results and the flat case are theorems under the hypotheses they state.
The mathematical claims have their hypotheses and arguments on the page.
The pointer thesis is a thesis, not a theorem. It proposes certification as
the event worth recording, because certified records get copied and relied on
by parties who did not produce them. Separately, and unnumbered, I argue
that the record and its price are part of computation itself and not
something added afterwards, and I state one thesis about physical
processes: that every physical process which computes with final answers
has an exact description as a machine run by one rule, with a next state
for every state, on which the answer is a reading no run ever turns off.
No proof settles that, because a physical process is not a mathematical
object. Neither is presented as a theorem.


WHAT I FOUND, WHAT I ASSUMED AND WHAT I CHOSE

Most of the time I had the hunch first and found the names later. Over and
over, somebody had been here before me. Sometimes that made my day.
Sometimes it pissed me off. Here is the line between what nothing let me
choose, what I had to assume, and what I did choose.

Found, which counting forces on any machine. Run a machine that gives each
state exactly one next state. It takes fresh states until it first comes
back to one it has used; that return lands on its start or is a merge, two
states with one next state; and after it the run only repeats steps it
already took. There is no fourth thing a step can do. So a reading that
goes from no to yes and never comes back is paid for in a merge or in
fresh states, and when only finitely many states read yes, the move that
raises it merges. Over any machine at all, a record that the computation
drives and that never comes down is a latch on one event. That is
counting, and the pigeonhole in it is older than the computer: Dedekind's
finiteness, Zermelo's recurrence objection to Boltzmann, Landauer and
Bennett on merging, and the floor Tarjan's potential method puts under a
run.

Assumed, what the found list is taken to stand on in the world. Landauer's
principle: a merge of states the machine really could have been in has a
floor under its heat, however slowly it is done. That is the premise from
thermodynamics, named and never proved here, with its critics named. A
real device can tell apart only finitely many states in a bounded place,
so its fresh states run out; nobody has measured where that ceiling sits.
And the clean start: a raised record means the event happened only because
the record was down at the start, and that start is put in by hand. It is
a past hypothesis in miniature, Boltzmann's idea under David Albert's name.

Chosen, what the model adds, each of which could have gone another way. To
put the account inside the step, as part of what a step is. The toll
itself: a whole unit, at least one, on the move that climbs. Which readings
matter: the ones that never come down. Which event gets a record, and what
price list it pays. And on the example machine, where its flat part ends
and its record begins.


WHAT A KEPT ANSWER REQUIRES

Give the box one rule for its next tick, and require a next tick everywhere.
It starts unfinished, then enters a collection of configurations where the
answer is held. From any configuration in that collection, the next tick
must stay in the collection too.

Suppose there are K such holding configurations. Include the unfinished
entrant and you have K+1 sources whose next states fit in K places. Two
arrows must land together. That is the merge.

What if the rule never sends different sources to the same destination?
Then the holding run cannot repeat. From two equal visits, walk one step
back on each: their predecessors must agree, since different predecessors
never land together. Walk back until the earlier visit reaches the unfinished
start. The later visit is still holding the answer. They cannot be the same
state. So every later holding tick needs another full configuration.

For a finite interval, K holding states permit at most K transitions from
distinct sources without a collision, starting at the unfinished state.
This maximum is attainable. Draw

  u -> h_1 -> h_2 -> ... -> h_K -> h_K.

The first K sources have different successors. Include h_K as a source and
its arrow meets the arrow from its predecessor. With K>1 this happens after
entry. A visible answer can stay fixed while the hidden configuration changes
and the eventual merge happens behind it.


PUT THE PRICE BESIDE THE ARROWS

The basic model supplies nonnegative instruction prices and a chosen reading.
Its toll is

  no -> yes implies price >= 1.

A run that starts at no and ends at yes contains a first crossing. That move
costs at least one, and the other prices are nonnegative. Add them and the
run costs at least one. This is the universal floor under the stated toll.

A stronger pricing rule can connect the bill to the holding graph. If every
instruction that merges distinct full states costs at least one, a permanent
raise into a finite held region pays by the counting argument above.
If each landing pile accepts at most 2^p distinct sources, N starting
alternatives and q surviving alternatives satisfy N <= 2^p q for one step.
Across steps with prices p_0,...,p_(T-1), multiply these inequalities:

  N <= 2^(p_0 + ... + p_(T-1)) q_T.

Write the pricing premise beside the bound. A natural-number ledger is not
a temperature or a heat measurement. A thermal conclusion needs the device,
input preparation and physical law stated too.


THE HORIZONTAL MOVES AND THE VERTICAL MOVES

Start with an ordinary working move. It changes the tiles by a rule that
consults only the tiles and the instruction you gave it. Whatever stamps
are on the side, the same tiles and the same instruction give the same next
tiles. The move also leaves the side record alone. That is the horizontal
rule I want.

Now shorten it. Call the full move F_i, its tile rule h_i, and the record
reading r. Starting from a full state s, either move the box and then
photograph it, or photograph it and apply the tile rule. The pictures
must agree:

  pi(F_i(s)) = h_i(pi(s)).

Keeping the record says r(F_i(s)) = r(s). These horizontal moves have zero
price on this ledger.

A vertical establishment move does the other job. The tiles stay put while
the record can rise. Call that move F_v. Taking the photograph before or
after gives the same picture:

  pi(F_v(s)) = pi(s).

The record never goes backwards: r(s) <= r(F_v(s)). For a yes-or-no flag,
that means a down flag may rise and an up flag stays up. The toll charges
at least one unit when it rises. A richer record can answer more questions;
the two-valued flag is the simplest reading to start with.

Take two boxes with the same tiles and different records. Give them the
same sequence of horizontal instructions. The first instruction sees the
same tiles, so it produces the same next tiles. Neither record changes.
The second instruction again sees matching tiles, and gives matching tiles.
Repeat to the end. The entire film agrees while the records remain different.
Handing that film to someone cannot tell them which record was there.


A RECORD THAT MEANS SOMETHING

Write an answer on a whiteboard. Under it write CHECKED = YES. Somebody who
did no check can write the same two lines. The writing alone cannot tell me
which happened.

A variable called checked has the same weakness if any allowed program can
set it. To make an accepting record mean a check occurred, the machine must
close the unchecked route to acceptance. Suppose a checked and an unchecked
history could end at the same full state. The verdict at that state would
have to accept one history and reject the other. There is only one state
there to read. It cannot do both.

This is why representing a record and enforcing its meaning are separate
jobs. The representation keeps a distinction. The instruction rules govern
how a program can reach it. An implementation has to preserve both.

Paying to raise a record does not, by itself, establish what that record
means.


THE EARNED-CHAIN COUNTER EXAMPLE

This is an example and not the Thiele Machine. The model is defined over every
machine that meets it. This small machine is here to show that the
definition can be met, and to give the argument something to run.

The example's state contains two natural-number counters, their edition
numbers, a sixteen-entry fact table, a commitment channel, a trap, a ledger
and a permanent flag. INC and DEC compute. CHECK, COMMIT and CERTIFY enforce
the earned chain and cost one each. HALT stops the guest program. The full rules, including failure and idle behavior, are proved move by move.

From a clean start, certification costs at least three because its three
required acts each cost one. A true claim checked, committed and certified
without an intervening write attains three. From any other start, a
commitment already in the channel or a flag already up, the count is a
different one.

Universal host programs follow the guest through the host's instructions and compare their records and ledgers at the matching points. The constructions study composition and the boundaries where one component can
invalidate another's earned claim. A shared writer that changes a counter
without advancing its edition opens precisely the stale-check route the
machine was meant to close.

Its earned chain.

It uses an earned chain. CHECK tests a claim about a
counter and enters a fact naming the claim, the counter and its current
edition. COMMIT selects a matching fact only while that edition is current.
CERTIFY requires a commitment, preserves the channel and raises the permanent
flag. Writing a counter advances its edition, so an earlier check cannot be
presented as a check of that newly written counter.

Start with an empty table, empty channel and flag down. If the flag goes up,
CERTIFY must have succeeded: no other instruction raises it. Its channel
must have been filled by COMMIT. That commitment must have found a fact
supplied by CHECK. Between that check and commitment the counter's edition
must still match. Following the three entry points backwards gives the
check, commitment and certification in their required order.

An instruction word may ask for any of these actions. It cannot dictate a
successful checker result or directly manufacture an accepting channel.
That boundary is part of this machine's rules.

Its trap.

It has one more moving part: a trap. A failed CHECK blows
the fuse, and later free moves leave the whole state still. Hide that failed
check under pi and demand autonomous free moves on every state. A live
state and its trapped successor now have the same picture. Since the trapped
one cannot move, their common projected next step must also stand still.
A failing predicate can be chosen at every native state, so every free move
becomes identity on the image of pi. The live-state slice avoids that
collapse by making its computational promise only while the machine is live.
A failed check leaves that domain.


OPEN THE GAUGE WIDER

The count below is a fibre of one projection on the counter example.

Now look at the example machine through a wider camera. Let the observer
see every native field, and add a separate file recording which claim/edition
entries CERTIFY published. The native flag is exactly the file's nonempty
reading on clean runs: successful CERTIFY both inserts a fact and raises
the flag; every other instruction preserves both readings.

Initialize A=16, B=0, both editions zero, line 1, ledger zero, an empty
table and channel, and both trap and flag down. This is the initial state,
not an executed write. Check A>=0 through A>=15. All sixteen checks pass on
edition zero. The resulting line is 17, the ledger is 16, the table has all
sixteen entries and the flag is still down.
For each fact choose one of two blocks:

  publish:      COMMIT fact; CERTIFY
  do not:       COMMIT fact; COMMIT fact

Both blocks take two instructions, charge two and end with the same fact in
the channel. Neither changes the counters, editions or table. The first also
publishes that fact and raises the flag; the second leaves publication alone.
After sixteen blocks the line is 49 and the ledger is 48. Every nonempty
publication choice ends with exactly the same full native state and flag up.
There are 2^16-1 = 65,535 such choices, with different publication files.

This is also the maximum. Every published fact came from a commitment backed
by the table, and table entries are never removed. The final publication set
is a subset of that table, which has at most sixteen entries. Put a bit beside
each entry to say whether it was published. Those bits recover the set; a
nonempty set uses a nonzero code. Sixteen bits suffice, and fifteen have only
32,768 values, too few for the exhibited 65,535 alternatives.

Here the fibre is above a whole native state, not just above its counter
values. Publication order and repetitions ask different questions from
membership in the publication set. The camera and the question stay named
throughout the count.


LOOK BEFORE THE VALUE DISAPPEARS

Suppose one job used sound rebar and another used bad rebar. Pour the concrete
so that the finished surfaces look exactly alike. Give the inspector only
those surfaces. Whatever verdict the inspector gives one, the same evidence
gets the same verdict on the other. Looking harder cannot restore what the
surface omitted. Finding a sound arrangement that could have been underneath
proves a possibility; it does not show what was actually there.

If the question is known before the pour, inspect while the rebar is visible
and keep the answer. For a succession of required checks, start a flag at YES.
A passing check leaves it alone. A failing check turns it to NO, and nothing
in the remaining checks turns it back up. After one check it tells you whether
that check passed. After the next it stays YES only if both passed. Carry the
same rule to the end and it tells you whether all of them passed.

Late questions ask for something else. Suppose the inspector may arrive at
the end, name any stage, and ask any yes-or-no question about the value there.
Two different histories disagree at some stage. Ask whether that stage held
the value from the first history: one answer is yes and the other is no.
A summary that answers every such question cannot identify those histories.

If each of k stages can hold any of M values, the first stage gives M choices,
the second multiplies that by M, and each further stage does the same.
There are M^k ordered histories, and every one needs a different summary.
Keeping the ordered history itself meets that count and answers the questions.


THE WIDER ARGUMENT

A growing ordered record has threshold readings: ask whether a particular
level has been reached, and once the answer becomes yes it stays yes. I follow those latches, study the recursion theorem with its input conventions, and make cost-framework comparisons under their stated rules.

The count in part 2 does not need the step to be deterministic. A noisy
step on finitely many states that keeps the even spread cannot raise a
reading that stays, and one that can raise it strictly concentrates the
spread. A unitary step on a finite-dimensional space that keeps a subspace
keeps its complement too, so no such step raises a reading that stays.
Each is the same obstruction in its own setting, and endless room is the
only way past it in each.

Its protocol examples, including RFC 9162, concern specified parts of those
systems. An observer model says what a particular window reveals. The
pointer thesis is a thesis about how that picture connects to the world.
Its proposed interpretation and the mathematical implications of a chosen
model have different jobs, and I state both.


HOW TO READ THE ARGUMENT

Start with the boxes and the camera, then follow the holding count, the toll,
the window and the earned chain. The history count opens the gauge to
more questions. Its proof shows why each extra recoverable answer needs
room in the full state.

Follow a move through the full state, then look at what reaches the window.
Put the price beside that move and add it to the ledger. Finally follow the
earned chain backwards from the raised flag to its check. These are the connections built here, with the constructions and their reasons on
the page.
