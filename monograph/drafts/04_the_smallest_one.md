The smallest one

The last chapter put a price on the move that settles something. Before I build
the smallest machine that carries that price, there's a question I had to ask
first. People have been making marks that other people are supposed to rely on
for a very long time. Did any of them put a price on the moment the mark goes on
and stays? I wanted to know what they thought they were doing, and what it cost
them. So here's what I could reach, and where the search ran out.


Clay, seals, and seven witnesses

Start a little over five thousand years ago, in the Near East, with people who
counted sheep with little clay tokens. To send a count across a distance without
the courier changing it, they put the tokens inside a hollow clay ball, pressed
the shapes of the tokens into the outside while the clay was soft, and sealed it.
The archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat has spent a career on these, and she
puts the practice at around 3300 BC; the ball is called a bulla.

You can do a version of it with a cookie tin. Glue a paper seal across the lid
and the rim, let it dry, and then try to open the lid without tearing the seal.
The bare lid would come straight off. The seal is what turns opening it into
something you can see afterwards. A substitute seal could fool you, so the tin
shows that somebody disturbed it, not that nobody could ever fake it. The clay
ball does the same: the outside shows what's inside, and opening it breaks it.
Keep that idea, because it comes back: a record is worth something only if it
can't be quietly undone.

Now law, which is where people get serious about who's allowed to say what
happened. The book of Deuteronomy, chapter nineteen: nobody is convicted on the
word of one witness; it takes "two or three." The Roman jurist Gaius, writing in
the second century, says a will the court will honour is one sealed by seven
witnesses. Why seven? Gaius doesn't say. The legal historian Henry Maine, in
1861, had an answer: the older Roman will was a mock sale, done in front of five
witnesses, a man holding the scales, and the buyer of the estate, seven people in
all, and when the ceremony fell away the seven seals kept the count. Maine's is
the only account of that I found, so treat it as his.

Try it. Write "paid five dollars, signed A" on a napkin and ask a friend to forge
it. It takes them ten seconds. Now have two other people initial it at the moment
of payment and each keep a copy. If whoever reads it later compares those copies
and trusts the people holding them, changing your napkin alone gets caught.
Stolen copies, forged initials or a reader who never compares them can still beat
it. The claim didn't change. What stands behind it did.

Which brings me to seals, and the medieval historian Michael Clanchy, whose book
is called From Memory to Written Record. England between the Norman Conquest and
the early 1300s was learning to trust writing. Until then it had trusted people
who remembered. The lesson was painful: seals were forged, charters were forged,
and historians today have a whole craft of telling real charters from fakes
because there are so many fakes. A seal is only as good as whoever holds the
stamp. In 2011 a company called DigiNotar, which issued the certificates web
browsers trust, found out the same thing at the cost of its existence: a
mathematically perfect signature is worth exactly what the custody of the key is
worth, and intruders had taken over the machines that signed. It's the same
failure nine hundred years apart, and I find that oddly comforting.

And then the obvious next question: if seals could be forged, what caught the
forgeries? It wasn't the seals. The most famous forged record in European history
is the Donation of Constantine, a document in which the emperor Constantine
supposedly handed rule over Rome and the western empire to the Pope. It was most
likely written in the eighth century, some four hundred years after Constantine
died, and it was leaned on for centuries. Around 1440 a scholar named Lorenzo
Valla took it apart by its Latin: the language belonged to a much later age, and
it used words, "satraps" among them, that no Roman of Constantine's day would
have used for his officials. (Valla wasn't even first. Nicholas of Cusa, a German
churchman who later became a cardinal, had already called it a forgery in 1433. I
know that from the reference works; I haven't read his book. Valla's is the
version people remember, which is a pattern that keeps turning up.)

Look at what kind of check that was. The question wasn't "is the stamp real." It
was "could these words have been written when they say they were." A record can
pass the first and fail the second. That was my first hint that what I kept
calling one thing, a record, was doing more than one job.


Two columns and a stamp

The one I'd never have guessed mattered is bookkeeping. Around 1300, the ledger
of a Florentine company called Farolfi, kept at its branch in Provence, shows the
earliest complete example anyone has found of a trick merchants were working out:
write every transaction twice, once where the money came from and once where it
went, and the two columns have to balance. The treasurers of the city of Genoa
kept their ledger for 1340 that way. And in 1494 a friar named Luca Pacioli
printed the method in a mathematics book, and it spread.

Do it for one day. Keep two columns of every dollar that moves, and total them at
night. Have a friend secretly change one entry in one column. The totals
disagree; you catch it. Now have them change the matching entry in both columns.
The totals agree, and you've caught nothing. So that's what double entry
actually is: a check for slips, which a careful liar walks straight through.

Did Pacioli see that? I can only get at it secondhand. A scholar named Forrester,
in a paper on the University of Strathclyde's accounting-history pages, reads him
as reporting that in many places the books were taken to the local consul to be
stamped before a single entry went in. The stamp is a witness again, from
outside. The two columns are a method. The stamp is somebody else agreeing to
stand behind the fact that these are the books. Keep those two apart, because one
is a habit and the other is a commitment.

Then I found where this got turned into a motto. The Royal Society, founded in
London in 1660 and still running, picked as its motto Nullius in verba: take
nobody's word for it. Where did they get it? From somebody's words, naturally.
It's half a line of the Roman poet Horace, from a letter to his patron, saying he
isn't bound to swear by the words of any master. So the society that takes
nobody's word for it took its motto from a poet and cut the line to fit. That's
not a contradiction. It's a nice picture of the method: borrow what's useful, say
whose it was, and don't swear by it.

The historian Steven Shapin wrote about what that motto meant in practice, and
the answer is more interesting than the slogan. Robert Boyle, pumping the air out
of a glass globe to see what happens to a candle or a bird, had three problems:
the machine, the report, and the witnesses. He solved the third by having
gentlemen in the room who'd sign their names, and the second by writing the
report so richly that a reader who wasn't there could picture the scene and count
as a witness anyway. Shapin calls that virtual witnessing, and he notes, dryly,
that the air pump was very expensive, the big science of the 1600s. So there was
a cost. It just wasn't a cost of declaring the fact. It was the cost of the
instrument and of getting enough people into the room. Hold on to that
difference, because the rest of the book hangs on it.


Closer to now

The rest of the lineage points the same way, so I'll go through it quickly.

Regulators who made stockbrokers keep their records on media that can't be
rewritten (the SEC required exactly that, write once and read many, from 1997
until 2022, when it allowed an audit trail instead) were paying for the hardware.
The rule put no charge on the act of writing.

Stuart Haber and Scott Stornetta, in 1991, chained time-stamped records together
so that changing an old entry breaks every entry after it. Bitcoin's footnotes
lead back to them. Try the idea with a running total. Take five entries, 1, 2,
3, 4, 5, and add each to the total so far: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Now change the second
entry to 3. The totals become 1, 4, 7, 11, 16. The change travels all the way
down. Now also lower the third entry to 2, and the last total is 15 again.
Simple addition can be fooled by changes that cancel out. A real chain uses a
much harder function than addition, and somebody has to keep a trusted copy of
the last value to compare against. (Ralph Merkle's hash tree, from his 1979
Stanford thesis, lets one short value stand for a great many records the same
way.) What the running total shows you is how each entry depends on the ones
before it. It doesn't put a price on writing one.

Proof of work, which Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor proposed in 1992 to make junk
mail expensive, and which Bitcoin later chained, is the one place where a record
really does cost something. But what it charges for is a search: how many tries
it took to find a lucky number. An empty block and a full one face the same
target. The work pays for the search. Nothing extra gets charged for the moment
anything becomes settled.

Robert Floyd and Tony Hoare, asking in 1967 and 1969 how you'd prove a program
does what it's supposed to, attached claims to points in a program: "whenever you
get here, this is true." The claim is backed by a proof, and the proof is paid
for somewhere else, off the machine.

The certificates of complexity theory, going back to Stephen Cook in 1971, are
answers designed to be quick to check: hand me the certificate and I can confirm
the claim fast. Whether they're also hard to find is the famous open question of
the field, P versus NP, and nobody knows. Either way, what's bounded is the
checking. Nothing is charged for raising a flag.

And from statistics, a ledger that runs the other way. Dean Foster and Robert
Stine's alpha-investing, from 2008, runs a long string of tests against a budget
of allowed false alarms: every test spends some of the budget, and every test
that finds something earns some back. That's a budget for how often you're
allowed to be wrong. A reward there doesn't mean the discovery was true.


Five jobs

Lay them all down together, and what shows through isn't one thing. It's five
different jobs the word "record" has been doing, and I'd been letting one word
carry all five. Most of them come with a corpse.

Durability: the record can't be quietly undone. The clay ball, the write-once
disk and the hash chain do this one. The corpse is the editable log, and the
company that kept its debts off the books: Enron hid debt in side companies set
up to keep it off the balance sheet, which is how the Justice Department
described it in 2002, and its auditor, Arthur Andersen, shredded paper. Andersen
was convicted that year, and the Supreme Court threw the conviction out in 2005
over how the jury had been instructed. Notice that this job doesn't need a
witness at all. A copy will do, as long as somebody else holds it.

Authenticity: the record came from who it says. Seals, signatures and the
consul's stamp do this one. The corpse is the forged charter, and DigiNotar. A
perfectly authentic entry can still be erased, and a perfectly durable one can
still be a forgery. They're different jobs.

Evidence: enough independent people stand behind the claim that lying gets
expensive. Two witnesses, seven seals and Boyle's gentlemen. This one is about
how many people put their own risk next to the claim. Paper has nothing to do
with it.

Accounting: the record checks itself for slips. The two columns. And the second
column isn't an outside witness. It's you, checking you. It catches a dropped
digit, and a careful liar gets past it.

Quick checking: a certificate, signature or fingerprint that makes one particular
check fast. Checking doesn't have to be cheaper than making, and I once thought
it had to. Plenty of records are checked by doing the whole thing over, and that's
fine when the thing is small.

Now the thing none of them says. Not one of those jobs puts a price on the moment
a record goes on and stays. Proof of work prices a search. Bookkeeping prices a
habit. Witnesses and seals price showing up and guarding the stamp. I looked,
with everything I had, and that's where it ran out. If somebody priced that
moment itself, they're on a shelf I never found.

So I had to try it myself, on the smallest thing I could build.


Two boxes and a coin

Take a sheet of paper. Draw two boxes and label them NO and YES. NO is not done,
YES is done. Put a coin in NO. Draw a line underneath for tally marks; that's
where you keep score.

You get one move. Call it the stamp. From NO, the coin slides to YES. From YES,
it stays in YES. Every time you make the move, whichever box you're in, you put
down one tally mark.

Play. Start in NO, make the move as many times as you like, and look at the
tally. Now try to get to YES with no marks at all. You can't, and you can see
why: the only way from NO to YES is the stamp, and the stamp makes a mark.

Now cross out the mark in the stamp's rule and play again. You get to YES with no
marks, every time. That's how little the mark is. The board doesn't force it.
Chapter three is the reason it's there: finite chairs, done stays done, and
Bennett's rule. Here it's written on a card.

Draw the arrows for the stamp. NO goes to YES. YES goes to YES. Two arrows, one
landing place. It's the same picture as 4 and 5 both going to 5 on page one.

That's a Thiele machine. A thing (where the coin is). A move (the stamp). A way to
tell how settled it is (NO or YES), where YES never goes back to NO. A run that
actually settles it (start in NO, stamp once). A price on the move (one mark,
never a refund). And the toll holds: the move that takes NO to YES costs one.

Is there a smaller one? Try.

One box? Then there's nothing to settle. The coin is always in the same place,
so no run ever takes it from not done to done.

Two boxes, then. A move either sends the two boxes to two different boxes, or it
sends both to the same one. If it keeps them apart, there are only two ways to
do that: leave each where it is, or swap them. Leaving them where they are never
settles anything. Swapping them is the coin from the second door: it takes NO to
YES, but it also takes YES back to NO, and done has to stay done. So a two-box
machine that settles anything has to have a move that sends both boxes to the
same box, and since YES has to stay YES, that box is YES. That's the stamp.

So nothing smaller works, and look at what that means. Chapters one and two
found that keeping something takes two arrows meeting or endless fresh
paper. Here there are only two boxes, so there's no fresh paper to be had, and
the arrows meet. The smallest machine there is already has the merge inside it,
in plain view. Nobody added it. A fuse that blows, a bead that drops into a dip
and stays, a stamp pressed onto a box: each one is two states and one move that
sends both to the done side, and each one, with that move priced at one, is this
machine.

I'll call it the latch, because that's what it is: something that catches and
holds.


What the tally is and isn't

Why tally marks? Because they're about the oldest receipt there is. The real
ones, notched sticks split down the middle, get their own chapter later, fire and
all. The tally on your paper is nothing like those. Nobody holds the other half.
You keep it, beside the board, and you could rub a mark out and nobody would ever
know.

Hold on to that. Of the five jobs, the latch does a small piece of the first one:
none of its moves takes YES back to NO. It has no witnesses, no signature, no
second column, and nothing in it checks anything. YES means the coin is in YES.
That's all it means. Whether a YES can ever be made to mean "this was checked and
can be relied on" is a real question, and it's the one the second half of the
book is about. For now the tally is a scorekeeper's receipt: you know what you
paid, and nobody else has any reason to believe you.


Any machine can wear one

Here's something the latch lets you do to any machine at all.

Take any machine, any states, any moves. Pick one state, call it x, that some run
can reach from somewhere else. Now build a new machine right beside it: the same states and the same
moves, with one extra coin riding along in its own two boxes, NO and YES. The
coin starts in NO. The first time a move lands on x, the coin goes to YES, and it
never comes back. Price every move at one.

Check it against the definition. The coin never goes back to NO. The run that
reaches x moves it to YES. Every move costs one, so the toll holds. It's a
Thiele machine.

Now cover the extra coin with your hand and watch the rest. What you see is the
machine you started with, move for move. Nothing about it changed. The coin rode
along and the old machine never knew.

And with your hand over the coin, you can't tell which box it's in. Let any spot
with the coin in NO count as a place to start. Now find a moment where the old
machine is sitting on x. Did it just arrive there, which put the coin up? Or did
it start there, with the coin still down? Both can happen, and the part you can
see looks exactly the same. The coin is the only place the difference lives.

So every machine that ever goes anywhere it didn't start is what's left of a
Thiele machine once you cover the coin. Turing's machine read through "has it
stopped?" doesn't even need the extra coin: chapter one showed it already has a
done that stays done, and stopping is already a step where two arrows meet. Both
ways, it points the same direction page one did. The machine with the price in it
is the general thing. The computer is that machine with the price left out.


Which question gets charged?

One more puzzle, because it shows why the definition picks one question and
charges only that one.

Put a tile on a table and slide it one place to the right. Let that move be free.
Now ask a new question: is the tile in the place it just reached? Before the
slide, no. After it, yes. If every question had to follow the toll, that slide
would have to cost at least one.

Try it with any free move that changes anything. Ask "is the machine where this
move just put it?" The answer goes from no to yes. So if the toll applied to every
question you could ask, every move that changes anything would cost something, and
nothing could ever be free. That's why the toll belongs to one question, the one
the machine says is "how settled," and not to every question you could think of.

And for that one question, a free move can't touch it. It can't take NO to YES,
because the toll charges that. It can't take YES to NO, because done stays done.
So a free move leaves the settled question exactly where it found it. That's the
whole reason the free moves and the settling moves come apart so cleanly.


The cheapest price list

Last thing about the latch. Put three price cards next to a button: zero, one and
two. Somewhere in the machine, pressing that button takes NO to YES. Cross out
zero; the toll won't allow it. One works. Two works too. The toll picked the
smallest card it could, without forbidding the bigger ones.

Now take a button that never takes NO to YES, from anywhere. Zero works. So go
through the buttons: one on each button that can settle something somewhere, zero
on each button that never does. That's the cheapest price list that follows the
toll, and there's only one cheapest. Notice the price stays on the button: the
stamp costs one even when you press it on YES and nothing changes. The toll is a
floor, not an exact bill.

And it counts. Every time a run goes from NO to YES, that step costs at least one,
so a run costs at least as much as the number of times it settled something. On
the latch that's at most once, because YES stays YES. On a switch that can be
turned off for free, you could settle it and unsettle it a hundred times, and
you'd pay at least a hundred. A latch pays once, and keeps it.
