Settled

The last chapter tied a record to a check: the record goes up only right after a
check of that very claim passed. But I kept saying two things in one breath, the
check and the closing, and they're not the same thing. This chapter pulls them
apart, and then asks a question that bothered me the whole time I was writing the
last one: is "established" really a switch, on or off? Or is it something with
more than two places?


Settled, whether it's true or not

I ran into Charles Sanders Peirce again here, the American scientist and
philosopher, in an 1877 essay called "The Fixation of Belief." He was asking what
inquiry is for, and his answer named the thing I'd been running together without
noticing. The point of inquiry, he says, is to get out of doubt and settle on a
belief. And when someone objects that surely what people want is a true belief, he
answers like a guy who's watched a lot of arguments end: "as soon as a firm belief
is reached we are entirely satisfied, whether the belief be true or false."

That's funny, and then it isn't, because he's right. Settling and being right are
two different events. A question can be closed on a wrong answer, and once it's
closed it acts closed. Peirce cared a great deal about which ways of settling hold
up; science wins, for him, because it has to answer to something outside any one
person. But the settling itself, the question snapping shut, happens every time,
and it never comes with a guarantee.

Karl Popper came at it from the other side. He was a philosopher from Vienna asking
what separates science from everything else, and in his 1934 book, the one that
came into English as The Logic of Scientific Discovery, he says every test has to
stop somewhere, at some statement somebody decides to accept, and nothing in the
evidence forces you to stop there instead of one step further. His picture is a
jury: the verdict is a decision about what happened, and the jury deciding it
doesn't make it true. Science, for him, is a building on piles driven into a
swamp, and the piles stop where somebody's satisfied they'll hold. They never reach
solid ground.

A real jury has a problem Popper doesn't mention, because a real jury is twelve
people. Lewis Kornhauser and Lawrence Sager, two law professors, noticed in 1986
that a court that votes by majority on each part of a case, and also on the
verdict, can end up contradicting itself, even when every judge is consistent.
Christian List and Philip Pettit turned that into a theorem in 2002: no way of
combining people's yes-or-no judgments on connected questions meets a short list of
fair-looking conditions and always hands back a consistent answer for the group.

Try it with three ballots. The rule is: a payment is owed only if a contract was
signed and a deadline was missed. One voter says yes to both. One says signed but
not missed. One says missed but not signed. Each voter applies the rule
consistently: one says owed, two say not owed. Now count the questions separately.
A majority says signed. A majority says missed. And a majority says not owed. You
can point at the contradiction on the paper without blaming any one voter's logic.
A group's settling is a rule's settling, and which rule you pick decides which
honest settling sticks. I haven't tried to put a group on the machine, and that's
where I'd expect the first wall.

Popper's swamp goes further down, because it's the oldest problem there is. Ask
why you believe something and you get a reason. Ask why you believe the reason and
you get another one. Sextus Empiricus, a skeptic and doctor who probably lived in
the second or third century, wrote down the ancient version, credited to someone
called Agrippa, and in 1968 the German philosopher Hans Albert gave it a name with a
joke in it: the Münchhausen trilemma, after the baron who claimed he pulled himself
out of a swamp by his own hair. Every chain of reasons does one of three things. It
goes on forever. It loops back on itself. Or it stops somewhere, because somebody
decided it stops.

It's the same shape as chapter one's step law, with reasons in place of states. Follow a chain: it keeps finding
new reasons, or it comes back to one it already used, or it stops. There's no fourth
thing. So "when am I done checking?" doesn't get a clean answer from anybody. What you
can do is be honest about where you stopped and why, and that's a lot of what this
book is: every place it stops is written down, with its name on it, so you can decide
whether you'd have stopped there too.

Lay Peirce and Popper on top of each other and what comes through is that the check
is one act and the stopping is another. The check can run as long as you like and it
never makes you stop. At some point something decides, and the deciding is its own
event. That's the copier's whole trick from the last chapter: it skipped the check
and kept the answer.


A proof is an act

Then a logician said the part I didn't expect a logician to say. Per Martin-Löf, a
Swedish logician, in lectures he gave in Siena in 1983: "a proof is, not an object,
but an act ... Only secondarily, and irrevocably, does it become the act that has
been performed." That took me a few reads, and the tenses opened it up: first the act
being done, then, for good, the act that has been done.

You could say that's true of everything that ever happened. It's the two computers
again: the sorter's check happened, for good, and the machine right now carries no
trace of it. The past can't be undone. The record of it can be lost, unless something
keeps one. That gap is where this book lives.

Then I found the people who had already built a record like that, on paper, and it
came clear only when I sorted them by three words: the act, the thing it produces,
and the trace the act leaves behind.

The Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer held that a mathematical truth is something
a mind makes evident, and from 1948 on he argued about an idealised mathematician
working in stages, the creating subject. The logician Georg Kreisel, in 1967, wrote
it as a formula: the subject has evidence for a statement at stage n. That's a trace,
something you can come back and ask about after the act is over. Anne Troelstra, a
Dutch logician, added in 1969 that the subject never forgets what it has made evident,
so the trace never fades. That's how Mark van Atten credits it in his 2018 history,
down to the page of Troelstra's book where it was added, page 95, and he suggests
calling the whole thing the Kreisel-Troelstra theory. The Stanford Encyclopedia's
entry lists the never-forgetting rule among the three axioms Kreisel gave in 1967.
Walter Dean and Hidenori Kurokawa's list of Kreisel's three, in a 2021 paper on his
methods, doesn't have it. That's two sources for Troelstra and one for Kreisel, and
I haven't seen Kreisel's own page, so I still haven't settled it. Van Atten reads the formula as "by stage n the
creating subject has made it evident." Look at the tense: has made. That's
Martin-Löf's "the act that has been performed," reached from the other end. Van Atten
even says the subject can't just accept a proof handed to it from outside; it has to go
through the steps and rebuild the evidence itself, which is the copier who runs the
check. And Göran Sundholm, a philosopher of logic at Leiden, has long kept the proof
as an act apart from the proof as a thing, and in 2014 worked through how Brouwer's
creating subject sits beside the idea that every procedure is a program. I know that
paper only by what it sets out to do, so I lean on it lightly.

Set their trace next to the record this book wants. Theirs happens at a stage; mine
goes up at a definite step in the run. Theirs never forgets; mine never goes back.
Theirs is never wrong; mine is earned. Nearly every column fills. The one that stays
empty is the price, and I think I see why. An idealised mind with unlimited memory pays
for never forgetting with fresh paper, and an idealised mind has endless fresh paper,
so nobody charges it. On a finite machine the fresh paper runs out, and keeping the
record makes two arrows meet. That's chapter two. So theirs is the closest thing to
established I've found, and it's been around for sixty years.

Push on mine from their side and it gives twice. Van Atten's subject won't take a proof
from outside, and my machine takes exactly one: its checker is trusted on a proof about
the checker, done once, outside every run. And their evidence is never wrong because,
for Brouwer, made evident is what true means. Mine is right only as far as the checker
is honest.


Not a switch

So far I've talked about established as if it were a light switch: on or off, the
sorter on and the copier off. That bothered me the whole time I was writing it, and
for a while I couldn't say why. It felt too small. I don't trust that feeling on its
own, because I've had it plenty of times about things that turned out to be nothing.
So I went looking for something outside my own head that would either give the
feeling something to stand on or show me I was imagining it.

I didn't know what I was looking for at first, beyond a rough shape: two things that
look exactly the same and differ in how far something about them has been settled. So
I started collecting anything that seemed to have that shape. Most of it fell apart as
soon as I looked closely, and how each one fell apart taught me more than the thing
itself did.

The first batch was all hidden things. A pea under one of three cups. A tree, whose age
you can't see from outside. A forged painting that looks right until somebody tests the
paint. A glass of gas, where the thermometer gives one reading and underneath there are
endlessly many arrangements of molecules that would all give it. Plato's prisoners. Every
one looked promising for about a minute. Then I noticed they all fail the same way: look
harder and the hidden thing shows up. Lift the cup. Cut the trunk and count the rings.
Test the paint. Turn around in the cave. The hidden part is a fact about the object that
better eyes could find. That isn't what the two computers have. You can see every lamp in
the warehouse and not find the check.

That gave me a crude test. Give someone perfect vision of the thing in front of them, as
much detail as they want. Could they read the difference off it? If yes, it's just
hidden. If no, it might be what I'm after. The test is a judgment, and I'm the one making
it, so it's a way of sorting my own examples, not a verdict.

The second batch passed that test and failed differently. A clock face reads three
o'clock and doesn't show how many days have gone by. And a computer can keep a complete
history of what it did: that's Bennett's machine from chapter two, which writes down every
step, copies out its answer, then runs itself backwards, wiping the history as it goes,
until it holds only the question and the answer, with no two arrows ever meeting. The
history it kept along the way was right out in the open. But it could be run back down.
If the thing I was after could be put up and taken down with nothing paid either way, it
would just be bookkeeping. So I needed something that only moves one way, and costs
something to move.

The third batch came closer. In 1782 James Six, a Canterbury man from a family of silk
weavers who had taken up watching the weather, described a thermometer for the Royal
Society. He wanted the coldest moment of every night, and he'd found that with an ordinary
thermometer you had to be looking when it happened, because, in his words, "if it be not
immediately noticed, it can never after be known." His fix was two little steel markers in
the tube that the column pushes along and that stay where they're left. A record that only
moves one way. Then he spoils it, for my purposes, in the same paper: every evening he
wrote the numbers down and slid the markers back with a magnet, "without a touch."

Since 1996, starting with polio vaccine, vials bought through UNICEF have carried a little
sticker, a square inside a circle. The square darkens as the vial takes on heat, and it
doesn't lighten again when the vial cools. When the square matches the circle, don't use
the vaccine. It only moves one way, and it can't be reset. But it's chemistry: the
reaction in the square is chosen to run about as fast as the vaccine itself spoils. Nobody
ran a check. The heat did.

Closer still: the international vocabulary that measurement labs work from says a result
is traceable only if it can be related to a reference "through a documented unbroken chain
of calibrations." Standing earned by checks, step by step. The same document adds that
traceability "does not ensure ... that there is an absence of mistakes," which is
"settled isn't the same as true," written into an international standard. And the top of
the chain wasn't fixed either. When the official copies of the kilogram were compared with
the original in 1889, 1946 and around 1991, they had drifted apart by tens of micrograms
over the century, and nobody can say which one moved, because the original was the
kilogram. In 2019 the kilogram was redefined by fixing a constant of nature instead.

The rules of chess have the shape too. The laws of chess say two positions are the same
only if the same player has the move, the same pieces are on the same squares, and the
possible moves of all the pieces are the same. They spell out that a king that could still
castle and one that can't are in different positions, even on an identical board. That
passes the perfect-vision test. And the rules say why: the castling right changes which
moves are legal. The hidden part steers the game.

Each of those got something right and missed something else.


How a statement gets established

The one that fit best, among the ones I found, was the standing of a mathematical
statement. Here's how it went for one statement, as far as I could follow it.

In 1611 Johannes Kepler, the emperor's mathematician in Prague, owed his friend and patron
Johann Matthäus Wackher von Wackenfels a New Year's present and, he said, had nothing to
give. Wackher, Kepler wrote, loved nothing, for the games you can play with the word, so
Kepler wrote him a little pamphlet about the nearest thing to nothing he could find, a
snowflake, which had landed on his coat as he crossed a bridge. Why six corners? Kepler
couldn't say in the end. But along the way he stacked equal balls in his head and said
that one way of stacking them, the way grocers stack oranges, packs them so tightly that
no other arrangement could fit more into the same box. He didn't prove it. He said it.

Then the sentence sat. In 1831 Carl Friedrich Gauss proved a special case. In 1900 David
Hilbert put the general question on his famous list of problems for the new century,
without mentioning Kepler. In 1953 the Hungarian geometer László Fejes Tóth showed how the
whole thing might be cut down to a problem with finitely many numbers in it, and guessed
that computers might one day finish it. Thomas Hales started on it in earnest in 1994,
and in August 1998 he and his student Samuel Ferguson announced a proof that leaned on a
great deal of computer work. The journal gave it to a panel of twelve referees. They ran
seminars on it for years. A journalist, George Szpiro, later reported the editor's letter
as Hales described it: the referees had "not been able to certify the correctness of the
proof, and will not be able to certify it in the future, because they have run out of
energy." The journal printed the human part in 2005 and the whole proof in 2006. Hales
started a project in 2003 to check every single step by machine. It finished on 10 August
2014.

Lay those dates out on the table and look at what moved. The sentence never changed.
Whether it's true never changed, as far as anyone can tell. What changed was how far it had
been established, and it changed in stages: a guess, a special case, a place on a list, a
plan, an announcement, a panel that ran out of energy, a printing, a machine check.

Then I tried to break that one too, because by now I'd watched a lot of good examples fall
over, and there's an obvious place to push. Standing can fall.

In 1852 a student named Francis Guthrie, colouring a map of the counties of England,
noticed that four colours always seemed to be enough to keep neighbouring counties
different. The question went to his old teacher Augustus De Morgan, who wrote to the Irish
mathematician William Rowan Hamilton that a student had asked him for "a reason for a fact
which I did not know was a fact," and that he didn't know it yet either. In 1879 Alfred
Kempe, a lawyer who did mathematics on the side and sang counter-tenor, published a proof,
and his description of finding it is wonderful: "After a somewhat arduous search, I have
succeeded, suddenly, as might be expected, in hitting upon the weak point." The journal's
associate editor, William Story, added a note saying Kempe had "substantially proved" it.
Eleven years later Percy Heawood, a lecturer at Durham who by one account brought his dog
to his lectures, showed that one step of "the now apparently recognised proof" doesn't
work.

So standing can fall. And then the part I didn't expect. The four-colour statement was
true all along. Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved it in 1976 with a computer, and the
proof was met with suspicion for years; a cleaner version came in the 1990s; and in 2005
Georges Gonthier finished a proof checked step by step by a small proof-checking program.
So one statement stayed true the entire time while its standing went up in 1879, came down
in 1890, went up again in 1976 against a lot of doubt, and went up further in 2005. The
fall wasn't a fall of the truth. It was a fall of how far the truth had been established,
and it was the right fall, because what had been established was less than anyone
thought.

Laying all of that out together is what changed my picture. If there are stages, then at
each one there's a line, with two sides: not this far yet, and at least this far. Each line
is its own yes-or-no question. But pick the question carefully. "Do I still trust this
proof?" can turn back to no, the way Kempe's did. "Did I carry out this particular check?"
is about something that happened. Cross out the trust and the check still happened. To keep
that answer, the machine has to keep a record of it and have no move that knocks the record
down.

So maybe the switch wasn't wrong. Maybe it was just the first line, and the real shape is
a direction with lines drawn across it, which is the row of cups from chapter five. I've
gone around many times on whether the lines make the direction or the direction comes
first and the lines are just where you cut it. I don't have an answer. The part I could
actually settle is the part chapter five did: one switch isn't enough once there are three
or more stages.


What settling looks like

Here's the picture, put together.

Draw every state the machine could be in as a field of dots. A yes-or-no question about
the state splits the field in two: yes and no. That's a sorting, the smallest one there is.
If one side is empty, there's no sorting at all, just a field, which is the empty no pile
from the last chapter. Now the extra thing that makes it settling: once the machine is on the
yes side, no step it can take leads back out. The door only swings in. A settled question is
a sorting plus a door that only swings one way.

At its best, what goes through the door is a relation: these numbers are in this order,
these two things belong together. But the door doesn't care. Hook it to a check that says
yes to everything and the machine walks through even when the claim is false. The question is
settled either way, true or false, like Peirce said. A door that only swings one way tells you
a question was closed. It doesn't tell you it was closed right. Price and correctness are two
different books, and I keep both, separately.

And one more edge, the one Gray's wedding already hinted at. What stays shut for good is that
the question got closed, about that version of that thing. Whether the claim is still true is
another matter. Change the list after the commit and the record still says, truthfully, that
the old version was checked and closed. If the claim turns out bad, the fix is another act on
top, a second record that says "withdrawn." The first one never quietly comes back down. So
"I can lean on it right now" is three things at once: closed, not withdrawn since, and the
thing unchanged.
