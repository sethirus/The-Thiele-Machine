What structure is

The chapters so far were about one thing settling: a coin going into YES, a cup
turning up, a box getting ticked. This chapter goes after the bigger word behind
all of it, and I'll give it to you now, so you can decide whether to stay:
structure.

I used that word for most of my life without once looking at it, which I suspect
is true of most people and most words, and it's fine, right up until you do
something stupid. The stupid thing I did was build a machine that charges for keeping something.
You can arrange things however you like, and the machine lets you put any price on
the arranging, including none. But the moment an arrangement has to stay, the
moment you ask the machine to keep it, or to vouch for it, to put its own name on
the claim that these things belong together, there's a charge, and it isn't zero.
Chapters two and three showed why. What was in front of me, standing there with the
thing built, was a model that charges for keeping structure, and I could not have
told you what structure was. It didn't care. It can't nod along the way a person
does. It needed the word to hold still, and the word had never held still for
anyone, least of all me.

So I went looking at the people who'd wrestled it before me. Some of them have
been dead two thousand years and are still, I'd bet money, better at this than I
am. I used everything I had: language models, more than one, books, libraries, my
own gut, anything in reach. I don't know if I found enough. I never do. There's
always another shelf behind the one I was reading. And being dead and famous is
not the same as being right, so I pushed on each of them, and what follows is what
I could reach, pushed on as hard as I know how. The one thing I'm asking is that
you push on it with me, out loud, at your own kitchen table, because the first
time through I was the only one checking, and that's not enough people.

The order I ran into them was a mess. Category theory came first, because I'd
built one by feel before I knew it had a name, and everything else came in
whatever order the next question dragged it in. I'm going to put them in the order
where each one leans on the last, which is mostly the order they happened. And
none of it is an oracle. History is where I start digging. It gets retold,
smoothed over, and now and then made up outright (there's a drowning coming up
that nobody can place), so every piece of it is a lead to check, and I check the
parts I lean on.

A string, a square, and a drowning nobody can place

Start with the oldest one, and start by doing it. Take a string. A guitar string,
a rubber band over an open box, anything that twangs. Pluck it. Keep its tension
as nearly unchanged as you can when you stop it at a new point; stretching it
tighter changes the pitch for a second reason. Now stop it exactly at the middle
and pluck half. With the tension held steady, that's the same note an octave up,
the one that sounds like the same note sung higher. Press at a third of the way
along and pluck the long side: that's the fifth, one of the handful of intervals a
bugle can play at all, which is why you hear it all through bugle calls. Half, two
thirds: small whole numbers, and your ear says yes before your brain does. About
twenty-five hundred years ago, in the south of Italy, the followers of a man named
Pythagoras are credited with finding exactly that, and it's as true in your
kitchen tonight as it was on their hillside.

I'll tell you what they did with it, as best anyone can tell. Nothing survives
that's surely from Pythagoras's own hand. The earliest fragments are from a later
member, Philolaus, and scholars argue over those. Mostly what's left is Aristotle,
one to two centuries later, writing in his Metaphysics about them the way you'd
write about the neighbours' religion, and he says they took the elements of
numbers to be the elements of all things: everything is number. That doesn't mean
everything can be counted; it means the parts of numbers are the parts of all
things.

Stop and ask what I asked. What does that even mean? Is a chair a number? Does a
chair have a number tucked inside it somewhere? Aristotle doesn't say, and he's
reporting a belief he doesn't hold. So don't take it on his word, or theirs, or
mine. But before you push on it, know what it was holding up, because you can't
test a load-bearing wall without knowing what sits on it. These weren't people
doing math for fun on weekends. They were a brotherhood, with rules about what you
could eat and when you had to keep quiet, and that plucked string was their
scripture. Halve the string, hear the octave: to them that was proof that under
the mess of the world there were whole numbers in clean ratios, that the cosmos
was a chord and they'd heard it. "Everything is number" wasn't a slogan; it was
what they'd organized their lives around. And that's exactly the kind of sentence
you should be suspicious of, mine included.

Now push, and push where they loved most: the square. Draw one, with a side one
unit long, any unit you like, and draw the line from one corner to the opposite
corner. Call that the diagonal. If everything is number, then the diagonal is some
whole number of parts long when the side is some whole number of parts, so the
diagonal divided by the side is a fraction, a whole number over a whole number.
Cancel anything the top and bottom share, the way four-sixths becomes two-thirds,
until they have no common factor left. I'll call the top $p$ and the bottom $q$,
and the letters are purely so I don't have to keep writing "the top" and "the
bottom."

You need one fact about squares, and it's the one Pythagoras is famous for. The
square built on the diagonal has exactly twice the area of the square built on the
side. If you want to see it for yourself, cut out two identical squares, cut the
second one along both diagonals into four triangles, and lay one triangle against
each side of the first, its long edge on that side. The outline is the square on
the first one's diagonal, and it's made of exactly two of the small squares; it's
a five-minute job with scissors. So $p$ times $p$ is twice $q$ times $q$. People
write "times itself" as a small raised two, so that's $p^2 = 2q^2$, and that is
the only piece of notation in this argument.

Twice anything is even. So $p$ times $p$ is even. An odd number times itself is
always odd: draw that many rows of that many dots, pair off the columns, then pair
the dots in the one column left over, and one dot remains. The same pairing works
for any odd side, so if $p$ were odd, $p$ times $p$ would be odd too. It isn't, so
$p$ itself must be even: it's two times something, and I'll call that something
$r$. Put it back in. Two $r$ times two $r$ is four times $r$ times $r$, and that
equals twice $q$ times $q$. Halve both sides: two times $r$ times $r$ equals $q$
times $q$. So $q$ times $q$ is even, so $q$ is even too, by the same step as
before. But you already cancelled everything the top and bottom had in common, so
they can't both be even. The argument has eaten itself, and when an argument eats
itself the thing you supposed at the start is false. There is no such fraction.
The diagonal of a square can't be named by any ratio of whole numbers, ever.
Aristotle reaches for this very argument in his Prior Analytics as his example of
proof by contradiction (suppose the diagonal can be measured, and odd numbers come
out equal to even ones), and you just did it with a pencil.

There's a story that a member of the brotherhood named Hippasus let this secret
out, and his brothers took him to sea and drowned him for it. I went and checked,
because it's a good story and good stories are the ones to check. The oldest
drownings I could find are in Iamblichus, writing around AD 300, seven or eight
hundred years after Hippasus. In Iamblichus the man drowned by name is Hippasus,
and what he gave away was how to fit twelve pentagons together into a ball, the
dodecahedron. A few pages on, Iamblichus tells it again about whoever first let
out the incommensurable, and that time he names nobody. Carl Huffman, who wrote
the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on Pythagoreanism, follows the
historian Walter Burkert there and finds no ancient writer who connects Hippasus
to this discovery at all. So the version with Hippasus drowned for the diagonal
looks like two old stories stitched together later. It's a legend, but notice that
the legend exists. Somebody, long after, felt that a man should have been drowned
for this. That's how much was riding on it. To whoever stitched the story
together, the diagonal wasn't a math problem; it was the end of the world.

So the claim is dead, and you killed it. I only handed you the pencil. Now the
question that matters: what's left? Look at your square again. The diagonal is
still there. It still stands in a perfectly definite relation to the side; you
drew it, and nobody drowned. That relation was real before anyone tried to express
it as a fraction, and it stayed real after that attempt failed. The relation came
first. The number, when there is one, is a name for the relation, and sometimes
there isn't a name. I'm going to keep that, and I want you to keep it too, because
you're going to watch the same move get made again and again as this book goes on:
somebody says "everything is X," and it's true enough to do real work with, and it
sounds right when you pluck it, and then somebody draws the diagonal. Whether the
thing I built survives its own diagonal is a real question, and a later chapter
answers it.

Shapes poured into nothing

About a century and a half after the brotherhood, Plato, in a dialogue called the
Timaeus, tried to build the whole physical world out of geometry. Fire, air, water
and earth were four regular solids (the pyramid, the eight-faced one, the twenty-
faced one, and the cube), and the faces of those solids were built from exactly
two kinds of right triangle. You can make one of each in a minute: cut a square
from corner to corner for the first, and cut an equilateral triangle from a corner
to the middle of the opposite side for the second. Everything else, in Plato's
story, was a receptacle, a featureless medium that takes on shape the way wax
takes a seal. Structure, for Plato, is shape laid into something that has no shape
of its own.

Why did he care? He wanted a world made on purpose, by a craftsman working from a
plan, and triangles were the plan. Is it true? No, but not for the cheap reason.
You can't refute a claim about what things are made of at the smallest scale by
picking one up; the smallest scale is exactly where your hands don't reach, and
Plato knew that. What sank it is that it made no prediction anyone could check,
and the people who eventually did reach that scale, two thousand years on, found
atoms, and atoms aren't triangles. The idea was a guess with no way to lose, until
it lost. But push on it and two things survive. One: structure is something laid
in, and the stuff it's laid into doesn't carry it by itself. That's a real idea,
and I'd ask you to keep it loosely for now; when you meet a record that's
established, you can ask whether the state it holds is the wax or the seal. Two:
Plato put a crack in his own forms himself, in another dialogue, the Parmenides,
with an objection that Aristotle later nicknamed the third man, and it's the most
useful mistake in this section. Take all the large things. Say they share one
thing, Largeness. But Largeness is itself large, so now there's a further thing
that all the large things and Largeness share, and that one is large too, and it
never stops. The extra move is treating Largeness itself as one more large thing.
Grant that again at each stage and it never stops; refuse it and this particular
regress never starts. The lesson, and I think it's right though Plato never spells
it out: the structure of a collection is not one more member of the collection.
The plan isn't a brick. Hold on to that one.

Prove it from what you and I both accept

Around 300 BC, in Alexandria, Euclid wrote the Elements, and the thing to take
from it is not the geometry. It's the method, and the method is the whole reason I
trust anything in this book, so give me a paragraph on it. He starts with
definitions ("a point is that which has no part"), five postulates (things he asks
you to grant, like "to draw a straight line from any point to any point"), and
five common notions (things nobody argues with, like "things which are equal to
the same thing are also equal to one another"). Then he derives everything else,
four hundred and sixty-five propositions, from only those, in order, each meant to
use nothing but what came before (he sometimes leans on things he never stated,
and later geometers filled those in). He never says "trust me" or "it's well
known"; he says, in effect, here are the rules you and I both accept, now watch.
If you've ever been in an argument that ended with "well, that's just your
opinion," Euclid is the man who found a way out of that room, and I'm not
embarrassed to say I learned the way out from someone dead for two thousand three
hundred years. Four hundred and sixty-five times he does the same trick, and the
trick is everything.

And then there's the one rule Euclid seems almost embarrassed by, and it's a hole
worth falling into. His fifth postulate, the one about parallel lines, is long and
awkward next to the others, and for two thousand years people tried to prove it
from the rest so it wouldn't have to be assumed. Proclus, the fifth-century
philosopher who wrote a commentary on Euclid's first book, tried. Mathematicians
in Persia and the Arab world tried. In 1733 an Italian priest named Girolamo
Saccheri spent a whole book assuming it was false so he could watch the
contradiction arrive, and he got pages deep into what is now a perfectly good
geometry before declaring that it was repugnant and stopping. A century later
three people, separately, took the step he wouldn't: drop the postulate, and what
you get is other geometries that hold up just as well, and nonsense never arrives.
Farkas Bolyai begged his son János to stay away from the problem, warning that it
had swallowed years of his own life; the son went in anyway and came out with one
of those geometries. The part I keep is that they found something real by refusing
to believe one of the rules everyone accepted, and checking what happened without
it. Refusing a rule and checking what happens without it is the push, and this one
went on for two thousand years.

What is structure, to Euclid? Whatever the starting rules force. And when are two
shapes the same? You can do this one yourself: cut out two paper triangles and lay
one on the other. If they coincide, they're equal. That's his fourth common
notion, and it's how he proves his fourth proposition, by picking up one triangle
and setting it down on another. Sameness is coinciding under a move. Keep that;
it's about to get very general, and I didn't see that coming either.

The statue and the bronze

Aristotle, Plato's student, pulled the forms down out of heaven and put them
inside things, which was a bit rude to his teacher and also, I think, correct. His
example, from the Metaphysics: a bronze statue is bronze plus a shape. And a
circle can be made in bronze or stone or wood, so, he says, the bronze is no part
of what it is to be a circle. The same shape in marble makes a different statue
with the same form. The bronze is the matter and the shape is the form, and the
form is what the thing is.

Push on it with two things from the kitchen. A cup can be ceramic or metal and
still hold your coffee. A sandwich carved in wood can keep the outline and fail to
be lunch. The two swaps preserve different things. If the question is shape, the
carving may pass; if the question is whether you can eat it, it does not.

Aristotle already makes a distinction of this kind. In Book VII, chapter 11, he
warns against treating a living animal just like the circle whose bronze can be
changed: its parts must be in a condition to do their work. The sandwich is a way
to make that difficulty visible, not a correction he never considered.

That is the bet I need for the record. Change the material, then check the
operations and the answers again. If they still agree, the record has survived the
change in the respects I promised. A later chapter states those promises over a
chosen base. Neither theorem says that every material can do every job, or that
the price in one material is the price in another.

Where things are, without numbers

In 1679 Gottfried Leibniz, who co-invented calculus and had opinions about
everything, wrote to the physicist Christiaan Huygens asking for something that
didn't exist: a way to reason about where things are directly, the way algebra
reasons about how much, without ever laying down coordinates. He called it the
analysis of situation. Try it yourself. Describe where the furniture is in the
room you're sitting in without using a single number. "The couch is against the
wall. The table is between the couch and the window. The lamp is next to the
chair." You just did it, and what you used was relations between places and
nothing else. Huygens wrote back that November and was not impressed. It took
roughly two hundred years for Leibniz's idea to become a field, and the field is
called topology, and Huygens is remembered for other things.

What survives the push is the same thing that survived the square, coming at you
from the other side. Position is a relation among places. The numbers you lay on
top are a convenience, and they're a convenience you can take away and still have
the room. I'll be honest about why this one landed for me: the thing I built keeps
track of which things belong together, and I spent a long time thinking that was a
list of numbers before I understood it was a relation that the numbers were merely
naming. Leibniz had that in 1679. Huygens told him no. I'd like to think I'd have
sided with Leibniz. I probably would have sided with the famous guy.

Naming a thing by saying when two are the same

In 1884, in a short book called The Foundations of Arithmetic, Gottlob Frege
noticed something you can check in your head right now. "Line $a$ is parallel to
line $b$" and "the direction of $a$ is the direction of $b$" say exactly the same
thing. There is no fact in one that isn't in the other. But the second sentence
has a new object in it, a direction, that the first one doesn't. Where did it come
from? From the sameness. Once you can say when two things count as the same,
you've earned the right to name the thing they share. Frege used exactly this move
to say what a number is: the number of things in a pile is whatever every pile you
can match up with it, one to one, has in common. The residue has teeth: a sameness
relation lets you name a new object.

Now push, because Frege pushed too hard, and it cost him. In his formal system,
Basic Law V treated value-ranges as objects and identified them exactly when the
corresponding functions agreed everywhere. Together with his unrestricted
comprehension principles, that let the self-membership construction in. The
familiar set version is easier to tell: In June of 1902, a young British
philosopher and logician, Bertrand Russell, wrote him a short letter. Consider the
collection of all collections that are not members of themselves. Is it a member
of itself? If it is, then by its own rule it isn't. If it isn't, then it
qualifies, so it is. Do it in your head; it flips every time you look at it, like
one of those drawings that's a vase and then two faces and then a vase. Frege's
floor had a hole in it, and he knew it the day he opened the letter, and his
second volume was already at the printer. One repair was Zermelo's 1908 axiom of
separation: a property selects a subset of a set already given. His system also
had other existence axioms, including power set and infinity; separation was not
its only way to obtain a set. What it removed was unrestricted sorting of
"everything." You sort a pile. I'll tell you now that this rule shows up again,
later, in a place where I had to obey it or fall through the same hole, and I'll
point at it when the book gets there.

Frege's reply is worth hearing, because it's the best example I know of how to
lose. He wrote back within days. He didn't hide it or argue around it. He said,
more or less, that arithmetic was tottering, and in an appendix to the volume that
was about to come out he wrote that hardly anything worse can happen to a
scientific writer than having a foundation give way right after the work is
finished. Then he tried to fix it. If I'm wrong about something in this book, and
I'd bet I am somewhere, that's how I'd like to take it.

Choose the changes, keep what survives

By 1872 there were too many geometries. Euclid's, and ones where parallel lines
meet, and ones with no such thing as distance, and nobody could say what made them
all geometry and kept them from being a pile of unrelated hobbies. Felix Klein,
twenty-three years old and taking up a professorship at Erlangen, published an
answer in a program paper that people still call the Erlangen program. Pick a set
of allowed changes: rotations, stretches, whatever you decide. Then a geometry is
the study of the properties that stay the same under those changes. Pick a
different set of changes and you get a different geometry on the very same page.
Euclid's, in Klein's reading, is the one where you may slide, turn and flip a
shape and scale it evenly, and stretching it more one way than another is off
limits. And I'd underline, if this were my book, which it is, that in Klein's
scheme any group of changes can define a geometry. The choice is yours. Nobody
tells you which changes to allow.

You can do this one at your desk. Take any drawing and ask what's still true after
you turn it upside down (lengths, angles), after you stretch it (lengths are gone
by then; which lines cross which is still there), after you bend and crumple the
sheet without tearing it (which drawn paths remain connected survives). Two
distant ink marks may touch across a fold; that new physical contact is not a new
connection in the drawing. You have to say which relation you are following. The
three changes give three answers and three structures, all from one drawing. The
residue is that structure is what survives a chosen set of changes, and "chosen"
is doing real work in that sentence. There's no structure of a thing from nowhere.
You have to say what you're allowed to do to it first. I didn't know that when I
started, and I'd been saying "the structure of" things for years.

Klein's move has a descendant in logic, and it goes all the way. In a lecture in
London in 1966, published only in 1986, three years after his death, the Polish-
American logician Alfred Tarski asked what makes a notion logical, and answered
with Klein's recipe at its far end: allow every one-to-one transformation of the
world onto itself, and call logical the notions that survive all of them. The more
changes you allow, the fewer notions are left standing, and with every change
allowed what's left is logic. Tarski's answer is the sentence above, "structure is
what survives a chosen set of changes," with the choice made as large as it can
be.

An invariance comes with a conserved quantity

In 1918 Emmy Noether, who at the time wasn't allowed to qualify for a university
teaching post because she was a woman, and for a while taught courses advertised
under David Hilbert's name, proved something that physicists still treat as close
to holy. Take a physical system whose laws can be written the way most of physics
is written, as "of all the paths the system could take, it takes one that makes a
certain total stationary" (the total is called the action, and stationary means a
small change to the path doesn't change the total to first order; the path needn't
make it smallest, and that's all I'll say about it here). For such a system, every
continuous symmetry that leaves the action unchanged comes with a quantity that is
conserved. If the action doesn't change when you shift everything in space,
momentum is conserved. If it doesn't change when you shift everything in time,
energy is. A symmetry isn't decoration. It has a consequence downstream that you
can measure with instruments. I can't hand you this one to do at the table; the
proof needs calculus I'd have to build first and I'd lose you and probably me, and
the "written as an action" condition is doing real work that I'm not going to
unpack here. What I can hand you is the shape. Structure, once it's really there
and the setting is the right kind, has consequences you can measure. That's the
sentence that made me think a machine could carry a cost for structure in the
first place. Noether makes it reasonable to ask. She doesn't make it true, and
nothing I build in this book is a system with an action.

All of mathematics as structures

Starting in the 1930s, a group of young French mathematicians began publishing a
vast treatise under one made-up name, Nicolas Bourbaki, as if he were a single
person. He wasn't. In 1948 he also applied for membership in the American
Mathematical Society, and the application was quietly turned down, which I find
comforting. (The historian Michael Barany has gone through that application, a
second one a year later, and the letters they set off.) Their goal was to rebuild
all of mathematics on one idea: a structure is a set of elements "whose nature has
not been specified," together with one or more relations on them that satisfy
stated axioms. They believed three kinds of structure were the mothers of all the
rest: algebraic, order, and topological.

Push on it. The historian Leo Corry went through the entire treatise looking for
the place where this formal definition of structure actually does any work, and
what he found is that it "plays no actual role in the presentation of theories
within the treatise." Outside the chapter that defines it, it does work almost
nowhere. And I found no argument in their writing for why the mother structures
number three. And the chapter on category theory, which they promised their
readers, never appeared; Corry is where I found that too, and Ralf Krömer's 2006
history of Bourbaki and categories in the 1950s follows the attempt. That does not
mean Bourbaki left maps out. Their Theory of Sets has sections on morphisms,
derived structures and universal mappings (Chapter IV, sections 2 and 3). Corry's
criticism concerns how much their general formal apparatus was actually used. The
lesson I can take for my own work is smaller: naming a structure is not enough;
show which constructions and comparisons it supports. I learned that one the slow
way, and the afterword tells it.

Philosophers of mathematics took the same idea further and gave it a name,
structuralism. In 1965 the philosopher Paul Benacerraf pointed out that the whole
numbers can be built out of sets in two equally good ways that disagree about
which set the number three is, and concluded that numbers aren't objects at all:
what arithmetic is about is the pattern, and anything standing in the right
relations to everything else can play the part of three. The philosophers Michael
Resnik (Mathematics as a Science of Patterns) and Stewart Shapiro (Philosophy of
Mathematics: Structure and Ontology), both in 1997, made the patterns themselves
what mathematics is about. The philosopher Geoffrey Hellman (Mathematics without
Numbers, 1989) kept the talk of patterns and did without the patterns, reading
each statement as a claim about what would hold in any system with the right
structure, if there were one. The logician Steve Awodey (1996) argued that
category theory says it best, a structure being what its maps keep, which is where
this section goes next. And the philosopher of science James Ladyman (1998)
carried the idea into physics: what a successful theory gets right about the world
is its structure. This section's thesis, that structure is the relations and the
things in them don't matter, is theirs. None of the formal results takes their
arguments as a premise, and the bibliography lists them under the same heading as
Bourbaki.

A set with some named relations

What mathematical logic eventually settled on, with the logician Alfred Tarski
giving the field its name, "the theory of models," in 1954, is almost boring, and
the boring ones are the ones that last. A structure is a pile of things plus
whatever relations a chosen vocabulary names on it. Pick the vocabulary (Klein
again: you choose), and a structure is anything that gives those names a meaning.

Now I want to say "the smallest one," and "smallest" means nothing until I say what
I'm measuring. So here's my ruler: count the names first; when that ties, count how
many things each name looks at at once; when that ties too, count how many answers
each name can give. The bottom of that ruler, for anything that sorts at all, is one
name, looking at one thing at a time, with two possible answers: one yes-or-no
question, put to each thing in the pile.

A relation like "these two go together" can sort a pile too, but it looks at two
things at once. A label for each thing can sort a pile, but it can give more than
two answers. So the ruler puts both above one question. Two limits, so you don't
have to find them. A single named thing, "this one, here," sits even lower on the
ruler, but by itself it doesn't sort anything; ask "is it this one?" and you've got
a split in two again, one question whose yes is a single thing. And a question only
sorts when some things say yes and some say no. If they all agree, there's one
group and nothing got sorted.

With those said: by this ruler, the smallest structure that sorts is one question.
A different ruler could say something different. This is the one I chose, and I'm
telling you so.

And notice one more thing about it. A yes-or-no question carries one bit more than a
plain split into two groups: which group gets called yes. That bit is a direction
across the split, a door that only swings one way. Keep it. It's about to turn into
the whole chapter.

The structure is in the arrows

I met this one first, and backwards. I'd built it by feel, in a rendering engine,
as "digital DNA," and only went looking for its name after the thing already ran.
In 1945 two mathematicians, Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane, published a
paper that founded a field now called category theory, and they said in plain
words that it was a continuation of Klein, which I'd have loved to know at the
time. The move they made was to stop asking what a thing is, and ask how it's
carried. A category is some objects and some arrows between them, where arrows can
be followed one after another and every object has a do-nothing arrow to itself,
plus two rules that make the following dependable: following three arrows in a row
gives the same result whether you pair up the first two or the last two, and the
do-nothing arrow really does nothing when you tack it onto either end, and that's
the whole definition. On the page the two rules look like nothing, and they're the
part people skip, so slow down on them for a second, because each one is there for
a reason you can see.

Picture three dots in a row, A, B and C, an arrow $f$ hopping from the first to
the second and an arrow $g$ from the second to the third. Composition says there's
an arrow taking the whole stride from A to C in one, $g$ after $f$. Without it,
every multi-hop path has to be named on its own, and the structure falls apart
into a pile of unconnected pairs, a phone book without a search function.
Associativity says that with a third arrow $h$ on the end, pairing up the first
two and then adding $h$ gives the same arrow as adding $h$ to the last two first.
Both spell the same three-hop path. With your paper triangle, bracket "slide, then
turn" before "flip," or bracket "turn, then flip" after "slide": you still carry
out the same three moves in the same order. Swapping the turn and the flip would
be a different question. If the two bracketings came out different, two people who
built the same chain in different orders would hold two different arrows, which is
a bug, because the chain is the thing and how you assembled it isn't. The identity
laws say that tacking the do-nothing loop onto either end of an arrow gives back
the arrow. If the loop changed things, it wouldn't be the loop. Those six terms,
objects, arrows, composition, identities, associativity and identity laws, are all
of Eilenberg and Mac Lane's definition.

Composing relations is older than the definition, too. Who put a name on that move
first? I went looking. Augustus De Morgan, writing on the logic of relations
around 1860, took the "is" out of the old syllogism, put a general relation in its
place, and composed two relations to get the conclusion. The example that gets
quoted from that tradition, Peirce's a decade later, is "$x$ is the lover of a
servant of $y$," which is relational composition with better gossip. When exactly
De Morgan read that paper to the Cambridge Philosophical Society, and on which
pages it was printed, the sources disagree, which is a small reminder that history
is where you start digging. Then in 1870 Charles Sanders Peirce, an American
scientist and philosopher you met in chapter nine, carried George
Boole's algebra of logic (the arithmetic of true and false, and Boole was a
largely self-taught schoolmaster) over to relations, with composition as one of
its basic operations, and he's usually counted as the real founder of the theory.
So following one arrow after another is older than computers, older than
categories, older than Turing's machine: it is a nineteenth-century logician's
product of relations.

And where did they get the word? They didn't get it from mathematics. They
borrowed category from the philosophers, from Aristotle and Kant, and functor (a
map from one whole category to another) from the philosopher Rudolf Carnap, and
then gave both words exact meanings none of those philosophers had in mind. So
Aristotle walks back into this chapter without being asked: the man who put the
form inside the bronze lent his word to the field that keeps track of forms by
their arrows. That doesn't change the laws. It's a reminder that a word can start
in philosophy and arrive in mathematics with a precise meaning bolted on, which is
the thing I've been trying to do with established since the start of the book.

What a thing is is how it maps. For now the residue is the emphasis I needed:
structure lives in the maps. Nothing later in this book needs more category theory
than that sentence, and the chapter on a box over any machine, where a record is pinned down
by the maps between a machine and the base it extends, is where the sentence does
its work.

Structure is the part that compresses

Play this one with me. I write "th" and you guess the next letter. You said "e,"
and you were right. Now "the q": you said "u." Congratulations, you just measured
the structure of English. In 1948 Claude Shannon measured it properly, in a paper
that founded information theory, and found that about half of written English is
"determined by the structure of the language." It's the reason a zip file of a
long letter is so much smaller than the letter. Be careful what that says. It does
not say that every other letter is fixed by the ones before it; you just guessed
"e" after "th" and you could have been wrong (think "than"). It says that, on
average and over long stretches, about half of what's written could be rebuilt
from the rest, so that the text is roughly twice as long as the choices in it. And
he said why that matters in one line that I'd frame if I framed things: the
structure "allows one to make a saving in time" (he added "or channel capacity").
The more you can predict, the less you have to read.

Thirty years later Jorma Rissanen, a Finnish information theorist at IBM's
research lab in San Jose, turned that saving into a price. His idea is called the
minimum description length principle, and it says that the best model of some data
is the one that makes the total description shortest, where the total is the
length of the model plus the length of the data once the model has explained what
it can. A model is paid for in bits, and it's worth stating only if it saves more
than it costs. Peter Grünwald, a statistician who leads a machine learning group
at CWI, the Dutch national institute for mathematics and computer science in
Amsterdam, in a tutorial you can find online, adds the footnote that keeps this
honest: the price of a model depends on the code you chose to write it in, so the
price itself needs a principle behind it, and that principle is something you have
to argue for, not something you're handed.

That's a lot of "length of" to swallow in one paragraph, so do one with me, with
numbers small enough to check on the back of an envelope. Every number in it comes
from a code I'm about to make up on the spot, and I'll say every rule of the code
out loud, because Grünwald's footnote is the whole point: change the rules and
every number moves.

The data is a row of thirty-two digits, each a 0 or a 1, and you and I agree in
advance that the row is thirty-two long, so nobody has to spend anything saying
so:

  [row: 0101010101010101 0101010101010101]

(The space is only there to rest your eyes.) Every description in the code starts
with one digit that says which kind of description follows: 0 means "here's the
row, written out," 1 means "here's a pattern, then the exceptions to it." Each
digit is one yes-or-no answer, one bit, which is what Shannon's unit is. Written
out, the row costs that first digit plus the thirty-two digits themselves, thirty-
three bits, and there's no model in it at all. A pattern description goes like
this. First come five digits giving the block length minus one: 00000 means one
digit, and 11111 means thirty-two. That covers every allowed length. Then comes
the block itself. The rule is "repeat the block, then stop at exactly thirty-two
digits, cutting off the last repetition if necessary." Then come five digits for
how many places the row disagrees with the pattern (up to thirty-one, which is
plenty here), and five more digits for each of those places, numbered zero through
thirty-one and listed in increasing order without repeats. At every listed place,
flip the predicted digit.

Do it for the row above. The block is 01, two digits long. So the model costs 1
(which kind) plus 5 (the block's length) plus 2 (the block), which makes eight
bits. Then comes what's left to say about the data once the model has said its
piece: the row disagrees with "repeat 01" nowhere, so that's five digits saying
"zero exceptions" and nothing else, five bits. The total is thirteen, against
thirty-three for writing it out. The pattern pays for itself, with twenty bits to
spare, and Rissanen's sentence has just happened on paper: a model is worth
stating when it saves more than it costs.

Now spoil the row. Flip two of its digits anywhere you like. The model doesn't
change, eight bits, but now the leftover says "two exceptions, here and here":
five plus five plus five, fifteen, for a total of twenty-three. It's still cheaper
than thirty-three; the pattern still pays, it just pays less, because the data has
more to say that the model can't. Flip four, and the total is $8 + 5 + 4 \times 5
= 33$, a dead tie, and a pattern that saves nothing isn't worth stating. Flip five
and it's thirty-eight, and now writing the row out is cheaper than explaining it,
and this particular pattern has lost to writing the row out. Another pattern might
still win. Flip places 5, 11, 17, 23 and 29 of the original row: now the block
010100, repeated and cut off at thirty-two digits, fits exactly. Its description
costs $1+5+6+5=17$ bits. Five changes defeated "repeat 01" and produced a better
description of their own. The comparison is between descriptions in the stated
code, not a count of spoiled digits alone.

  [table: three descriptions of the row]

So "is there structure here?" has turned into
a question with a number on each side, and a break-even point you can find
yourself: four spoiled digits, in this code.

And now change the code, because I promised you'd see the numbers move. Say I'd
used seven digits for each exception's place (wasteful, but nobody stopped me).
Then three spoiled digits cost $8 + 5 + 3 \times 7 = 34$, one more than writing
the row out, where the five-digit code charged twenty-eight and the pattern paid.
The pattern stopped paying one spoiled digit sooner. The row is the same, the
pattern is the same, the verdict is different, and the only thing that changed was
a convention I chose. That's Grünwald's footnote, and it's why I won't let you
take any of those numbers anywhere else. They're the price of a description in one
made-up code, a length of a written-down message, and nothing more. They are not
the charge I opened this book with. That charge, as page one states it, is on
one step a machine takes. How long anybody's description is doesn't enter it, and
if the two ever turn out to be related, that has to be shown. Both of them being
counted in whole numbers doesn't get to stand in for the showing.

Now push, because this is where "structure means information" gets born, and it's
wrong, and it's an easy thing to believe. Flip a fair coin two hundred times and
write down the string. Usually there is no substantially shorter way to say that
string than to say the whole thing. You can watch it happen in the little code
above, cut to thirty-two digits: a coin-flip row disagrees with "repeat 01" in
about sixteen places, which would cost eighty-odd bits to list, and even the one
block guaranteed to fit with no exceptions, the whole row used as its own block,
costs $1 + 5 + 32 + 5 = 43$. The written-out row at thirty-three beats those two
descriptions. Another block could do better; a lucky run of coins can make a
pattern, so check it. The general reason long random rows usually resist
substantial compression is a count: put each short description beside the one row
it prints, and there are too few short descriptions to stand beside more than a
small fraction of all the long rows. Nikolai Vereshchagin, a mathematician at
Moscow State University, and Paul Vitányi, a computer scientist at CWI in
Amsterdam, in a 2004 paper on exactly this question, put it without mercy: every
bit of a string like that is "meaningless information, random noise." Such a
string carries maximum information and zero structure. So in this sense structure
is the part that compresses, the part where the "th" tells you about the "e," and
most information and most structure are different things. Hold that one tightly,
because if you built something that charged for information, you'd be charging for
the wrong thing, and for a while I wasn't sure which one I'd built.

And now notice that the word just changed meaning on you, and on me. I'm pointing
at it so it doesn't slide past. Shannon and Rissanen and Vereshchagin mean
regularity: a pattern that lets you say something shorter. Tarski, a few pages
back, and Paul Halmos, a Hungarian-American mathematician whose short textbook on
sets is coming up, mean a set with named relations on it, and those two are not
the same thing. You can run the test yourself: number a thousand objects, flip a
coin for each, heads goes in group one, tails in group two. The result is a
perfectly good sorting in the set theorist's sense, every object in exactly one
group. And the list of who went where is a thousand coin flips, which is to say it
compresses about as well as a thousand coin flips: typically very little under a
code fixed before the tosses. Feed it to a code built like the little one, with
the fields widened to fit a thousand, and you get the coin-flip row again, only
longer: typically most of the assignment still has to be written down. A lucky
pattern can save some of it; the coin tosses alone do not guarantee there is none.
By the set theorist's rule, the sorting is the structure, and it's every bit as
much a sorting as "evens in one group, odds in the other," which compresses to
almost nothing. The two are the same kind of object with wildly different
description lengths. So the two meanings don't even rank things the same way: one
asks "is there a sorting?" and the answer is yes for both; the other asks "does a
short rule say who went where?" and the answer is yes for evens and odds and
typically no for a substantial saving on the coins. The coin sorting has structure
in the set theorist's sense without necessarily offering a shorter description.
I'll keep both meanings, and I'll try to say which one I'm using, and when I slip
you'll know what to look for.

No place for a grouping

You built Turing's machine on a table in chapter one: a tape, a head, a state, and
a card of rules. Chapter one also kept three claims apart: what's proved about it,
the Church-Turing thesis, and the claim about physics. Every laptop and phone and
server computes nothing a Turing machine can't. What differs between them is speed,
space, energy and cost. Hold on to that last word. Cost is where this book lives.

Now do the thing I did, and do it honestly. Look through those four parts for a
place that says "these squares belong together." There isn't one, and that's not
me failing to look. Everything the machine is at any moment is which state it's
in, where the head is, and what's on the tape, and the table says what happens
next, and that's the full list. So any grouping has to live in the states and the
table, as a rule, or on the tape, as symbols, which is to say as bytes like any
other bytes. The machine can hold a grouping. It just has no place where holding
one is different from holding anything else.

In 1972 David Parnas, a software engineer, wrote a short paper on how to cut a
program into modules. He took one small program and cut it two ways. Both worked.
But one was easy to change later and the other wasn't, and the easy one could be
"much less efficient" if talking between modules cost anything. His point was that
a module is "a responsibility assignment," a decision about who answers for what,
and how you cut decides what every later change costs. If you've ever maintained
someone else's code, you've paid for their cut the way Parnas said you would,
probably at two in the morning. What survives the push is that a division is a
commitment: it changes what the work costs, and it is no caption, no label stuck
on afterwards. I'd like to say the thing I built understood that from the
beginning. It didn't. I did, eventually.

Laying them all down

Now lay every one of those on top of the others and see what shows through. I see
seven things. They're my overlay, the places where I see the sources agreeing. The
sources never proved it together; some of them never met, and some of them
disagree (Eilenberg and Mac Lane put maps and their composition at the centre;
Shannon's structure and Tarski's are two different words, as I just showed you).
You've pushed on each source yourself by now, so you're in a position to tell me
if I'm seeing things.

A list of seven slogans would be easy to nod at and impossible to check, so I'm
going to carry one thing through all seven and let you watch each one add to the
one before it. The thing is the square you drew for the Pythagoreans, side,
diagonal and all. Draw a few more on the same sheet: one small, one big, the small
one again but tilted, and one stretched out into a long rectangle. You'll want a
pile.

Structure is about relations among things, and the things themselves aren't it.
You see that in the diagonal, the bronze, Leibniz's room, Bourbaki's unspecified
elements, Tarski's named relations and the arrows. On your sheet, what makes a
square a square was never the ink. It's that the four sides match and the corners
are square, and that the diagonal stands to the side the way it does whether or
not any number names it. Every one of those is a relation among parts. But on its
own this first thing is too loose to use, because a sheet of paper is drowning in
relations: this square is left of that one, this one is nearer the coffee ring.
Which relations count?

Structure is what stays the same under a chosen set of changes. You see that in
Euclid's triangles laid together, Klein's allowed moves, Noether's symmetry, and
Frege's direction that every parallel line shares. This second thing gives the
answer the first thing was missing: the relations that count are the ones that
survive the moves you've decided to allow. Slide the small square across the
sheet, turn it, and the diagonal still stands to the side exactly as before; "left
of the coffee ring" doesn't make it across. The second thing is a filter on the
first.

The same structure can be carried by different stuff: the statue in marble, or two
sets that answer the same vocabulary the same way. The third thing is the second
again with one more change allowed, which is to swap the material. Cut the square
out of cardboard, or lay it out in string on the table, and if the relations
survive, it's the same square in every way you agreed to care about. Aristotle's
bronze is one more thing you agreed not to look at, and the wooden sandwich is
what happens when the material was never on that list.

The first act of structure is a sameness that sorts a pile you were already given.
It's there in Euclid's first common notion, Frege's direction, Tarski's one
question, and Russell's letter, with Zermelo's repair that you must start from a
pile. At this point the overlay stopped being a list for me and turned into a
shape, so slow down here with me. Look at your pile of drawings and say: two of
them count as the same when an allowed move, for now a slide or a turn, carries
one exactly onto the other. Watch what happens to the pile. It falls into groups.
The small square and its tilted twin go together; the big one sits on its own; the
rectangle sits on its own. And it falls cleanly, nothing half in one group and
half in another, for a reason you can check. Klein's allowed moves always come
with three guarantees: doing nothing counts as a move, every move can be undone,
and two moves in a row is again a move. Those three are, in different clothes,
"everything is the same as itself," "if this matches that, then that matches
this," and "if this matches that and that matches a third, then this matches the
third." Put the last two together and you get Euclid's first common notion,
"things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another." So the
second thing, sameness under chosen moves, is a sorting of the pile the moment you
ask which drawings it calls the same. Frege did it with lines and parallels.
Tarski's one question does it in the crudest way there is, by cutting the pile in
two. And the pile has to be there first, because a sameness has to be a sameness
among something; that's Russell's letter, and Zermelo's repair.

Adding structure rules possibilities out. Every one of Euclid's postulates rules
one out. Half of English is already decided. A conserved quantity limits where a
system can go. On your sheet: once the small square and its tilted twin are in one
group, you've given up the right to answer any question the moves respect
differently for the two of them. Tell me the side of one and the diagonal of the
other isn't an open question any more. A sorting rules out a whole bundle of
possibilities at once.

Held structure gets used without re-deriving it. You see that in Shannon's saving
in time, Rissanen's compression, Parnas's one-module-at-a-time, and Leibniz's
analysis of situation (reasoning about where things are straight from their
relations, with no coordinates laid down first). The fifth thing pays off here:
the possibilities you ruled out are ones you never have to check again. Know which
group a drawing is in and you know everything the group shares without getting the
ruler out, the same way the row of 01 never needed writing out. There's one
catch, and I'll need a whole section for it: it only pays if you kept the sorting
somewhere you can read it back.

And structure is always relative to a chosen frame, whether that frame is Klein's
group, the vocabulary, the code you compress against, or Parnas's criteria. Nobody
gets to say "the" structure of a thing without saying from where, and I do it
constantly, so watch me for it. On your sheet: allow stretching as one of the
moves and the rectangle walks over and joins the squares; allow only slides and
turns and it doesn't. Same pile, same ink, different groups, because I chose
different moves. The groups were never in the paper. They were in the paper and
the choice, together.

I think the seven hang together this way. Relations are what structure is made of.
The chosen moves pick which relations count, and a change of material is just one
more move. Asking which things the moves call the same sorts the pile. The sorting
rules possibilities out, and what's ruled out is what you get to skip later. And
because the moves were chosen, all of it is relative. Read that way, the fourth
thing sits in the middle: it's where the first three turn into something you can
write down, and where the last three get their start.

That's my reason for starting the next subsection with a sorting and a door across
it, and I want to be exact about what kind of reason it is, because it's mine.
None of these people said "start with a partition." Klein would tell you to start
with the moves, Eilenberg and Mac Lane with the arrows, Shannon with the
probabilities. What I can actually point at is narrower. In the framings about
when two things are the same shape or the same kind (Euclid laying one triangle on
another, Klein's moves, Frege's directions, Tarski's question) there's a moment
where somebody decides what counts as the same, and that decision, written down,
is a sorting of a pile. In Shannon, Rissanen and Noether I can't find that moment
as cleanly, and I'm not going to bend them until I do. So the overlay doesn't
prove that a sorting comes first. It shows me where the framings I trust most keep
putting their first foot down, and I'm putting mine there too, with one thing
added that none of them needed and the toll does: a direction across the sorting.

I looked for a framing that denies any of the seven and didn't find one, which
could mean there isn't one or could mean I didn't look where it lives. If you know
of one, I'd like to see it.

The smallest piece

Now the sentence I wanted to say at the top and had no right to until here, and
even here I have to split it in two, because half of it is a textbook fact and the
other half is me. The piece of structure I'm going to start from is a cut: a pile
sorted in two, with a door between the halves that swings one way. A partition is
the cut with the door taken off, and the partition comes with a textbook fact, so
I'll do that half first.

The textbook half: a partition and an equivalence relation are the same object.
That's Paul Halmos, Naive Set Theory, 1960, section seven, and you could read that
section before dinner. I'll say it in words first. Take any pile of things. An
equivalence relation on it is a way of saying "these two count as the same" that
behaves the way sameness should: everything is the same as itself; if $a$ is the
same as $b$ then $b$ is the same as $a$; and if $a$ is the same as $b$ and $b$ the
same as $c$, then $a$ is the same as $c$. Every such relation sorts the pile into
groups, the things that count as the same go together, and every sorting into
groups hands you back a relation, "same group." Do one and then the other and you
land where you started, in both directions. So a partition and an equivalence
relation are one object seen from two sides, and that's the fourth thing on the
overlay, the sorting of a given pile, now with a name and a page number.

You can check that with the buttons from chapter four, or anything else in a pile.
Say two things count as the same when they're in the same group. Is everything the
same as itself? Yes, it's in its own group. If a is with b, is b with a? Yes. If a is
with b and b is with c, is a with c? They're all in b's group, and b is only in one
group, so yes. Now go the other way: start with any "counts as the same" that follows
those three rules, and gather everything the same as a into one group. Could two of
those groups overlap without being the same group? If something is the same as a and
the same as b, then by the rules a is the same as b, so their groups are one group.
So the groups never half-overlap: you get a clean sorting. And doing one and then the
other lands you back where you started. That's the whole proof, and it's textbook
mathematics. I'm claiming nothing new in it.

And the smallest structure that sorts, by my ruler, the one question, is the same
object a third time. Put a yes-or-no question to every thing in the pile. The ones
that say yes are one group and the ones that say no are the other. Going the other
way, a sorting into two groups becomes a question the moment you point at one group
and call it "yes." The only thing a question has that the sorting doesn't is which
group got the label. So the set theorist's first step and the logician's first step
are the same step, and nobody has to pick a side, which is a relief, because it's an
argument you can lose with yourself for a long time.

Now put the door back. In chapter five you met "at or below," with its two rules:
every place is at or below itself, and at or below carries across a third place. It
doesn't have to go both ways. Take the places that are each at or below the other,
and put them in one group. That "each at or below the other" follows all three of
sameness's rules, so it's a clean sorting, the buckets. And between buckets, "at or
below" only goes one way: if two buckets were each at or below the other, they'd be
one bucket. So an order is exactly a sorting with doors between the groups, and every
door swings one way. Take the doors off and a sorting is what's left.

A yes-or-no question is the smallest case of that: two buckets, no and yes, and one
door from no to yes. The thing settling on one side of a line, from the first page of
this book, is a move through that one door. What chapters one through three found
keeping takes is what it costs to go through it and not come back.

So much for the textbook half. The other half is mine, so hold it looser, because
Halmos says nothing about "smallest" or "first," and I'd be lying if I let his page
number carry my sentence. In what I build, the order goes: a pile, then a split of
the pile with a door across it, then more splits at once, which is an order. The
groups come first because a question needs something to be about, and a door needs
two sides to stand between. That's an order for my construction. It isn't a law that
every account of structure has to start by sorting. You could start with arrows
between the things themselves and no groups at all, and people do, and nothing I've
shown you rules that out. So the honest sentence is: a split with a door across it is
where I chose to start. It's the smallest by the ruler I stated. If you have a smaller
start, by a ruler you can state, I want to see it. It would change this book.

Buckets

So I'll do the symmetric half once, for real, because it's the half you can do
with your hands. Take everything in front of you and sort it into buckets, with
nothing in two buckets and nothing left on the floor, and that's the whole of the
definition. It's the thing you do with a kindergartner and a pile of blocks, and I
am aware that I've just marched you past Pythagoras, Plato, Euclid, Frege and
Noether to arrive at a pile of blocks. Keep the blocks anyway. They're about to
cost you something.

Take ten blocks, and put the red ones in one bucket, the square ones in another
and everything else in a third. Now a red square turns up, as they do, and you've
got your first crisis: it belongs in two buckets and the rule says one. So this
isn't a partition yet, and you have to decide which bucket wins. That decision is
the whole thing, because a partition is a pile of small rulings like that one, and
once you've made them all, the pile is holding a fact it wasn't holding a minute
ago.

The next part isn't kindergarten. Why would that fact cost anything? Watch what it
does to one question. Before the buckets, "are these two in the same group?" means
working it out: go find the first one, go find the second one, apply the rule to
each, and compare. After the buckets, it's a glance: read the first one's bucket,
read the second one's bucket, and you're done. With each block's address already
in hand, and a table that retrieves a label in one operation, that is two lookups
whether the pile has ten blocks or a billion. Those are access and cost
assumptions. A pile with no addresses, or an index that must be searched, has not
been given that same two-lookup rule. What you're looking at is Shannon's saving
in time and Parnas's one-module-at-a-time, the sixth thing on the overlay, showing
up in a kindergarten.

Look at what makes that glance possible: a table, with a written-down line for
each block saying which bucket it went in. The partition is the fact; the table is
one way of holding the fact so it can be read back fast, and you had to build the
table, and you have to store it, and it has to be the kind of thing you can read
in one look (a bucket number, say; a list of every block in the bucket would have
you searching through it again). Anybody who's used a database has met this table;
it's what an index is, and an index is a thing you build, store, and keep up to
date, and every part of that takes something. Take the table away and keep the
partition and you're back to working it out. And sometimes there was never a
search to save: if the rule is "evens here, odds there," you can answer "same
group?" by looking at the last digit of each, no buckets needed. What survives is
that holding a sorting in a form you can read back is worth something, and
building that form cost something, and those are already two different events.
Building the table, storing it, checking that it's right, and deciding to rely on
it are four different things. The first cost question I want to put to that
structure lives in the gap between the third and the fourth. Nothing in the
kindergarten has shown a charge in that gap. It has separated the acts well enough
to ask.

So who, in everything I read, says what has to be paid? I found three answers that
come close, and I'll be exact about how close, because this is the edge of what I
know. Rolf Landauer, in 1961, and Charles Bennett after him, say that any step
which takes two possible situations and makes them one, erasing a bit or merging
two paths a computation could have taken, has a heat floor when it removes
uncertainty under their thermodynamic assumptions. That is a physical claim about
how the device and its surroundings are prepared, Landauer's principle, and in
this book it is always a premise, never something I prove. It's the place, of all
I've found, where a fact about information turns into a fact about heat, though at
room temperature the amount for one bit is far too small to show up on anybody's
electricity bill. In 2012 a lab in Lyon erased one bit, held in a single tiny
bead, and measured the average heat it gave off coming down toward Landauer's
floor as the erasing was slowed, and other labs have found the same since. Not

don't. Is sending blocks to buckets a step of that kind? Push on it, because the
answer depends on what you keep. If you keep each block and write its bucket next
to it on a spot you knew was blank, you've merged nothing; you can read the block
right back off the pair. You have spent something, though: the blank spot the
label now sits on, which the machine hadn't used before, and any heat from the
writing depends on its implementation. Bennett's whole point in his 1973 paper was
that a computation which keeps its inputs, and keeps enough of its working to
retrace its steps, can be run without any merging step at all, and it pays for
that by taking fresh states. If instead you throw the block away and keep only the
bucket label, then two different blocks can leave the same label behind, and that
is a many-to-one step, the kind Landauer charges for. Both are things people call
"sorting into buckets." Only the second loses a distinction. So the bucket by
itself doesn't hand you Landauer's heat. The forgetting does, when there is
forgetting, and keeping instead is paid for with fresh states. Whether a given
machine forgets is a question about that machine. It has to be answered on the
page, and I won't wave at it here. Rissanen says a model is paid for in the bits
it takes to state, and Grünwald says the price depends on the code. Parnas says a
bad cut is paid for later, at two in the morning. And that's where it stops.
Rovelli, back in chapter six, prices a choice that sticks, and his choice is one
nobody could predict, which that section showed is a different act. I couldn't
find anyone who says that the moment you vouch for a grouping, the moment you
stand behind "these belong together," has a price of its own. Just arranging them
that way is a different act. That could mean nobody says it. It could mean I
didn't find the person who does. I honestly can't tell you which.

So that's where I am at the end of one word. A grouping, the act of making it, the
information kept about it, and a commitment to rely on it are different things.
The blocks let you separate them with your hands. Now take them down to the
smallest case there is, a bucket with two sides, one block, and a ruling that
sticks, which is a thing settling on one side of a line and staying there, the
case the first chapters started from and showed what keeping it takes. The construction I will
study keeps selected distinctions in a state. Some are there from the start. Some
are newly recorded. Those are different cases: a box that already says YES can sit
there forever under a do-nothing rule, with neither a merge nor fresh states. The count in
chapters one through three starts with NO and follows a new YES that stays under a fixed total
rule. That is the case to which its merge-or-fresh conclusion applies. A grouping
by itself does not supply that run or its price.
