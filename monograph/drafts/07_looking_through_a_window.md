Looking through a window

Page one ended on two sheets of paper. One person sorted a list and checked it.
The other copied a sorted list off somebody else. Same numbers, same order, and
nothing on either sheet tells you which one was checked.

The obvious answer is: look harder. This chapter is about why looking harder
doesn't help, exactly when it doesn't, and what does.


Two boxes under a camera

Put two boxes on a table. Each one has tiles on top, and the tiles spell out the
same answer. On the side of one box, a stamp is pressed. On the other, it isn't.
Hang a camera straight above them and take a photograph. The two photographs are
identical.

Now hand me the photographs and ask which box was stamped. Take a better
photograph. Blow it up. Count every tile. None of that gets the side of the box
into the picture. The difference is real; it's on the box. It just isn't in what
you handed me.

That's the thing I want a word for, and the word is window. A window doesn't see
anything. It's a viewing port, an opening that faces one way and narrows what you
can take in from where you're standing. The camera above the boxes is one: it
faces down, so the tops come through and the sides don't. A display is one. So is
a printout, or anything else that lets part of a machine through to you. And behind
any one picture there's a pile: every full state of the boxes that would give
exactly that picture. The tiles can agree while the boxes differ in whether they
were stamped, in what checks were run, in what was erased along the way. All of
that sits in the pile behind one photograph. There could be two states in the
pile, or a million, or endlessly many.


A cave, and the people who got good at it

This one is Plato's, from the seventh book of the Republic. Socrates describes
prisoners chained in a cave since childhood, facing a wall, with a fire behind
them and people carrying objects along a walkway between the fire and their
backs. All the prisoners have ever seen is shadows on the wall. To them, he says,
the truth would be nothing but the shadows. Later in the story Socrates reads it
out himself: the cave is the world we see, and the climb out is the soul's climb
toward what's really there.

The part I need comes a little before that. One prisoner gets freed and taken up
into daylight, and he thinks back to the cave. The prisoners, Socrates says, hand
out honours to whoever is quickest at spotting the shadows as they pass and best
at guessing which comes next. Read that twice. The prisoners get good at it. They
build a whole science of shadows, with experts and prizes, and it works, on
shadows. What they can't do is say anything about a carried object that leaves no
mark on the wall. That isn't because they're stupid. The information isn't on the
wall. Skill inside a window doesn't reach past the window, no matter how much
skill.


The shadow of a cube

Try this one tonight with a lamp and a wall. Keep the lamp well back. Hold a cube
face-on to the wall and mark the outline of its shadow. Then hold up a square
card in its place and move it until the outlines match. Show a friend only the
outline. They can't tell you whether the thing had thickness. Ask them, and
they'll tell you, truthfully, that they can't know, and that staring at the
square won't change it.

Now turn each one a quarter turn. The card's shadow becomes a thin line. The
cube's shadow stays a square. Now your friend knows. What changed wasn't how
clever they were. It was the window. A second look from a different angle is a
bigger window, and what paid for it was the turn.

Edwin Abbott wrote a whole novel about a window like this in 1884: Flatland. The
people of Flatland live in a flat world and never see a shape. Put a coin flat on
a table and lower your eye to the edge, and the coin turns into a line. That's all
the view there is. What gives a Flatlander anything more is fog: a near line is
bright, and a line running off into the fog goes dim, so a trained eye can read a
triangle or a pentagon off how fast the brightness fades. The training takes time
and money, and in the story only people with money get it. The window got bigger,
and it was paid for.

Then a sphere passes through the narrator's flat world. What he sees is a line
whose ends fade wrong, changing size in a way no flat shape could. He only works
out it's a circle by feeling it, and the circle grows from a point to its widest
and shrinks back to nothing as the sphere passes through. From one slice he can't
put a sphere together. From the slices one after another, seen and felt and
argued over, he starts to. The way out of a window is never "think harder." It's
always a different window, one you can tell two things apart through when you
couldn't through the old one. Every time, in every story here, that cost something: a turn, a second
look, training, time.

Who was Edwin Abbott? Not a mathematician. He was headmaster of the City of London
School for twenty-four years, a clergyman, and the author of more than forty books,
a Shakespeare grammar among them. He put Flatland out under the name "A Square."
His full name was Edwin Abbott Abbott, two A's: his father, Edwin Abbott, had married
his own first cousin, Jane Abbott. Is the pen name a joke on that, A squared? People
say so. The earliest I found is Rudy Rucker in 1984, who says it "seems possible" he
was nicknamed that; Ian Stewart in 2002 says he "might have been"; and William
Lindgren and Thomas Banchoff, in their 2009 edition, call it a pun on his initials
outright, EAA, E A squared. None of them quotes Abbott saying so. So it's a reading of
his name, not his word. The part I like best comes
from the sketch of his life in the MacTutor archive of mathematicians' lives: Flatland, by far
the most read thing he wrote, isn't mentioned at all in his obituary in the London
Times. The official record of the man was a window too. Through it you got the
headmaster and the grammarian, and not the one book people still read.

A video about that book is where the hunch behind this one started. That story is
in the afterword.


The whole fact

Here's the whole thing, and it's small.

You've got the states, the real things. You've got a window: for each state, it
lets through some part of it, and that part is all you get. And you've got a question about the state: give
it a state and it hands back an answer. What you want is a reader: something that
takes what you can see and gives the right answer to the question, every time.

When does one exist?

Take two states that give the same picture. The reader sees the same picture for
both, so it gives the same answer for both. If the question has different answers
on those two states, the reader is wrong on one of them, and there's nothing it
can do about it, because it never saw the difference. So a reader can exist only
if the question gives the same answer on every state in the same pile.

And the other way round: if the question does give one answer per pile, a reader
exists. For each picture, answer whatever the question says about any state behind
it; since they all agree, it doesn't matter which one. Existing isn't the same as
being easy to find, though. To work out the answer for a picture you still have to
find a state behind it, and on some windows that search is hard, or endless.

Try the smallest case by hand. A state is a position, 0 or 1, plus a hidden flag,
up or down. Through the window you get only the position. Start at position 0 with the flag
down. One move goes to position 1 and puts the flag up. Another move goes to
position 1 and leaves the flag down. Through the window, both look like position 1. Now write down every
possible reader from "what I see" to "is the flag up." There are exactly four:
from two positions to two answers. Check each one against both results. None gets
both right, because both show position 1 and one has the flag up and one doesn't.
There's no fifth reader hiding anywhere.


People who found this on their own

The fact is so plain that it got found separately in every field that has to deal
with seeing part of something. Laying them side by side is the kind of evidence I
trust: people who never met kept walking into the same wall.

In 1956 Edward Moore, working on the small switching machines that became
computer hardware, asked when somebody who can only feed a machine inputs and
watch its outputs can tell two of its inside states apart. He made "can't tell
apart from outside" precise, and found a sting: even when every pair of states can
be told apart by some experiment, there are machines where no single experiment
tells you which state you started in. What you can always do is find out which
state you're in now.

In 1960 Rudolf Kalman asked the same question for systems that change smoothly,
called it observability, and gave a test you can compute. His unobservable states
are exactly a pile: states whose difference never reaches the output, however long
you watch. Try it with two toy cars on identical roads, a mile apart. Give them
the same speed at every moment, and let nothing about the road depend on the mile
marker. Their speedometers tell the same story forever. No amount of rereading
that story gives you the starting position. A landmark, or a hill at a known
spot, would.

Where did Kalman's filter go first? Toward the Moon. In the autumn of 1960 Kalman
visited Stanley Schmidt at NASA's Ames Research Center, not knowing what Schmidt's
group was working on. They were stuck on how a spacecraft going around the Moon
could work out where it was from a few angles its crew could measure through a
sight. That's a window problem with a capsule in it: the state is position and
speed, the view is a handful of angles, and the job is a running best guess from a
string of views. With real work, his method fit, and it went into the studies for
navigating Apollo. Leonard McGee and Schmidt, telling the story in 1985, called it a
fortunate sequence of events. The filter didn't make the angles show more. It used
how the spacecraft moves, plus a string of noisy views, to estimate what the views
alone couldn't pin down.

In 1977 Patrick and Radhia Cousot built a whole way of analysing programs on
throwing information away on purpose: run the program on rough descriptions of its
values instead of the values themselves, and only accept answers that are true for
everything the rough description could stand for. If all you know is that a number
is between minus one and one, you know its square is between zero and one. You
don't know which square. In 1970 Edgar Codd, inventing the relational database,
gave users views, deliberately partial windows onto the stored data, and in 1981
François Bancilhon and Nicolas Spyratos worked out the problem from the other side:
if a user changes what they see through a view, which of the many stored states
behind it should the database move to? Several are legitimate. That's the pile,
seen from the writing side.

Two more fields have it as a founding idea. In the logic of knowledge, what an
agent knows is modelled by sorting the possible states into piles it can't tell
apart, and it knows a fact exactly when the fact holds everywhere in its pile. The
economist Robert Aumann used that in 1976 to prove that two people who start from
the same beliefs, and whose estimates are common knowledge between them, each
knowing the other's, knowing the other knows, and so on without end, can't agree to
disagree, and Ronald Fagin, Joseph Halpern, Yoram Moses and Moshe Vardi built a
whole logic on the same piles in 1995. And in computer security, Joseph Goguen and
José Meseguer defined noninterference in 1982: one group of users doesn't interfere
with another when nothing the first group does changes what the second can see.
David Parnas, whose cut of a program into modules comes up later, hid things on
purpose: a module is defined by the decision it hides from whoever uses it.

There are more. Oliver Kosut and Jörg Kliewer asked in 2018 when a receiver, with
a possible attacker on the line and no shared key, can always either get the
message right or notice the attacker, and the answer turns on whether the attacker
can make what the receiver sees look exactly like an honest message. That's the
pile with the probabilities taken out. And in 2022 Anvith Thudi, Hengrui Jia, Ilia
Shumailov and Nicolas Papernot asked how a company could prove a trained model had
forgotten someone's data, and found the same finished model can come out of
different training sets, so the model alone can't show whether a point was used.
The only thing you can check is the procedure that was run. Same wall: the finished
state is a window, and what happened is in the run.


Notes passed across a room

Philosophers who study being told things hit the same wall from the other side.
Read one after another they blurred together for me, until I built a picture of
where each one stands.

Somebody passes you a note across a room. It says the answer to question seven is
42. What crossed the room? Ink. Whatever made the writer believe 42 stayed on their
side: the working-out, how careful they were, whether they knew or guessed. Pull
that apart and there are five things in play: the claim on the paper, the writer's
head, whatever got the writer there, the trip across the room, and your head at the
other end. Every position I found puts its weight on one of those five.

The two ends are John Locke and Tyler Burge. Locke, in 1690, says believing
something because someone with authority said it isn't knowing it: if the reason
didn't cross the room, you don't have it. Burge, in 1993, says you're entitled to
accept what you're told, if it's presented as true and makes sense to you, unless
you have a reason not to. I get the pull of Burge; nobody could get through a day
checking every note. But a default anybody can trigger by writing something
sensible is a record nobody paid for, the thing this book keeps refusing. So on that
dial this book sits at Locke's end, and staying there means carrying the check
across the room every time.

Jennifer Lackey, in 2008, cuts the wire between the writer's head and what
arrives: a teacher who doesn't believe the theory she's required to teach, and
students who come away knowing it anyway. Sanford Goldberg, in 2010, puts the
weight on the process behind the writer's word, machinery the reader can't see. I'm
not comfortable with that, and that discomfort is why this book exists: machinery
you can't see is machinery you can't check. Christoph Jäger and Nicholas Shackel
built a model in 2025 where the writer knowing is neither needed nor enough for the
reader to know, landing where Lackey's teacher did from a completely different
direction. Wesley Holliday, in 2009, put trust into a logic of changing your mind,
with a graph of whose word each person takes. I've only read a summary of
Holliday's paper, so I'm taking that on somebody's word, which is a funny thing to
admit in this paragraph. And Michael Burrows, Martín Abadi and Roger Needham, in
their 1990 logic of authentication, said: if I believe an authority has the say
over a statement, and I believe the authority holds it, I believe it. That's
Burge's default with one change: you named the authority before the note arrived.

Kenny Easwaran, in 2009, went after what crosses the room itself. Why do
mathematicians accept a long proof, even one partly done by a computer, and turn
down a proof based on chance that can be far more certain? His answer is a
property he calls transferability. A step-by-step proof puts the reason on the
paper: every step crosses the room, so you can learn from the writer without
trusting the writer. A proof based on chance leaves the reason behind and sends
only the writer's word about what the dice did. That's the cleanest statement I
found of what a checked record should do: make the reason cross the room, and run
the check on your own side. That reading is mine.


Slips handed out at desks

People who build systems out of many computers hit the wall too, and one pair
measured how far it goes. Picture people at desks, working from one rulebook and
passing slips to each other. Nobody sees into anybody's head; all anyone gets is
the slips. Somebody may be cheating. What can the honest ones do about it with
nothing but slips?

Andreas Haeberlen and Petr Kuznetsov asked in 2009 which kinds of cheating an
outside observer can catch at all. As I read them, it depends on whether the
cheating ever shows up in a slip. A cheat who breaks the rulebook without changing
any slip, or who writes a slip an honest player could have written too, can't be
caught by anyone holding the slips. A cheat who fails to send a slip that was due,
or sends one the rulebook would never produce, can. The first two are a pile: an
honest room and a crooked one handing out the same slips.

Their group's PeerReview system, from 2007, is the ladder up to that wall: every
desk keeps a secure log of its slips, and a checker replays the log against a copy
of the rulebook. That's close to "show me everything," built. Close, because a log
of slips only stands in for a whole desk if the rulebook can rebuild what the desk
did from them, and only if the checker's rulebook is the right one.

Then the people asking what you can say once you're at the wall. Ralf Küsters,
Tomasz Truderung and Andreas Vogt, in 2010, defined a verdict that names who
misbehaved, and showed that being able to check a result is a weaker form of it:
one says something went wrong, the other points at a desk. Radha Jagadeesan, Alan
Jeffrey, Corin Pitcher and James Riely, in 2009, looked at systems that enforce
their rules after the fact, by audit. Robert Künnemann, Ilkan Esiyok and Michael
Backes, in 2019, built a way to check it automatically by making it a property of
the whole run. And in September 2026 Timo Treitz and Künnemann gave the public logs
of web certificates the full treatment, and found that the plain version names a
culprit only if the log itself is assumed honest. Somebody always has to be trusted
somewhere.

Turn that on me and it bites twice. A raise of the stamp that never reaches the
window is the first kind of cheating, the kind nobody outside can catch. And every
verdict in this book is "yes" or "no" about one claim; none of them points at a
desk.


Moves that keep to the tabletop

Back to the two boxes. Give them two kinds of move.

Tile moves move the tiles, and only by looking at the tiles. They cost nothing and
never touch the stamp. Stamp moves leave the tiles alone and can only press the
stamp or leave it as it is. Once pressed, it stays pressed, and pressing it pays
the toll.

Now take the stamped box and the unstamped box, with the same tiles, and give them
the same tile moves. After one move the tiles still agree, because each next
picture depends only on the picture before and the move. After two moves, they
still agree. After a hundred. Meanwhile one stamp stays pressed and the other
doesn't. You can watch the tabletop for the whole run and never see the difference
you started with.

There's one way to break that, and it's worth seeing. Let somebody read the stamp
and choose a different tile move because of it. Now the stamp can signal through
the tiles. Here both boxes get the same moves, on purpose, to keep the question
fixed: what can come through the window, if nothing that reads the stamp is allowed
to steer the tiles?


The clerk with two photographs

Now point the camera at the price, because that's what this chapter is really for.

Say you want to charge for pressing the stamp, exactly: at least one for every move
that presses it, nothing for a move that doesn't. And say the person charging is a
clerk who only looks through the window: a photograph before each move and a photograph
after. The clerk's charge can only depend on those two photographs.

Can the clerk charge exactly? Not if there's a pair like this: two moves that give
the clerk the same before-photograph and the same after-photograph, where one
pressed the stamp and the other didn't. A stamp pressed inside a closed drawer,
next to somebody opening and shutting the drawer. The clerk has one charge for that
pair of photographs. If it's at least one, the move that pressed nothing gets
overcharged. If it's zero, the press gets undercharged. Nothing read off the
photographs gets both right.

The fix is the plain one: put the stamp in the photograph. Then "one when the stamp
goes from not pressed to pressed, zero otherwise" works. Showing the stamp isn't the
only fix. Any window with no such pair works too; a later chapter has one that
never shows where the record is but always shows when it moves.

You can even find the clerk's best careful prices. Put all the moves that give one
pair of photographs in a tray. If you never want to miss a press, charge one
whenever the tray has even one press in it. If you never want to overcharge, charge
one only when every move in the tray is a press. When the tray is all presses or no
presses, those two agree. When it's mixed, they can't, and that's the bad pair
again. Those are "some member" and "every member" applied to the same tray, which
is exactly how the mathematician F. William Lawvere described logic's two
quantifiers in 1969, and Robin Cockett, Isabelle Geng, Carlo Maria Scandolo and
Priyaa Srinivasan used the same idea in 2023 to stretch measures of resources along
a map.

Control engineers have hunted pairs like that for a long time. In 1995 Meera
Sampath, Raja Sengupta, Stéphane Lafortune, Demosthenis Teneketzis and Kasim
Sinnamohideen asked when an observer can always work out, from what it sees, that a
hidden fault has happened. Anooshiravan Saboori and Christoforos Hadjicostis asked
in 2007 the question the other way round, for secrets: when can an observer never
be sure the system is in a secret state? A colliding pair like the clerk's is one
place where the observer can't tell right away. Their versions are richer than mine.
What this chapter adds is the price.

Why can't the clerk just watch the move itself? A clerk who sees the whole state
and the move charges exactly with no trouble. So the problem bites an observer who
doesn't see the move, or who's handed an account of it they can't trust. If the
names of the moves come from a log the program wrote, the program can write
whatever names it likes, and a clerk reading that log is reading one more window,
one the program painted.


Look before the concrete covers it

An inspector arrives after the concrete is poured. The question is about the steel
underneath. On one job it was sound. On the other it wasn't. Give both jobs exactly
the same finished surface, and give the inspector only that.

There's no amount of staring that tells those jobs apart. Hunting for a sound
arrangement that could have made this surface doesn't do it either; a sound
arrangement was possible on the bad job too. What matters is which one was there.
It's the four readers again: one input, two answers needed.

While the steel is still showing, you can inspect it and keep the answer. Or keep
enough evidence to inspect later. Either way, something that tells the good job from
the bad one has to survive. Wiping some unrelated detail does no harm. Wiping the
last difference your question depends on leaves the next inspector with the same
surface and two possible pasts.

For one question asked again and again along a run, what you have to keep can be
tiny. Start a bit at yes, and at each check, set it to yes only if it was yes and
this check passed too. It says exactly whether every check so far passed. A later
failure turns it down for good. Put that next to a stamp that says "some check
passed at some point" and stays up, and you can see they answer different
questions.


Three ways past the window

Say you're outside, and you want to be convinced a run paid. The window you have
doesn't show the count. How bad is that?

Take the smallest setting. A run that presses the stamp and counts one, and a run
that only moves the tiles and counts zero, from the same start to the same picture.
Call the before-and-after photographs the transcript. Both runs leave the same
transcript. Now try to build a checker that reads the transcript and says yes
exactly when the count is one. It has to say yes to the first run's transcript. The
second run left the very same transcript, so it says yes there too, and the count
there is zero. No checker of that transcript is both never fooled and never unfair.
Dress the transcript up with extra parts, and the same thing happens, as long as the
extra parts are the same on both.

Economists hit the same wall: Jerry Green and Jean-Jacques Laffont in 1986, and
Jesse Bull and Joel Watson in 2007, worked out what a system can decide when people
can only claim about themselves what their evidence backs up. An audit can only tell
apart the cases the evidence on offer tells apart.

So what works? I know three things, and cryptography seems to use all three. Each
gets past the window, not past the cost: the run that pressed the stamp paid either
way. And each rests on trusting something.

You can be shown the whole state. Then there's nothing to work out; you read the
count straight off. What you trust is that the state you were shown is the state
there was.

You can be shown a promise: the ordinary window plus one extra mark that's set
when, and only when, the claim holds. Reading that mark works exactly when it can't
be set falsely and is always set when it should be. What you trust is whoever can
set the mark. A mark anybody can set is just another window. Bruce Schneier and John
Kelsey worked out in 1999 how to make a log that an intruder who takes over the
machine can't quietly rewrite, and their honest limit is the honest limit here: it
protects what was written before the break-in, and nothing after. Mihir Bellare and
Bennet Yee had named that property forward integrity in 1997. Point it at an AI
agent keeping its own log, and the line moves to the very start: an agent that
writes the wrong thing from its first step is past the break-in for the whole log.
A 2026 preprint by Laurent Bindschaedler, Quentin Botha and Christoph
Siebenbrunner builds exactly such tamper-evident trails for agents and calls it an
Agent Flight Recorder.

Or you can ask. The outside party puts a question and gets an answer that pins it
down. What you trust is the honesty of whoever answers, because a liar's answer is
another shadow. The refined form is Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles
Rackoff's zero-knowledge proofs, from 1985, where a checker is convinced by a
conversation built to separate true from false with a guarantee against a cheating
prover. Joseph Halpern, Yoram Moses and Mark Tuttle gave them an analysis in terms
of knowledge in 1988. My asking, in this chapter, is one round with one fixed
question, and nothing in it handles a liar. It's a toy of the real thing, and I'm
not going to let the real thing's reputation stand behind the toy.

The real thing even has a cave in it. In a paper presented in 1989 and printed in
1990, "How to Explain Zero-Knowledge Protocols to Your Children," Jean-Jacques Quisquater and Louis
Guillou, with their families and Tom Berson, told it as Ali Baba's cave: a ring of
tunnel with a magic door at the back that opens only for a secret word. Peggy goes in
down one side while Victor waits outside; then Victor walks to the fork and shouts
which side she should come out of. If she knows the word, she always can. If she
doesn't, she gets away with it only when he happens to call the side she went in.
Play it with a coin for Victor's shout. One round, a cheat gets through half the
time. Twenty rounds, about one time in a million. And here's the part that belongs
in this chapter: Victor ends up convinced, but a video of the whole thing convinces
nobody else, because a staged run, where the two agreed beforehand which sides he'd
call, makes exactly the same video. The video is a window, and the real run and the
fake one cast the same shadow.

What would it take to make that promise-mark binding for real, against somebody
who wants to cheat? Manuel Blum's paper "Coin Flipping by Telephone," presented in 1981, is
where I'd point. Alice and Bob have just divorced, they live in different cities, and they
want to flip a coin over the phone for who gets the car, with nobody both trust to
flip it. If Alice flips and announces, Bob has to take her word. If Bob calls first,
Alice can lie about how it landed. Blum's way out is for one side to commit first,
sending something that fixes the choice without showing it, and open it after the
other side calls. What keeps that binding isn't a promise. It's a bet that a certain
kind of big number is hard to factor. So in Blum's protocol, binding is earned, on
that condition. In this chapter it's only assumed. That's why I won't say these
escapes beat a cheater. Andreas Winter, Anderson Nascimento and Hideki Imai worked
out in 2003 how fast a noisy channel can carry promises like that, and Claude
Crépeau, Rafael Dowsley and Nascimento in 2020 how that rate drops when the one
promising can steer the noise.

One more relative: verifiable computation. A box hands back its answer with a short
proof that the answer is right, which a checker can verify without redoing the work
(Pinocchio, built in 2013 by Bryan Parno, Jon Howell, Craig Gentry and Mariana
Raykova), and proof-carrying data passes proofs like that along a whole chain of
parties who don't trust each other (Alessandro Chiesa and Eran Tromer, 2010). That's
the strongest way out there is, and what it carries out is the claim. The history of
which machine ran it, and when, stays behind unless the proof is built to carry it.

Now point all three back at a stamp kept in a machine's own state. That stamp is
bits too. Hand somebody a copy of it with a note saying "it's pressed," and they're
reading a window: the run that really pressed it and a box built to print that it
did cast the same shadow. So keeping the record inside the machine answers "was this
earned?" for whoever can see the machine's state and trusts the step that wrote it.
For anybody else, it moves the question from "did it run?" to "do I trust the box
that says it ran?" Nothing in this book supplies any of the three ways out. The
record here lives in one machine, with nobody's signature on it.


What gets through

Put the two boxes back under the camera. Their tops agree and their sides differ.
The photograph answers questions about the tiles. To answer the stamp question, turn
the camera far enough to see the side. The whole state was there all along. What
changed was which part reached you.

Taking more photographs from the same spot only helps if something different
eventually comes into view. When the two runs give the same picture at every step, a
longer album keeps the same blind spot. That's Kalman's unobservable states over a
whole run.

And none of this is the famous kind of impossibility. Henry Rice proved in 1953 that
no program can sort every program by a question about what it does, unless the
question gives every program the same answer. That's a deep
fact about computing, and a later chapter takes it up. What this chapter shows is
smaller and comes first: hand a reader one picture for two states that need two
answers, and it's finished before any question of computing comes up. One input, two
answers needed.

Which leaves the question I've been pushing ahead of me since the two sheets on page
one. Fine: the stamp got pressed, it got paid for, and you can see it through the
right window. What does a pressed stamp actually tell you? Anybody can write
"CHECKED" on a whiteboard. What would make the stamp mean the list was checked?
That's the next chapter.
