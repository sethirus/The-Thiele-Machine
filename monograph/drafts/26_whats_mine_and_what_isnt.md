What's mine, and what isn't

Chapter twenty-two gave the short form of who did what. This is the long form, and it's the place in
the book where I'd most like to be caught out, and caught out early. For each piece the book could be
accused of claiming, it says who had it before me and what's actually mine.

Two warnings about my own evidence first. "I didn't find it" is a fact about my searches, and the
literature is bigger than my searches. I looked with every tool I had, and some of those tools will
cheerfully invent a citation if you let them, so every citation in the sources was checked against the
work itself or its publisher's record before it went in. Still, assume it may well be sitting in a paper I haven't read. And a proof,
if it's right, tells you a statement is true. It doesn't tell you it's new. A thousand correct proofs
could be a thousand careful ways of agreeing with somebody who got there first.

There's a law about tables like this one, and it's a joke that keeps turning out true. In 1980 the
statistician Stephen Stigler proposed that no scientific discovery is named after the person who first
found it, and then credited the idea to the sociologist Robert K. Merton, who had been writing about
fights over priority in science since 1957. So Stigler's law is an example of itself. Is it a law? No,
it's a pattern, and nobody proved it. But this book has already tripped over it: the pigeonhole
principle wears Dirichlet's name, and Leurechon had it two centuries earlier. And I put my own name on a
machine. By Stigler's law that makes it somebody else's. The table is my best attempt at finding out
whose.

What I compare each time is what a result is about. Landauer's bound is about heat, a physical quantity,
under a physical model. The toll is a floor on one kind of step, the step that settles something more
than it was, in a model I chose. So the comparisons below are about the shape of each result. They
aren't claims about who reached a place first, and they don't show that settling is the only event
worth pricing.

What I'm claiming

Six things, the same six as chapter twenty-two, each with the conditions it holds under.

First, keeping: on any machine with one next state, a run that raises a reading that stays either makes
two of its states land on one or never repeats a state, and with a price on two arrows meeting, the toll
follows. The pieces are old. Recurrence goes back to Poincaré and Zermelo, the pigeonhole to Leurechon,
the price on a merge to Landauer and Bennett, and Ouldridge and Wolpert had the counting with a physical
price on it in 2023. What I haven't found is the toll forced that way on a reading that stays, in a
model of computation, with the noisy and the finite quantum versions in chapter twenty-one.

Second, the cup over any machine: a box that answers only to what the machine is doing and never comes
down is a cup turned by some event the machine passes through. History variables and the "once"
operator are the cup built by hand. What I haven't found is the other direction, that such a box can't
be anything else, with the toggle and the hidden clock as what falls outside.

Third, the blindfold: every machine that steps is a Thiele machine with its record covered and the
price of settling set to zero. Turing's machine fits, read through whether it has stopped, with the
price left out. This computes nothing new. It's a way of reading what's already there.

Fourth, the four doors: a machine that reads its box to skip work it already paid for, under chapter
sixteen's two conditions, pays in fresh editions, a box that comes down, room for every value checked,
or wrong answers, and there's no fifth. Every door has been used by programmers for decades, and the
chapter names who. What's mine is that they're one bill, and the proof that there's no fifth.

Fifth, the exact room counts of chapter twenty-one. Bennett showed in 1989 that a reversible machine can
trade time for room. The counts of how much room it takes to hold off a merge, through a promised number
of repetitions, I haven't seen anywhere. One of them, two levers that keep states apart everywhere,
forever, is stated with the machine that reaches it and not argued on the page, and the ledger says so.

Sixth, the ledger: every claim in the book sorted into shown, chosen, assumed, cited, thesis, or not
claimed. That isn't a result. It's a promise about how the book was written, and every row is a place to
check it.

Under all six is the bet, and the bet is mine: that the price belongs in the step.
