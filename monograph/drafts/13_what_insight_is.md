What insight is

I named the toll "No Free Insight" before I had any careful account of what insight
is. The book has been narrowing it down ever since. Chapter six took looking,
copying and finding out off the list: those can be free. What's left is the part
that stays. So it's time to say what that is, in a way that someone who studies real
insight, in real heads, wouldn't laugh at. Who are those people, and what did they
find? Here's what I ran into, pushed on as I went.


Sultan and the sticks

In 1913 the psychologist Wolfgang Köhler went to Tenerife to run a research station
with a colony of chimpanzees, and the First World War kept him there until 1920, five
years longer than planned, with the chimpanzees to watch. The famous one is Sultan.
Food is outside the cage, out of reach. Two sticks are inside, each too short, but one
fits into the end of the other. Sultan tries each stick and fails. He sulks. He plays
with the sticks. And then, by Köhler's account, at a moment you can see, he joins them
and reaches the food. Köhler's word was Einsicht, which came into English as "insight,"
and the whole school of psychology he belonged to, the Gestalt school, said that's what
thinking is: a rearranging, the moment two sticks stop being two sticks and become one
tool. Piling up tries until one works was, for them, something else.

Karl Duncker gave the human version, in German in 1935 and in English in 1945, five
years after he died at thirty-seven; he'd spent his last years working with Köhler at
Swarthmore. You can set it up yourself: a candle, a box of tacks, matches, and the job
of fixing the candle to the wall. The hard part is seeing the box as a shelf while it's
sitting there being the thing that holds the tacks. And he found that if you hand people
the box empty, with the tacks poured out beside it, they solve it far more readily. Same
objects. What changed was which relation between them was on the table.

I like both of these for the same reason: the insight didn't add an object. Sultan had
both sticks the whole time. The box was there the whole time. What arrived was a
relation, a piece of structure in chapter ten's sense, and it arrived all at once. Hold on
to that, because the thing the toll charges for also arrives in one step, and at its best
it's also a relation.


It isn't a feeling

A feeling of getting closer is a different thing from solving the problem. In the 1980s
the psychologist Janet Metcalfe asked people to solve problems while rating, every fifteen
seconds, how close they felt to the answer, how "warm." On ordinary problems, like algebra,
the warmth climbs steadily and peaks at the answer, which is what you'd expect. On insight
problems, riddles of the candle-box kind, the warmth stays flat, flat, flat, and then the
answer arrives. And the people who felt warmest beforehand were, if anything, more likely
to be wrong. She put that in the title of a 1986 paper: premonitions of insight predict
impending error. That's about how well warmth predicts in those tasks. It doesn't show that
solving something comes with no feeling, or that everybody follows the same curve.

Try it yourself. Write a number from one to seven every fifteen seconds while you do a long
sum, then while you do a riddle, and compare the two rows of numbers.

And the most famous insight story there is fits Metcalfe uncomfortably well. Archimedes,
asked by Hiero, the king of Syracuse, whether a goldsmith had cheated him on a crown, gets
into a bath, sees the water rise, and runs home naked shouting "eureka," I've found it. That
comes from Vitruvius, a Roman writer on architecture, writing about two centuries later. Is
it true? Galileo Galilei, at twenty-two, in 1586, took it as known that Archimedes caught the
goldsmith. What he didn't buy was the method. Water spilling over the side, as Vitruvius tells
it, was to him "a crude thing, far from scientific precision," and he guessed some writer had
written down a rumour. So he wrote a little paper, La Bilancetta, proposing a balance weighed in
air and in water instead, which he thought was probably what Archimedes actually did, since it
rests on Archimedes's own results. Whether there was ever a bath, or a shout, nobody can check
now.

Look at what the legend kept: the bath, the shout, running home naked. The feeling. What it left
shaky is the part that would count as the check, the test that could have caught the goldsmith.
The bit everybody remembers is the bit that tells you least.

So an account of insight that keys on the "aha" is keyed to something the evidence says is
unreliable. Whatever insight is, it isn't the feeling of having it. That's a relief, because a
machine doesn't have feelings, and a word that only meant a feeling would be cheating if I hung
it on one. The version this book can price has to be keyed to something that happens in a step.
The question is which step.


What happens when a computation learns

Keep the board from chapter four in front of you: the NO box, the YES box, the tally line, and
the stamp. Add one more move that does nothing to the boxes; call it WORK. It stands in for every
ordinary step a machine takes.

The first thing that can happen is plain looking. Something comes in and gets written down. A
count ticks. Nothing is claimed about what it means, and the box doesn't move. The demon's notepad
in chapter six was this. The statistician Dennis Lindley measured it in 1956, in a paper about how
much information an experiment gives you, as how much your uncertainty is expected to drop. That's
a real quantity, and it's about what was seen. On the price list it can cost nothing: the toll says
nothing at all about a move that doesn't take NO to YES, so a price list may charge it zero. "May"
is the word that matters. A price list is allowed to charge for looking anyway. And in a real device
a look takes something too: chapter six showed it uses up a blank page, borrowed until the page is
wiped.

Lindley's measure has a back story, and it runs through Bletchley Park. I. J. Good, who was Alan
Turing's main statistical assistant there in 1941, wrote in 1979 that Turing had worked out how much
evidence an experiment should be expected to give, to judge one of the codebreaking methods before
running it, partly getting to Lindley's idea fifteen years early, in work that stayed secret. Turing
measured evidence in "bans," with a tenth of one called a deciban, and the name came from the town of
Banbury, where tens of thousands of the sheets used in that method were printed. And Good adds that
Turing didn't know the measure he was using had already appeared in 1878, in a paper by Charles
Sanders Peirce, the same Peirce from chapter nine, who'd called it the weight of evidence. It changes
nothing here, but it shows how well trodden this kind of thing is. For a century and a half careful
people have measured what an observation tells you, and every one of those measures is about what was
seen. The toll leaves that side of the line alone.

The second thing that can happen is the raise. Some move takes the box from NO to YES, and that's where
the toll applies: that move costs at least one, and every run from NO to YES pays at least one somewhere.
What gets charged, at its best, is the Gestalt thing: a relation committed to, one tool claimed out of
two sticks. I say "at its best" because chapter four was careful about what YES means: the coin is in YES,
and nothing more. A raise that writes down which relation it's committing to carries Sultan's tool with
it. A bare stamp commits to nothing at all, and it pays exactly the same.

The third thing isn't a kind of step. It's something about the price: it's the same, whatever got
settled. Say the YES state also shows something striking, a deep relation, a result nobody could have
guessed, Sultan's two sticks become one tool. The toll charges the raise at least one. It doesn't charge
the striking raise more, because the reason it charges anything only looks at the box before and after.
On the board, the stamp from NO makes one mark whether you were thinking of Duncker's box or of lunch. A
different price list could charge them differently. The toll doesn't make it.

The candle box has a footnote that says the same thing from the other side. In 1962 the psychologist Sam
Glucksberg ran Duncker's problem with money on it: some people were told they could win five or twenty
dollars depending on how fast they solved it. With the tacks in the box, the people chasing the money did
worse than the people who weren't. With the box empty the problem was easier for everybody, and I'll leave what
the money did there alone. Paying more
didn't buy the insight, and on the hard version it got in the way. That's a fact about heads, not
records, and I won't push it further. But it makes a nice parallel to the board. On the board a deeper
raise isn't charged more, and in Glucksberg's room a bigger prize didn't bring the insight sooner. In
neither place does the price measure the depth.

So: looking can stay off the bill, the raise can't, and the toll doesn't charge more for a deeper claim.
Who decided that split? Not the prices alone. The toll prices exactly one thing, the move that settles.
A price list is allowed to put a floor under more moves than that, and allowed to leave others at zero.
The sorting into "moves that settle" and "moves that don't" is by whether the box moves, and that sorting
is mine. The part that's forced is the floor under the move that settles, once you grant finite chairs,
done staying done, and a price on two arrows meeting. That's chapter three. Anything a price list charges
beyond that is a choice, and any real machine owes you its whole price list so you can see which choices
it made.


Test, then commit

The philosopher Karl Popper, who you met in chapter nine, said a theory earns its standing by being
exposed: you make a guess, you try to kill it, and what survives a real attempt counts for something,
where "real" means the test could have failed. His word for that earned credit is corroboration.

Add a move to the board: CHECK. It looks at something, a sum, a coin under a cup, and it can come out pass
or fail, and it doesn't move the coin. Charge it whatever you like. Charging it on the attempt, pass or
fail, fits Popper, because what made his test count was that it really ran and could have failed. CHECK is
the test, and it never takes NO to YES. The stamp is the commit. So the honest way to go is two moves:
test, then commit, with two separate costs.

But nothing on the bare board makes the stamp wait for the check. The stamp takes NO to YES from anywhere.
Press the stamp with no CHECK before it, and you land in YES and make one mark, the same box and the same
mark as the player who checked first. Their tally lines differ by whatever CHECK was charged, and if you
charged it nothing, they don't differ at all. The receipt says a commitment was paid for. It doesn't say
the commitment was earned. Popper's credit needs the test. The toll doesn't.

You can build a board that makes the stamp wait: let CHECK leave a mark when it passes, and let the stamp
only take NO to YES when that mark is there. That's chapter eight's established record, and it's a good
design. It's a design. Nothing in the definition asks for it. The gap between the word and the bare machine
is real, and I'm leaving it where you can see it: the toll prices the commit, and whether a test came first
is a fact about the run, or about a design somebody chose.


What the word means

So here's insight, in the sense this book prices: a distinction made to stay. A move that takes something
from not settled to settled, for good, and that move costs at least one, on any machine with the toll,
whatever it's about and however deep it is. At its best, what's settled is a relation committed to, a
claim checked and closed. A bare stamp that commits to nothing pays the same, and the toll can't tell the
two apart. An established record is one way to make sure it's the first kind.

Three limits, each from a different place. From Metcalfe: it isn't a feeling, so a machine's version has to
be a step. From the board: the floor is the same for a shallow commit and a deep one, so price isn't depth.
A machine can pay a lot to check something trivial, and Duncker's box, a real insight, is one small commit.
And from test-then-commit: a paid stamp proves nothing on its own. Price and correctness are separate books.

Which leaves a suspicion I'd have in your place. Everything so far has been one box on one sheet of paper,
with a rule I chose and a count I added. Is the shape of that box something I picked, or is it what any box
the machine itself turns on has to look like, on any machine at all? That's the next chapter.
