What a ticked box lets you lean on

I keep writing "you can lean on it" and then backing off. Let me stop doing that and
say what it would take. A box went from not ticked to ticked. The toll charged the
move that ticked it. It stays ticked. Chapter eight made it established: it could only
be ticked right after a check of that very claim passed. And the right window lets you
see it.

Now: what is a later step allowed to do because of that? That's a different question
from "is the claim true," and the gap between those two is what this chapter is about.


The coin that moved

Run this on a friend. The feeling of it is the point.

Put a coin under one of three cups while they watch; say cup one. Send them out of the
room. Move the coin to cup three. Then, and this is the trick, put a second coin under
cup one. Bring them back and ask: is there a coin under cup one?

Say they answer yes, because they trust the coin they saw has stayed put. There is a
coin there, so they're right. And their reason was a good one: they watched it go in, and
nobody usually moves coins around. But look at what happened. Their reason is about the
first coin. The answer came out true because of the second coin. They were right by luck.

That's Gettier's trap again, from chapter eight, done with cups. Edmund Gettier's three
pages in 1963 broke a definition of knowing: that you know something when it's true, you
believe it, and you've got good reasons. Gettier took that definition from two
philosophers of the 1950s, Roderick Chisholm and A. J. Ayer, and the only place he mentions
Plato is a footnote saying Plato "seems to be considering" something like it. It's often said
people had leaned on that definition since Plato. As far as I can find, they hadn't. The
Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on the analysis of knowledge calls the idea that it was ever
widely held "a bit of a convenient fiction," and Julien Dutant, a philosopher at King's
College London, wrote a 2015 paper called "The Legend of the Justified True Belief
Analysis," arguing that the older tradition asked for something stronger: a mark on the
belief that can't be wrong. I've read only summaries of Dutant's paper, so take that last
part as his, secondhand.

Bertrand Russell had a related case in 1948: somebody looks at a stopped clock at the one
moment it happens to be right. They're right about the time, and they know nothing. And
1948 isn't the bottom either. The Stanford Encyclopedia gives a case from the
eighth-century Buddhist philosopher Dharmottara: you're looking for water on a hot day, you
see water ahead, it's a mirage, and when you get there you happen to find water under a
rock. And one from Peter of Mantua, a logician who died in 1400: Plato is running right
next to you, you take him for Socrates, so you believe Socrates is running, and Socrates
is in fact running, in Rome, where you can't see him. People in different centuries,
languages and traditions kept building the same trap by hand: a reason that points at one
thing, and a truth that happens to be sitting on another.

What would fix your friend's reason? Lift cup one again. Now there's a new look at the
coin that's actually there. That gives them evidence about this coin, now, instead of a
memory about the coin that left.


Plato nearly had it

The dialogue where Plato goes after knowing is the Theaetetus, and it's named for a real
person, so I wanted to know who. He was a mathematician. In the dialogue he's a teenager,
and he tells Socrates that his teacher had been showing them, one case at a time, that the
side of a square of area three, or five, and so on up to seventeen, can't be written as any
fraction. That's the Pythagoreans' diagonal from chapter ten, done case by case. The young
man's idea was to stop going one case at a time and find one word for all of them. Euclid's
tenth book, on lengths that share no common measure, is thought to be largely his work. The
dialogue opens with him being carried home to Athens from the army at Corinth, badly wounded
and sick, barely alive; the usual date is 369 BC. And the conversation itself survives as a
record: Euclid of Megara, not the geometer, says he took notes as soon as he got home, filled
them out later, and had Socrates correct them whenever he came to Athens. So the book about
what turns a true belief into knowing survives as a record Socrates himself corrected.

In it, Socrates tries three different meanings for the "account" that should turn a true
belief into knowledge: a statement you can say out loud; a list of the thing's parts; and,
third, the mark that sets this thing apart from everything it could be confused with. Each
one fails, and the dialogue ends with nobody knowing what knowledge is, which is one of the
more honest endings in philosophy. But the third one is the one I'd underline: the account
as the thing that tells the real case apart from its rivals. He couldn't make it work because
he had no way to say what that account had to be attached to.

Socrates gives his own reason for dropping it: if the account means knowing the mark, then
knowledge has been defined as true belief plus knowledge of something, and the word you were
defining is back inside its own definition. A circle. Does a check get out of it? It gets the
word out: "a check that ran and found the mark" isn't defined in terms of knowing. Whether the
check was any good is still a real question, and the rest of this chapter puts a price on it.
So the circle moves rather than vanishing. It moves somewhere you can look at it.


Three things on hand

Here's what I mean by being entitled to lean on a claim. Three things have to be there.

An object, kept in the machine's state: a grouping, a list, a count, something you could
print out. A belief doesn't count.

A checked relation between that object and the claim: a check that actually ran, with its
result on the record. "A check exists somewhere" doesn't count.

A paid event: a step of the run where the object was put in place, and which the price list
charged at least one. In a Thiele machine, the step that settles something is a paid step,
because the toll charges it.

Keep the second and third apart, because they come apart. A note saying "coin under cup one,
checked" can be written without anyone lifting a cup: an object, but no checked relation. Now
actually lift the cup, find the coin, and write it down, but let that step be free, so the box
never gets ticked and nothing gets charged. The object and the check are both there. The paid
event is missing. And the other way round: ticking the box costs one, but the toll alone says
nothing about what the step looked at. Payment by itself supplies no check.

And here's the sentence most likely to get lost: nothing in the bare machine makes a later
step read the box. A move is handed the state and goes where its rule says, and its rule is
allowed to ignore the box completely. So when I say a run is entitled to lean on a claim, I
mean what somebody checking the run afterwards may conclude. The machine doesn't have to act
on it, and unless somebody builds it to, it won't.

Line up the three things this book has called by related names, so they don't blur:

The bare toll: a paid move from not done to done. Nothing says a check happened at all.

Entitlement: an object, a check that ran with its result on the record, and a paid event.
Nothing yet says the check was about this very claim.

An established record: from a clean start, it goes up only right after a check of the same
property, on the same thing, with nothing changed in between, and the raise pays.

Even that last one is missing two things. Whether the check is honest is a fact about the
checker, not something the record holds. And nothing asks whether the check could have failed:
a check that says yes to everything is perfectly legal, true, and useless.

Why does the honest checker carry so much? The philosopher Linda Zagzebski argued in 1994 that
any account of knowing that leaves even a small gap between having good reasons and being right
lets a Gettier case through: take a case where bad luck makes the reasons point wrong, then add
good luck that makes the belief true anyway. The three conditions plus an honest check close
that gap, and only half of honesty is needed: the half that says no when the claim is false. A
check that can be wrong, a set of tests that sometimes passes broken code, puts you right back
in her gap, and then the record establishes something smaller and still true: this procedure
said yes.

And who vouches that the check is honest? The record doesn't. Honesty is about what the check
does on every possible input, and a finished state only holds this run. A proof about the
checker can be written down and checked, but the checking is a check too, with its own honesty
to vouch for, and somewhere the chain stops at a proof somebody accepts. In this book that proof
is read once, outside every run, and nothing on the record pays for it. So an established claim
is two things stuck together: something about the run, which the record carries, and something
about the checker, which a proof carries.

The engineering version of this was built in 1996 and 1997. George Necula called it
proof-carrying code: when you send a program to a computer that doesn't trust you, you send a
proof with it that the program follows that computer's safety rules, and the computer checks
the proof that arrived before it runs anything. Not your reputation, not "a proof exists
somewhere," but the one in the box. Stephen Cook's certificates from 1971, in chapter four, have
the same bones: a claim comes with something that's quick to check, and the checker checks that.

And David Hume, in 1748, arguing about miracles, laid out what a receipt replaces. He weighed
testimony against evidence and wrote that "a wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the
evidence." The way I read him, his whole argument is a method for how much to trust a witness
when you have no receipt. The move in this book is to stop weighing. A claim on the record is
either backed by its object and its paid event, or it's testimony from outside, and a run that
leans on testimony hasn't earned it itself.

Robert Brandom, a philosopher at the University of Pittsburgh, built a whole account of language
on two words this book leans on, commitment and entitlement, in Making It Explicit in 1994. In
his picture, as I read it and as his reviewers describe it, people talking keep score on each
other: what each speaker is committed to, and what each is entitled to claim. Many claims count as
entitled by default until somebody challenges them, and entitlement can be borrowed: I can claim
a thing on your say-so and point to you if I'm challenged. Lay mine beside his and the differences
are the content. His score is kept by the other people in the room; here it's in the machine's
state. His entitlement often comes by default; here it's an event that has to happen and gets
charged. His borrowing is my testimony, let back in on a leash: borrowed entitlement is only as
good as the copy is genuine, which is chapter seven's problem.


Changing your mind about the cups

There's a whole field about how a reasonable mind should change what it believes when something
new comes in, and I couldn't keep its people apart until I put them back on the table with the
cups.

Your friend believes the coin is under cup one. They can learn something that doesn't clash with
that, say cup two is empty, and just add it. They can lift cup one, find nothing, and have to
change their mind about a table nobody touched. Or you can tell them you moved the coin. Those
last two felt alike to me at first. They aren't. In one the friend was wrong. In the other the
friend was right, and then the table changed.

Krister Segerberg, a Swedish logician, built a logic in 1998 for accepting something so it can
never be given up. Isaac Levi split taking on a belief into a deliberate kind and a routine kind:
deliberate is reasoning your way there, and routine is accepting whatever a procedure you trusted
in advance hands you, the way you'd accept a reading off a thermometer you've already decided to
trust, or what's under a cup when you lift it. The trust goes in once, before the answer comes. A
passing check behind an established record is routine in exactly that sense.

Neil Tennant and Gösta Grahne I first took as two separate complaints. They're one move made from
opposite sides. The target is the best-known impossibility result in that field, Peter
Gärdenfors's theorem from 1986, that a natural rule for "if" statements can't live with
reasonable mind-changing. Tennant argued in 2008 that it only works by treating some additions as
changes of mind: the friend who learned cup two was empty gets counted as having changed their mind.
Grahne argued in 1998 that it only bites real changes of mind, so the friend whose coin was moved
is out of its reach. Side by side, they squeeze the theorem down to one case: being wrong about a
table nobody touched.

The line this book leans on hardest is between those two kinds of change. Revising is changing what
you believe about a world that stayed put. Updating is changing it because the world moved. Hirofumi
Katsuno and Alberto Mendelzon drew that line for databases in 1991; Arthur Keller and Marianne Winslett
had been working on updates since the mid-1980s. And look at what the friend sees: an empty cup one is
the same empty cup whether they misremembered or you moved the coin. What they see can't tell revising
from updating. That's a window with two states behind one view. What tells them apart is a count of how
often the cups were touched. A machine can keep that count, a version number for each thing, and treat
every write as an update: a write moves the version number, so every fact about the old version stops
counting for the new one.

Alexandru Baltag, Bryan Renne and Sonja Smets gave a logic in 2014 that keeps apart three things
Gettier's cases blur: good belief, knowing with the reason written out, and evidence that settles it.
The friend who watched has good belief. The friend who lifted the cup has evidence that settles it.

Put that together and it says something exact about this book's record. A record that's never wrong
and never corrected would be a stubborn fool's dream if its sentences were about the present. They
aren't. Each one says a check passed on one version of one thing, and that sentence stays true after the
version number moves on. The record can be never wrong and never corrected only because its sentences
have the version stamped on them. Strip the stamps, and the first write would turn it into a liar that
can't take anything back.

Push on that and it gives twice. The record is Segerberg's never-give-it-up acceptance every time, with
no changing its mind in it. If a check turns out dishonest, the only fix that fits a record like this
is a second record on top saying "withdrawn," the way a list of cancelled certificates works. And the
record is never wrong partly because it only talks about past versions. Whoever reads it wants now, and
getting from "held at that version" to "holds now" crosses every write since. The record doesn't make
that step for you.

Chapter eight met the logicians who taught knowing to count, Ho Ngoc Duc and Artemov and Kuznets. One
more thing about them, now that a record is on the table. Duc's knowing points forward, at what an
agent could come to know with more steps of reasoning. A record points back, at what was checked. They're
one arrow, read from opposite ends. A check is one of Duc's trains of thought, and what I've added is a
direction and a charge on stopping. Where the charge sits follows from one choice: I want it to bind
every program the machine runs, and a charge that does that has to sit inside the step, since a program
can skip any charge kept somewhere it can write.

Three things entitlement isn't. It isn't permission: a system that says who may write to what grants
that from outside and says nothing about truth. It isn't "it's true, so I can use it": that's the cups.
And it isn't "right by luck": a shortcut that gives the right answer because the inputs happened to
cooperate is the stopped clock.


Count the rivals

Now the number, because a book about what keeping costs has to say how much, and the idea fits in a
game with three playing cards.

Shuffle cards marked 1, 2 and 3 face down. Sort them using only questions of the form "is this one bigger
than that one?" and count the questions. There are six orders the cards could be in. Each question has two
answers, so after one question you've split the six into at most two piles, after two at most four, and you
need to get down to one. Four is less than six, so two questions can't always do it, and three can. Draw the
tree of questions and you'll see it: six leaves, three deep. The number of questions you need is at least
the number of halvings it takes to get from all the possibilities down to one, rounded up. Donald Knuth gives
this, in the sorting volume of The Art of Computer Programming, as the floor for all sorting by comparing:
a tree so many questions deep has at most two to that many leaves.

Is the floor always the price? Try five cards. There are 120 orders, and seven halvings get you under that,
so you need at least seven questions, and seven is enough, though not by the obvious method. Finding the way
is a good evening with a deck of cards; Knuth has it, under the name merge insertion, from Lester Ford and
Selmer Johnson in 1959. Now take twelve cards. They can be in 479,001,600 orders, so the count says at least
29. Ford and Johnson's method takes 30, and in 1965 Mark Wells showed by a computer search that nothing does
better: twelve cards need 30. The floor was 29 and the price is 30. So the count is a floor and only a floor.
It never promises the floor can be reached.

Ralph Hartley measured information exactly this way in 1928, before Shannon: by the number of possibilities,
with no chances in it, just the count. That's the measure I use here, and it's Hartley's.

The same count works with something you can hold. Put three coins under three cups. Each coin is heads or
tails, so there are eight possible rows. Somebody tells you heads under the first cup and tails under the
second. Lift the first cup and compare: if it matches, four possible rows are left. Lift the second and
compare: two left. The last coin could still be either. Eight, four, two.

This is a parlour game, and an old one. In A Christmas Carol, in 1843, Scrooge's nephew Fred plays a game
called Yes and No at his Christmas party: he thinks of something, and everybody else asks questions he may
only answer yes or no. He's thinking of an animal, a disagreeable, savage one that growls and grunts and lives
in London, and the answer is Uncle Scrooge. Most people know it as twenty questions, and twenty is a good
number for it: twenty questions that each split the field in half can single out one of about a million
things. But the halving only happens because Fred answers truthfully about what's actually in his head. Lie
once and the count still shrinks, around the wrong animal. The cups showed the same thing: the four and the
two are real because you lifted the cup and compared. Being told what was under it wouldn't have made them
real.

Now put it on a run. Say a run comes with a list of possibilities still on the table, and a shorter list with
some crossed off. Picture the crossing off as a tree of questions, like the card tree. If the tree is no
deeper than the number of paid steps the run took, and it has enough leaves to cover the narrowing, then the
run paid at least the number of halvings it takes to point at one thing on the long list, minus the same for
the short list.

Chapter six is why the payment has to go in as an assumption. Looking can narrow a list without costing
anything: the demon's watcher narrowed two candidates to one for free. So the narrowing alone doesn't force
anybody to pay. Wherever this book puts a price on narrowing, the payment goes in out in the open: either a
tree no deeper than the paid steps is handed in, or the run does the narrowing itself, with every test step
charged at least one and every test keeping at least a set share of the list.

That last condition does real work, and you can see it fail. Take a list of eight things and one test: "is it
this one?" If it passes, one thing is left. That's three halvings for one paid step. If every test has to keep
at least half the list, that test isn't allowed, and with it allowed, the bound breaks. So the bound is: if
every test keeps at least a share of one over two-to-the-k, the halvings dropped are at most k times what the
run paid.


Narrower means a stronger test

One link here is pure logic, and Plato's third account is the whole of it. Take a list of candidates and
something you can observe about each one. The list's test passes an observation when some candidate on the
list would show it. Shrink the list, and the test can only get pickier: anything the short list's test passes,
the long list's test passes too.

Pickier isn't the same as strictly pickier, though, and the cards show the gap. Take all six orders as the
long list, and as the short list the three where card 1 sits left of card 2: 1-2-3, 1-3-2 and 3-1-2. Observe
"is 1 left of 2?" Every survivor says yes, and the dropped 2-1-3 says no. So the long list's test passes yes and
no, and the short list's test passes only yes: strictly pickier. Now, with the same two lists, observe "is the
first card a 3?" The survivor 3-1-2 says yes and the other two say no. Among the dropped ones, 3-2-1 says yes and
2-1-3 says no. Both tests pass yes and no. Same strength, even though three orders are gone.

What made the difference is a dropped candidate whose observation no survivor shares. That candidate is
Plato's mark, the thing that sets this case apart from everything it could be confused with, turned into
something you can check. Narrow the list and keep one candidate like that, and the test is strictly stronger.
Narrow it without one, and the test may not have moved at all.


What you've got

Put the pieces on one list. Narrow a list of candidates with a test that tells some dropped candidate apart
from every survivor. Find the paid step. Count the charges against a tree the run paid for. Then three things
follow, on any Thiele machine: the short list's test is strictly stronger than the long one's; some step of
the run took the record from not done to done, and that step was charged at least one; and the run's charges
add up to at least the halvings the list lost.

A statement like that is true by packaging. It takes the conditions in and hands the conclusions back. The
right word for it is a checklist, a checklist for leaning on a claim. It doesn't discover anything about
leaning.

And a checklist isn't nothing. On 30 October 1935, at Wright Field outside Dayton, Ohio, the Army was
evaluating Boeing's new four-engine bomber, the Model 299. Shortly after take-off it plunged back onto the
field and caught fire; that's how the wire report of that day has it, and later histories add that it pitched
up and stalled first, which is what controls locked in place would do. Two men died of their injuries: the
Army's pilot, Major Ployer Hill, and Boeing's chief test pilot, Leslie Tower. The airplane was fine. The crew
had left the gust locks on, a mechanism worked from the cockpit that holds the control surfaces still while the
plane sits on the ground. There's a famous line that a newspaper called it too much airplane for one man to fly.
I couldn't find that line in any paper from 1935; the earliest place I could find it is a New Yorker article from
December 2007 by Atul Gawande, a surgeon who writes about how people get complicated work right, and he names no
newspaper, so take it as a lead. What I can find is what came after. The answer was a card: a list of things to
check, in order, every time. The most careful history of it I found is by Walter Boyne, a retired Air Force
colonel. By his account it wasn't the first written routine for a pilot; those go back before the First World War.
And it's this crash, he says, that made the checklist the way a crew flew. The airplane went on to be the B-17.
Nothing on that card was a discovery. Every item on it was something the pilots already knew. What the card did
was make sure none of it got left out when it mattered.


What standing behind a grouping gets you

Chapter ten ended on buckets. The toll never needed them: it prices any yes-or-no question. Here a grouping gets
a job, and an exact one.

Put two red buttons and two blue buttons on the table, numbered 1 to 4, with 1 and 2 red. The question is colour.
Draw circles round 1 and 2, and round 3 and 4. Call a grouping sound when every two buttons inside one circle are
the same colour. This one is. Split the red circle into two circles of one button each, and it stays sound;
splitting can't put two different colours together. Join 1 and 3 into a circle instead, and it fails. Rename the
circles North and South and nothing changes, because soundness only looks at which buttons share a circle.

Now the record. Whatever keeps the fact that a grouping was found sound, a mark on a card or a box in a machine,
is a question that goes from not done to done and stays, so everything the first chapters said about that applies
to it: the raise pays the toll, and keeping it takes two arrows meeting or fresh paper. None of that depends on
what the mark is about. What the mark lets you lean on does depend on it.

Here's exactly what it lets you lean on. If your circles are sound, then any way of cutting them smaller is sound
too, whatever the question is: every pair the smaller circles keep together was already together, and already
matched. And for any grouping that isn't a cutting-smaller of yours, there's a question your circles pass and that
one fails. To see it, find two buttons it puts together that your circles keep apart. Give everything in the first
button's circle one colour and everything else another. Your circles are all one colour each, so they pass. The
other grouping has both colours in one circle, so it fails.

So standing behind a sound grouping entitles you to exactly the groupings you get by cutting it smaller. A grouping
that isn't a cutting-smaller can still happen to be sound for this question; paint all four buttons red and every
grouping passes. But nothing in standing behind yours promises it. Change a button's colour after the mark was made,
and the mark is still about the old colours. A record says what held when it was made, nothing more.

That's the three things from the start of this chapter in their smallest case: the object is the grouping, the
checked relation is soundness for the question, and the paid event is the raise of the mark that keeps it. Like
everything in this chapter, it says what somebody checking the run may conclude.

Which leaves the word on the cover. I named the toll "No Free Insight," and I've been using "insight" as if it meant
one thing. The next chapter says what it means.
