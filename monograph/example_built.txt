What this volume is

The book The Thiele Machine: No Free Insight develops the model and the argument.
This volume holds, word for word, what was written for the book and would have
doubled its length: the proofs of the theorems the book states about its example
machine and its universal programs, the register-by-register programs and the
worked runs, the full algebra of the CHSH check, the book’s own ledger of its claims
and its assumptions, and the works read for the book that its finished text does not
name. Theorem and section numbers cited here and not found here are the book’s.

Proofs for “What it takes to be
certified”

Proof of the theorem “Certified is Thiele-complete plus certification” (book
Theorem 15.23).
Proof. (1) Clause (a) is (A) together with “loaded states are clean”. Clause (c) is
(C), with the three record kinds as the marks. Clause (b) says clean states have the
record down, which with clean loads gives (B), and it gives the earned chain. Clause
(d) gives (D): the claim’s three moves end with the record up from the loaded state
where it holds and down from the one where it fails. Going back, the same pieces
put the four clauses together again. (2) is (1) read left to right. (3) From any state
with the record down, counting A to zero costs nothing and the first mark then
raises the record: a raise for one mark from every clean state, where a certified raise
from a clean state takes three (Theorem 15.25(3)).

Proof of the theorem “CHECK is a unit-cost test” (book Theorem 15.24).
Proof. At a start the latch is down and the table is empty, so the check passes exactly
when eval(Ge(m), a) says yes, that is when m ≤ a; a failing check sets the latch.
The price table charges a CHECK 1.

Proof of the theorem “What every certified Thiele-complete machine has” (book
Theorem 15.25).
Proof. (1) A raise of the record isn’t made by a base move, by (a), so it’s a record
move, which costs one by (c). Running a program on the base is induction on its
steps, each matched by (a). (2) is (c), added up along the run. (3) Clause (b) finds
a CHECK, a COMMIT and a CERTIFY at three different places, and (2) counts them.
(4) is (d): the three moves certify exactly where the claim holds, the claim fails at
some loaded state, and the check can’t pass there because (b) says a passing check
means the claim holds; at the loaded state where it holds, the three moves raise the
record. (5) Cut the run just after the move where the record rises. That shorter run

Proof of the theorem “The language first, and what a certificate then means”
(book Theorem 15.26).
Proof. (1) Take the plain interface the language-first one becomes. (2) The checker’s
soundness and the keeping proof are the ones clause (b) already supplies. (3) Clause
(b) gives the chain; the checker’s soundness proof against L gives c’s meaning at the
state checked; L’s keeping proof carries it, through states where the thing c is about
is unchanged, to the state committed.

Proof of the observation “A clock is a Thiele machine and isn’t certified Thiele–
complete” (book Observation 15.27).
Proof. Every move costs one, so any raise costs at least one: the toll holds for any
record. The machine’s own moves are the two-counter instructions, which gives the
universal base. But a certified Thiele-complete machine has a move priced 0, by
Theorem 15.25(6), and this one has none.

Proof of the theorem “Each clause does work” (book Theorem 15.28).
Proof. (1) The base moves leave the flag alone, TICK never lowers it, the base costs
0 and TICK costs 1, which gives (a) and (c). Through any interface, (a) gives a
loaded state, which is clean, so its record is down by (b), and a move that puts
the record up from every state raises it alone. Clause (b) would need a CHECK, a
COMMIT and a CERTIFY inside that one-move run, and one move can’t be three.
(2) The base gives (a) and costs 0, which with no record moves is (c). No run ends
with the record up, so (b) asks nothing of a run, and a checker that always says no
is sound for any meaning. Clause (d) needs a run from some loaded state that raises
the record, and none does.

Proof of the theorem “The four clauses are independent” (book Theo-
rem 15.29).
Proof. (a) Nothing moves a trapped state’s flag, so a run that uses DROP ends with
the flag down. A run from a clean state that ends with the flag up never used DROP;
it’s a run of this chapter’s machine, and Theorem 15.11 gives its chain. DROP is
priced 0 and read as a base move, so (c) holds, and (d) holds as it does for the
plain machine. A state with the flag up has it down after DROP. That breaks the

                Proof of the theorem “Any unit” (book Theorem 15.30).
                Proof. (1) At unit one every listed price is unchanged.
                   (2) Changing the size of the unit changes no state transition, claim or record.
                Thus the computing, earned-chain and distinguishing-claim requirements stay as
                they were. To multiply the prices by c, read the old ledger multiplied by c too. Every
                addition is then multiplied by the same amount.
                   In the other direction, count complete groups of c units in the ledger. There may
                be a remainder smaller than c at the start. A step adds either nothing or exactly one
                complete group, so it leaves that remainder alone and increases the group count by
                the divided price. The equation for that count is

                    (3) The move for an ordinary counter instruction is a base move and costs zero
                at every unit. A machine charging for every move has no such move.
                    (4) Find the passing CHECK, matching COMMIT and raising CERTIFY in the

Proof of the theorem “The small machine, the generic one and the sorted one
are certified Thiele-complete” (book Theorem 15.31).
Proof. (1) Clause (a) is Theorem 15.3 together with the step line: INC, DEC and
HALT never touch the flag, the flag never comes down, and a start is clean with its
trap down. Clause (b) is Theorem 15.11 with checker soundness (Lemma 15.2),
and a meaning about c keeps holding while c’s value doesn’t move. Clause (c) is the
price table. Clause (d) is Theorem 15.22 with the claim “A is zero,” which holds
at (0, b) and fails at (5, b). (2) and (3) are the same argument with the property
language left open. For (3), read a number as a list in the list code: 0 is the empty
list, and 2x (2y + 1) is x followed by the list numbered y (every positive number
splits that way in exactly one way, and y is smaller than the number, so the reading
ends). The meaning of sorted is that each entry is at most the one after it. The
checker reads the list from the front: yes on the empty list and on a list of one entry,
and on x followed by a list that starts with y, yes exactly when x ≤ y and it says yes
on that rest. Induction on the length of the list shows it says yes exactly when the
meaning holds, and a language with one property has an exact equality test. The
sorted claim holds of the list numbered 18, which is [1, 2], and fails of 20, which
is [2, 1]. (4) is (1) of Theorem 15.25 and Section 2’s floor. (5) The interface over
the language is the one of (1), (2) and (3) with the meanings handed over by the
language, and the checker’s soundness is Lemma 15.2 and its generic form.

Proof of the observation “Every certified Thiele-complete machine hides its
record and its ledger from its window” (book Observation 15.32).
Proof. Theorem 15.25(4) gives a loaded state and a run from it that raises the
record, and (3) says that run paid at least 3. Say it ends with window (line ℓ, x, y).
Two-counter instructions can steer any configuration to any other: empty A, fill it to
x + 1, empty B, fill it to y, then one DEC of A that jumps to line ℓ. Those are base
moves, so from the same loaded state they reach the same window, cost nothing,
and leave the record down, by (a), (b) and (c). That’s two runs, one window, two
records and two ledgers, which is (2). A function of the window would have to give
both answers on that one window, which is (3). For (1), load ( x + 1, y) and take just
the last DEC. (4) is (3) read as a Thiele machine, whose runs are the machine’s.

Proof of the theorem “The small machine runs the eight-state machine” (book
Theorem 15.34).
Proof. (1) By cases on the move. On a live state CERTIFY runs, because a committed
claim is on the channel; it raises the flag, moves the line on by one and charges
one. INC B moves the line on by one and charges nothing, and it touches only B
and B’s version, so the fact about A and the commitment stand and the state stays
live. In a jump, each COMMIT finds its fact in the table and succeeds, charging
one, and the INC and the DEC charge nothing and end at line a, whose slot is a;
the flag isn’t touched. (2) After each compiled move, the state is live again, so the
next compiled move starts with exactly the condition just used in (1). The window
agrees after each move and each move adds its own charge. At the end of the list
the windows agree and the ledger has added the sum of those charges. (3) The
eight-state machine’s own toll (Section 3) says a run of its moves that takes the flag
from down to up is charged at least one, and (2) carries that charge across. (4)
CHECK (A ≥ 0) passes at every value of A and the COMMIT after it passes, which
takes line 1 to line 3, slot 3, with two units of cost paid; then (2) applies. A raised
flag was raised by a CERTIFY, and Theorem 15.11 gives the chain.

Proof of the theorem “The small machine’s merges” (book Theorem 15.35).
Proof. (1) Only CERTIFY changes the flag, and it costs one. The two states with
the flag down and up, and a committed claim, are the same state after CERTIFY.
(2) Take a core at line 1 with A at 1 at version 0, and the same core with A at 0 at
version 1. DEC A 2 takes the first to A = 0 at version 1, line 2, a write; it takes the
second to line 2 with A still 0 at version 1, because DEC on zero is no write. Same
state, zero cost.

Proof of the theorem “A floor with no supplied tree” (book Theorem 15.37).
Proof. Write each rival’s answers on a strip, one box per CHECK in the run. One yes-
or-no question gives two possible strips; a second gives four; each further question
doubles the possibilities. With c questions there are at most 2c strips. Put rivals with
the same strip in the same pile. The premise limits each pile to m rivals. Hence at
most 2c m rivals can be on the original list.
   Now ⌈log2 m⌉ bits suffice to number m places inside a pile, and c bits suffice to
name its strip. Together they number every rival using at most c + ⌈log2 m⌉ bits.
The least number needed for |Ω| rivals cannot be larger. Subtract the inside-pile
allowance, clipping a negative result to zero, to get the claimed bound of c.
   Each CHECK is a record move. Counting all record moves can only increase that
number, and each contributes exactly one to the ledger while base moves contribute
zero. This gives the two remaining comparisons.

Proof of the theorem “Structural entitlement, with the narrowing made by the
run” (book Theorem 15.38).
Proof. (1)–(2) Start with the prior list. At each CHECK the actual run passed, run
the preceding instructions from each surviving rival and ask that same question
there. Keep the rivals that pass. Nothing adds a rival, and the actual start s0 passes
every one of its own tests, so it stays throughout. A missing rival has a first test that
removed it. That is the required failed CHECK, at the stated place in the run.
    (3) Name the successive list sizes q0 , q1 , . . . , qn , with q0 = |Ω| and qn = |Ω′ |.
The before-and-after sizes for test i are consecutive members of that list. Multiply
the after-sizes by q0 , and multiply the before-sizes by qn : each side contains exactly
q0 q1 · · · qn . This is the displayed product identity.
    (4) The share premise says qi−1 ≤ 2k qi at every test. Apply it first to q0 , then to q1
in the resulting bound, and keep going. After n tests it gives q0 ≤ 2kn qn . Numbering
qn final places takes ⌈log2 qn ⌉ bits; another kn bits can number up to 2kn times as
many. Thus the difference in rounded index lengths is at most kn. Each of these

                n tests is a CHECK the run actually passed, so it is among the paid record moves.
                Exact prices make the number of those moves the ledger increase. Multiplying that
                larger count by k preserves the bound.
                   (5) The run starts clean and ends with its record up. The earned-record require-
                ment therefore finds its CHECK, COMMIT and raising CERTIFY. Those three different
                acts each cost one, so the ledger increased by at least three.
                   (6) The actual start remained on every list, so every qi is positive. The ratios
                q0 /q1 , q1 /q2 , . . . , qn−1 /qn multiply to q0 /qn : each intermediate size cancels once

                of the per-test logarithms.
                    To account for rounding, set u = log2 q0 and w = log2 qn . The integer ⌈u − w⌉ +

                each per-test logarithm up and adding gives an integer at least as large as their
                sum. It cannot be smaller than rounding the sum up just once. This proves the last
                comparison.

Proof of the proposition “The eight-start member” (book Proposition 15.41).
Proof. From any loaded start, the first CHECK passes exactly when a is even. If it
passes, the second has room in the table and passes exactly when a ≥ 2. Nothing has
changed A, so their matching COMMIT and CERTIFY then succeed. If either check
fails, the trap prevents certification. This proves the claim for every a, b. Within the
eight rivals, a can only be 0, 1, 2, 3; of those, only 2 is even and at least 2, whatever
b. From (2, 0) and (2, 1) the COMMIT then finds its fact with A’s version unchanged,
nothing having written A, and the CERTIFY raises the flag; from every other start
the first failing CHECK traps the machine, and a trapped machine raises nothing. So
the posterior is those two. The hypotheses: the posterior narrows the eight strictly
(M1); a dropped rival whose run fails a CHECK shows receipts no survivor shows
(M2); the run ends with the record up and its receipts, two passes, are the survivors’
(M3); the tree has depth 2 and the run 4 record moves (M4); two survivors (M5);
and the fibres, the four rivals with B at 0 for the survivor (2, 0) and the four with B
at 1 for (2, 1), cover all eight, add up to eight, and have four members each, the
leaves of the tree, so 8 ≤ 4 · 2 (M6). Then ⌈log2 8⌉ − ⌈log2 2⌉ = 3 − 1 = 2; the four
record moves are two CHECKs, a COMMIT and a CERTIFY, and the ledger rises by
exactly those four by clause (c); and the chain is in the run. For Theorem 15.37,
the run’s questions are its two CHECKs, and two rivals answer them alike exactly
when their a’s agree on being even and on reaching 2, which puts the eight starts in
four classes of two, so m = 2 and ⌈log2 8⌉ − ⌈log2 2⌉ = 2 is the number of CHECKs.
With the two CHECKs alone there’s no CERTIFY, so the record stays down, the ledger
rises by 2, and the index bits are still 2.

Proof of the theorem “A search through n bits” (book Theorem 15.42).
Proof. Each question is a CHECK of whether one register holds a given bit, which
passes exactly when it does; a failing CHECK traps. So the program certifies exactly
when every answer is right, which is when v begins with the answers. The counting
is the count of bit strings: the 2m fibres of 2k values each add up to the 2k+m of
the prior, and none is longer than the 2k leaves of the tree, so the representation
theorem applies with every hypothesis a theorem. The cap is the fact table: it holds
sixteen facts, never evicts, and a CHECK on a full table traps (Theorem 15.16), so a
seventeenth CHECK before the CERTIFY ends the run in a trap.

Proof of the theorem “No host fact on an exact copy of the guest’s counter”
(book Theorem C.1).
Proof. A fixed program mentions finitely many properties, so Q is finite, and the
translation sends the infinitely many Ge(0), Ge(1), . . . into it: two of them land on
the same member. The guest’s COMMIT asks for Ge(m), and its table can only hold
Ge(n), so it traps (or its CHECK already trapped). The host sees one property twice:
its CHECK passes, since the guest’s would, and its COMMIT finds exactly what that
CHECK wrote.

Proof of the theorem “U keeps pace with its guest” (book Theorem C.2).
Proof. The one-step part is a case for each thing the guest can do at its line: stop;
INC; DEC, taken or not; CHECK, passing, failing, or with a full table; COMMIT,
found or not; CERTIFY, passing or not. The block invariants and the case analysis
in Appendix K show where each matching stretch lands. The relation carries the
bookkeeping that makes the next case go through: each count register equals the
number of slots in use for its counter, each slot in use holds (property number,
checked value), a slot’s fact is current exactly when the matching guest fact is, a live
slot’s mirror is the property’s number plus one and a stale one’s is 0, and unused
slots, unused mirrors and the dead end are 0. At loading, the counters and guest
line have their specified starting values, and both tables and channels are empty,
so the matching conditions hold before any step. Now take one guest instruction.
The matching stretch just described ends either at the next matching point or with
both machines stopped. In the first case its slot and mirror conditions are exactly
what the following instruction needs, so you can do the next stretch. Repeat this for
the first m guest steps. Each running stretch takes at least one host step; if neither

Proof of the theorem “One host runs every computably presented Thiele ma-
chine” (book Theorem C.7).
Proof. Two layers, each proved on its own. The bottom layer is the compiler: for
every presented machine, its guest program’s run passes through points that code
M’s states in order, with the guest’s ledger equal to M’s ledger plus the surcharge
and the guest’s flag equal to M’s latch, and the guest’s own flag stands on an earned
chain whose claim is M’s reading program applied to the coded state where the
reading first said yes. Here is why. The guest keeps a few registers packed into its
counters the way Minsky packed them: the coded state, a latch bit, the price still
owed, and a register that counts the reading’s steps. It starts at the reading with
nothing owed, and goes round one loop. Run the compiled reading on the coded
state, counting its steps. If it says yes and the latch bit is 0, run CHECK, COMMIT
and CERTIFY on the claim “reading program r says yes of v,” v being the packed
registers with the step count as fuel, set the latch bit, and take three off what’s owed,
stopping at zero. Pay what’s owed in PAYs. Run the compiled driver, and halt if it
says stop. Otherwise run the compiled step and price for the move it picked, put
the new coded state and that price in place, and go round again. Everything but
the PAYs and the one chain is INC and DEC, at no cost. So at the point just after
the PAYs and before the driver runs, once n moves have been made, the coded state
is M’s state after n moves, the latch bit is M’s latch, and the ledger is M’s ledger,
except that the move where the latch bit was set was paid with the chain’s three
and the larger of c − 3 and 0 in PAYs instead of its price c (or, if the reading is yes at
s0 , the start was charged the chain’s three). The CHECK passes, because the fuel is
the reading’s own step count, and at such a value the fixed checker says what the
reading says; the COMMIT finds that fact, since nothing wrote B in between; and
the latch bit lets the chain run once. The top layer is UP , proved the way U was in
the previous theorems, with the PAY case added: it keeps pace with any guest of
this kind, halting and flag matched, ledger equal at matching points, and its flag
earned on a slot. Compose the two. The earned part takes one more step: the host’s
chain gives a guest CHECK that passed, and a guest run that ends with its flag up
has exactly one passing CHECK, the compiler’s CHECK of the reading on counter
B. So the guest CHECK the host’s chain points to is that one, and its claim is M’s
reading at the first state where it said yes.

Proof of the theorem “The recursion theorem, up to the record” (book Theo-
rem C.18).
Proof. The construction is Theorem C.17’s, with a different evaluator. The first step
is to see that a record is small. From a clean start, a host program that stops has a
record I can read off five numbers, in this order: what it left in register 0; how many
facts the table holds; what is left of the ledger once the facts, the trap and the flag are
paid for, which I’ll call the replay allowance; 1 if the flag is up and 0 if not; and 1 if
the trap latch is up and 0 if not. The record obeys a short rulebook, and you can read
each rule off the price table. A run stops at its first trap, so its ledger is its passing
CHECKs, plus its passing COMMITs, plus its passing CERTIFYs, plus one if it trapped
(only those three can fail, and the failing one is charged). Its passing CHECKs are
its facts, at most sixteen. Its flag is up exactly when some CERTIFY passed. So the
allowance is the passing COMMITs plus the passing CERTIFYs less one for the flag,
which is never negative, and the ledger is the facts, plus that allowance, plus the
flag, plus the trap. A COMMIT needs a fact behind it, so a positive allowance means
at least one fact. A CERTIFY needs a commitment, so a raised flag means a passing
COMMIT, and so a positive allowance. And the channel is empty exactly when no
COMMIT passed, which is exactly when the allowance is zero, since a CERTIFY can’t
pass before a COMMIT has. The allowance counts repeated CERTIFYs as well as
COMMITs; it counts the cost the replay can spend on commitments, not the original
commitment events. Those are the only properties of a record the replay will use.
    The second step is five blocks, one for each number. Each block reads the code of
T, runs T on the number of the specialised program, runs the program that comes
out on x, and returns its number. All of that happens on plain data: the blocks are
programs of INC and DEC, built the way W was, from a finite evaluator translated
into INC and DEC. So if T has marks of its own, they are simulated and never paid.
    The third step is the payment. The evaluator puts 1 in a fresh register, which is
⟨0, 0⟩, the pair of Zero and 0, so it satisfies the host’s one property, and CHECKs it
once for every fact; there are at most sixteen, so the table has room. It COMMITs to
that claim once for every unit in the replay allowance. It CERTIFYs once if the flag is
up. It copies the first number into register 0. And if the trap latch is up, it CHECKs a
register that holds 0, which fails, since 0 is no pair, and the host traps. Everything
before the payments is INC and DEC and costs nothing, and the payments cost what
the record costs, so the ledger is exact. Specialise the evaluator to its own number,
as before.

it. If the guest’s line is 0, or past the end of the program, it stops. Otherwise it peels
instructions off the front of the copy, one per line, until it holds the one at the guest’s

• INC c: INC the guest’s counter c, nudge every slot about c and zero its mirrors,
  add one to the guest’s line, back to line 1.
• DEC c j: DEC the guest’s counter c. If it was 0, add one to the guest’s line. If it
  wasn’t, nudge every slot about c, zero its mirrors, and set the guest’s line to j.
  Back to line 1.
• HALT: stop.
• CHECK p c: let k be the count of passed CHECKs on c. If k is below 16, build
  ⟨number of p, value of c⟩ in slot k of c, run CHECK Slot on that slot, set its mirror

  back to line 1. If k is 16, run CHECK Slot on the dead end, which holds 0, which
  is no pair, so it traps, just as the guest’s seventeenth CHECK does.
• COMMIT p c: look through the sixteen mirrors of c for one equal to the number
  of p plus one. At the first match, run COMMIT Slot on that slot. With no match,
  run COMMIT Slot on the dead end, which traps.
• CERTIFY: run CERTIFY, add one to the guest’s line, back to line 1.
   Why the mirrors? COMMIT has to find the slot holding the claim, and it can’t
read the slot to find out, because reading would make the fact stale. So beside
every slot sits a mirror the program is allowed to read: the property’s number plus
one while the slot’s fact is live, 0 once the guest has written the counter. If a guest
CHECK fails its property, the host’s CHECK on the slot fails too, since the slot holds
exactly the pair the guest checked. If the guest table is full across both counters, the
host’s own sixteen-entry table is full as well, even when this counter’s slot count is
below sixteen; its CHECK then traps for lack of room. So both kinds of refusal come
through.

Reading the instruction rules

The instruction rules settle some concrete questions about running this machine.
Follow them through a check, a zero decrement and a trapped trace.
• The check price is flat. CHECK costs 1 whether it’s asking “is this 0” or “is
  this at least a billion.” The property language is fixed and each property is one
  evaluation, so “pays for its work” here means that flat price, the same however
  hard the question was. A machine that charged by the difficulty of the check
  would be a different machine, and I haven’t built it.
• DEC on zero isn’t a write. It doesn’t change the counter, so it doesn’t change
  the version. That’s the right call for versions (nothing changed), but it means
  DEC on zero is a way to move the line number that adds nothing to the ledger
  and leaves no trace in the record. The idle run in the subsection on the window,
  earlier in this chapter, uses exactly that.
• After a trap, a trace still pays. On programs, a trap stops the run. On arbitrary
  traces, instructions after a trap are each charged their cost and none of them
  changes the core. That isn’t a separate choice. A price belongs to the instruction,
  the same from every state (Definition 2.2), so a CERTIFY on a trapped machine
  costs what every CERTIFY costs, and clause (c) of Definition 15.7 has the ledger
  add it; Theorem 15.6 has no exceptions for that reason. The choice is one level
  up, in putting the price on the instruction and not on the instruction at a state,
  and what it brings with it is that a trapped machine is still charged for instructions
  that change nothing.
• “Checked” means what the property language can say. On this machine that’s
  is zero, is even and is at least n, and a live fact means one of those holds of one
  counter’s current value. It doesn’t mean the program is correct, or that anything
  follows from the property, or that the property was worth checking. So can a
  proved machine of this kind check “sorted,” the claim the sorter-and-copier table
  of Section 14 runs on? It can, through an encoding. A copy of this machine,
  instruction for instruction, with its property language left open, has provenance,
  checker soundness, no forging and the floor of three proved once for every

  three are all stated from a clean start. Hand the machine a table pre-loaded with

  start, on a step that runs as written, with nothing else writing to the machine.

5.1 Taking the coat off

The first executable is generated straight from the formal definitions, the way you’d
strip the coat off a proof and keep the program underneath. The second was written
separately, by hand, from the same description. Both implement the small machine,
the host with a register for every whole number, the host with PAY and the loaders,
and both run the universal programs; their instruction lists the second build reads as
data printed from the definitions, so it checks them by running them, not by writing
them again. The compiler below has an executable runner too.
    Whole numbers matter here. A machine word in one build is 63 bits wide, and the
guest codes U reads go up past 2100 , so ordinary fixed-width arithmetic would wrap
around and lie. The mathematical machine never overflows, so its executable uses
integers of any size, with the arithmetic chosen to match the definitions: subtraction
stops at zero and division by zero gives zero. What sits between the definitions and
the running code, the mappings, the compilers and the routines that read input and
write output, is code I had to trust.

              5.4 From source programs to the host

              Running guests one CHECK at a time doesn’t tell you whether the built machine
              can do anything you’d recognise as a program. You’d want to hand it gcd, or a
              sort, written the way people write them, and get the answer out, and writing gcd
              by hand in INC and DEC is no way to find out. So the last piece of the build

              number variables, addition, subtraction that stops at zero, comparisons, if, while,
              and procedures that return several results, with the rule that a procedure can only
              call ones defined before it, so there’s no recursion. Each translation stage has a
              proof that it preserves the program’s result: the procedures are inlined, the result is
              flattened to assignments and jumps, that is compiled to Minsky’s counter machine

Proof. Six arrows and a runner, each argued below, and the statement is their

   The simulation argument. Take two machines S and T, each deterministic, where
S has a next state from every state inside its program, and a relation ∼ between
their states such that: a state of S inside its program is related only to states of T
inside its program, and a state of S outside it only to states of T outside it; and from
any related pair with S inside its program, one step of S is matched by one or more
steps of T that land related to S’s next state. Then from related starts, S halts exactly
when T does, and they halt related. Forward: start with the related pair. Follow the
stretch of T that matches S’s first step; its endpoint is the related start needed for
the second stretch. Keep joining stretches until S stops. There are only finitely many
steps to match, so this gives a finite run of T whose final state is related to S’s final
state. The latter is outside its program, so the former is outside its own. Backward:
suppose T halts after k steps and S doesn’t halt. Then S takes at least k + 1 steps
without leaving its program, the matched stretches give T a run of at least k + 1
steps all of whose stretch ends are inside its program, and T, being deterministic,
has only one run from its start, which halted at step k. That’s a contradiction, so S
halts, and by the forward direction it halts related to where T did.

one by the orders from card zero; then do card two, using the already expanded
lower cards, and continue. There are finitely many cards, and every replacement
has already been built. The process ends with calls replaced by actual orders.

the called procedure’s block immediately after it, starting at b + n. A call becomes

the caller’s named boxes. A local variable numbered x in the called procedure is thus
the global box numbered b + n + x. No assignment reaches below the procedure’s

The proof in Section 13 uses a program that takes the code of a term and returns
the code of that code. Here’s how to build it.
   Draw the instruction “add one” on a card. Now make a second card describing
the first card’s marks, rather than carrying out the addition. The count has not risen:
you have described an instruction. Describe the second card in turn and you have
a description of a description. Quoting does that second job mechanically. In the
language below, take the variable of index 0. Its code is a tree with three lambda
nodes, then an application of variable 2 to the numeral for 0. Quoting that code
builds a description of those three lambdas and that application; it does not look up
variable 0 or execute the term it describes.
   Nothing in the operation lets a program inspect itself by magic: its input is an
ordinary tree encoded as a closed lambda term.
   The construction follows Forster and Smolka (2017), whose construction for L
has the same three syntax builders on Scott-encoded children, an auxiliary procedure
for numerals, and a quote procedure Q with the same specification, defined by the
same recursion on codes; the self-application that uses Q in Section 13 is their
fixed-point lemma, after Barendregt’s (1984) proof for the full lambda calculus.
What follows is my rebuilding of it, with the reductions written out.

Put a few beans in one cup and leave a second empty. Write a short list of orders:
add one, take one away if there is one, go to this line, stop. A finger on the page
keeps your place. Carry out the orders literally. The question in this appendix is how

    Agree on how the input is laid out and how to read the output before either
starts. One person must not quietly solve the problem while preparing the other’s
cups. The reductions in this appendix concern functions on natural numbers. To
compute a relation R(⃗x, y) means to halt with output y exactly when R(⃗x, y) holds,
and to produce only numerical outputs on halting runs. The machines below are
deterministic, so any relation they compute is the graph of a partial function. A
translation must preserve both the output and whether the run terminates. Its input

7.1 Finite programs made from two instructions

A counter holds a natural number. INC adds one and advances to the next line.
DEC subtracts one and jumps to its named line when the counter is positive; at
zero, it leaves the counter alone and advances. A finite program uses finitely many
counters. An unconditional jump to j is INC r followed by DEC r j: it restores r’s
value and always jumps. A zero test is DEC r to a positive branch that first restores
r; its fall-through is the zero branch. These operations preserve values, not versions.
They are used here for computation, where there are no live facts to preserve.
    Try copying a cup with three beans. Put two empty cups beside it and keep a
supply of spare beans. Each time you remove a bean from the first cup, put one in
each of the other two. When the first is empty, move the third cup’s beans back into
it. Now the first and second each have three, and the third is empty. Try zero beans,
then five. At every pass one more of the original beans has been counted into both
other cups; that is why it works for any starting count, not just the three you tried.
These are the moves behind the copy C ( x, y, t) in the next appendix.

              7.2 A tape in finitely many counters

              Cut a strip into numbered squares and put a finger on one square. Lift the squares
              on its left into a stack, nearest on top, and do the same on its right. Moving your
              finger one place right is now two acts: put the square you were reading on top of
              the left stack, then take the top of the right stack as the new square. Write a new
              mark before moving and the stack keeps the changed square. Move left and the two
              stacks exchange jobs. You can walk the strip either way without losing its order. An
              exhausted stack supplies a fresh blank square.

              with base 3. Push a mark onto an empty stack: its code is 2. Push a blank on top:
              its code is 3 · 2 + 1 = 7. Count seven beans in groups of three. The one left over
              says “blank on top”; the two groups say the remaining stack has code 2. Pop that
              and the marked square comes back. Multiplication pushes and division pops.
                  In general, number a finite tape alphabet by 1, . . . , q, including its blank, and put

              has code n by bn + d; the empty stack is 0. A push is multiplication by b followed
              by d increments. A pop is division by b: the remainder is the top symbol and the
              quotient is the remaining stack. The digits are nonzero, so no nonempty stack has
              code 0. Popping an empty stack gives remainder 0, which is no digit; the control
              reads it as the blank and leaves the stack empty.
                  A tape configuration is a control state, the scanned symbol, and two stacks: the
              symbols to the left of the head, nearest first, and the symbols to its right, nearest first.
              The control state and scanned symbol have finitely many possibilities, so they fit in

              and pop the right stack for the new scanned symbol. A left move does the reverse.
              The arithmetic just described implements each transition in a finite terminating
              stretch of counter instructions. Extra stored blanks do not change the represented
              tape. A finite number of tapes uses two stacks per tape and finitely many scratch
              counters. Halting states translate to halting lines. Induction over tape steps proves
              the simulation; termination of every arithmetic stretch proves its converse.

              7.4 Why halting has no decider

              Write two responses on a card: “If the predictor says STOP, keep going forever. If it
              says KEEP GOING, stop.” Suppose somebody offers a predictor that always finishes
              and always gets a program’s eventual behaviour right. Give it this very card, with
              these instructions for using its answer. Work through both possible answers. STOP
              makes the card keep going; KEEP GOING makes it stop. Waiting longer for the
              predictor does not create a third correct answer: its promise was to finish with one
              of the two.
                  The card can be a program because each response is a finite instruction, and
              waiting for and branching on the predictor’s answer are computable operations.
              Here is how to include the input explicitly. Finite tape programs can be written
              as finite binary words, and a program can interpret such a word by looking up
              the next transition and maintaining the simulated tape. Invalid descriptions may
              be assigned one fixed halting program. Suppose a terminating algorithm H ( p, x )
              decided whether the program described by p halts on x. Build a program D which,
              on input p, computes H ( p, p), loops forever if the answer is yes, and halts if it is no.
              Let d describe D. Either answer to H (d, d) contradicts what D (d) does. So no such
              deciding algorithm exists.
                  Translate a tape program and its input by the stack construction and then prime
              packing. Both translations are finite algorithms, and the translated two-counter
              run halts exactly when the tape run halts. A decider for two-counter halting would

7.5 Seven descriptions of the same computation
Here are the translations behind Theorem D.6. Each one keeps the partial function,
including where it runs forever. The constructions are the familiar ones behind the
results of Minsky, Shepherdson and Sturgis, and Forster, Larchey-Wendling and their
coauthors; the encodings and the reason each translation works are given here.
    Binary stacks. A binary stack is a finite word with push and pop operations,
with a distinct empty case. Encode the empty word as 1 and pushing d ∈ {0, 1} as
n 7→ 2n + d. Division by two pops it, with 1 tested before division. Finite counters
implement these operations. Conversely two stacks hold a tape as above; encode
every symbol of a larger alphabet by a fixed-length binary block. A push or pop of
that block takes a fixed finite number of stack operations, and the control stores a
partially read block. A tape simulates counters, so the translations connect binary
stack machines and counter machines in both directions.
    The two decrement conventions. Some counter machines jump on zero and fall
through after a positive decrement; the convention of this book does the reverse. To
translate a conditional decrement, send its two outcomes to separate short blocks
that unconditionally jump to the intended destinations. In this book’s convention
an unconditional jump is the restoring INC/DEC pair above. Under jump-on-zero
that pair falls through, because the DEC finds the counter positive; there the jump
is a DEC on one extra counter that nothing ever increments, so it is always zero.
So either convention implements the other’s instruction, with the same final values
on the original counters and one finite stretch per step, and the translation into
jump-on-zero spends one counter more. Relabel each target to the beginning of
its translated block. This proves the equivalence of the two Minsky instruction
conventions without identifying their individual steps.
    FRACTRAN. A program is an ordered finite list of positive rational numbers. At a
positive integer N, multiply by the first fraction whose product with N is an integer;
halt if none applies. Counter machines simulate this by fixed-denominator division,
multiplication and an ordered scan of the finite list. For the reverse translation,
assign distinct primes pr to counters and qi to control states. Represent a counter
configuration by qi ∏r prnr . Every translated instruction uses a fresh intermediate

    The language L. Fold two instruction cards shut. One says “put three beans

reading a terminating run backwards gives the evaluation tree. Scott numerals are

of them keeps time, space, or a ledger of what was paid just because it keeps a
partial function. Where I need a ledger kept, I build that in on purpose, as the
universal program of Appendix K does; time and space I don’t claim at all.

              7.6 Searching for a finite computation
              Give yourself one token and run one instruction of the program you want to test.
              If it has not stopped, reset its starting arrangement, give yourself two tokens and
              try again. Then three. A program that stops after seven instructions is found on
              the seven-token attempt. Seeing six attempts fail says only that it has not stopped
              within six instructions; it does not tell you it runs forever.
                  The evaluators used in Appendix C.4 work by that search. A program and a
              natural budget determine a finite simulation. Enumerate budgets 0, 1, 2, . . ., and
              stop at the first one that exhibits a halting run, returning its output. If no such
              run exists, the search continues forever. Encoding a finite program, decoding an
              instruction, and performing one step use the finite operations above, so this search
              is itself a partial recursive function and has a finite counter program. The host can
              execute that program instruction for instruction, using as many registers as the

8.5 Why the blocks simulate a guest
Run a case that catches more than a wrong sum. Start the guest with four beans
in A and run CHECK Even A. Put its fact on a card saying “even, version 0.” The
host copies the value into an unused slot as the pair (Even, 4), whose code is
(2 · 4 + 1)21 = 18, and checks that slot. Put one unit on each ledger.

version is 2. The old fact still says version 0. On the host, each of those guest writes
bumps the slot by one and back: 18 to 19 to 18. The slot’s value ends where it
began, while its version rises and its mirror is cleared. Try COMMIT Even A using
the old check. Both machines must refuse, even though four is even. Both pay one
more unit for that failed attempt. A translation that merely compares the piles at
the end would miss the failure this experiment is designed to catch.

9.1 Systems and their conditions

Definition 9.1 (Thiele machine with a two-place record). A Thiele machine (Defini-
tion 2.1) has a record in any order. The results of this appendix are stated on the
two-place record of Definition 2.2: a set of states S, a set of moves I, a step function
step : S × I → S, a cost cost : I → N, and a reading cert : S → {no, yes}, satisfying
the toll: every move that turns the reading from no to yes costs at least one. This is
the condition of Section 2, called A2 in the table below. Every result stated “for any
Thiele machine” in this appendix assumes it.


Definition 9.2 (Quantitative Thiele machine). A quantitative Thiele machine is
a Thiele machine with a witness, a function W from states to N, and a threshold
N ∈ N, satisfying: (A3) W (s) + cost(i ) ≥ W (step(s, i )), the witness grows by at
most what the move cost; and (A5) a state that reads yes has W (s) ≥ N. An optional
additional condition is (A4) W (s) ≤ W (step(s, i )).

Theorem 9.1 (The floor raised to a threshold). In a quantitative Thiele machine,
every run from a state with witness w to a state that reads yes has total cost at least
N −̇ w; from a state with witness 0 (this starting condition is called A6) that is at
least N.

Proof. Put the witness readings in a row: w0 = w, w1 , . . . , wn . Beside the move
from w j to w j+1 write its charge c j . A3 says that move can add no more than c j to
the witness: w j+1 − w j ≤ c j . Add these inequalities. Each intermediate reading
appears once with a plus and once with a minus, so only the last reading minus the
first survives:
                              w n − w ≤ c 0 + · · · + c n −1 .
At the end the reading says yes, so A5 puts wn at least at N. The bill is therefore at
least N − w. If the witness started above N, that subtraction is negative and supplies
no positive floor; the bill is still at least zero. That is what the truncated subtraction
N −̇ w says. At w = 0 the floor is N. For an empty run the same argument reads
wn = w and bill zero. Nothing here required the witness to rise on every move (A4),
or used the toll.
   A3 and A4 bound opposite directions, and neither implies the other: witness 1,
cost 0, witness 0 satisfies A3 and breaks A4, and witness 0, cost 0, witness 1 does
the reverse. These conditions describe a general setting. The small machine’s own
floor of three is proved directly (Observation 15.21) and never goes through it.

                   Four remarks on the table. First, the recursion premise holds by construction for
               the nat family and by proof for L (Section 13) and for the programs of the small
               machine’s host (Appendix C.4). Second, the substrate used for the diagonal also
               carries a different rule from the toll: the counter never goes down on a step. Neither
               implies the other, and the diagonal uses neither. Third, merge pricing is an abstract
               lower bound on every merging instruction. It is the pairwise restriction of worst-case
               entropy pricing, which is equivalent to the halving price. Landauer’s premise instead
               bounds heat from the uncertainty actually removed under its thermal conditions. A
               known starting state supplies no positive floor from that premise (Theorem 3.17).
               Preparing or erasing an uncertain record of that start would be a separate physical
               process with its own premises; knowing the start alone proves no payment. Under
               the fixed-protocol entropy-balance assumptions in Section 3.7, a protocol merging K
               inputs has a worst input with at least k B ln K of entropy production. This compares
               inputs to one protocol rather than charging every known-state execution. Riechers,
               Boyd, Wimsatt and Crutchfield (2020) derive additional work bounds under time-
               symmetric control of metastable memories. Their low-error bound depends on the
               initial distribution and the memory’s time-reversal behaviour; it is not a universal
               heat charge on each logical move.
                   Fourth, finiteness, because it’s the premise people most often think the book leans
               on, and it leans on it less than it looks. The step law and the first half of merge
               or a fresh state need none: on any machine run by one rule, finite or endless, a
               run that raises a reading that stays up either merges two of its states somewhere or
               takes a fresh state at every step. Finiteness is what takes the second option away.
               With infinitely many states reading yes, a price list that charges only merges lets a
               machine certify at a cost of zero, the machine that keeps every history, the first way
               out (Section 3), by taking a fresh state at every step. That isn’t a way around what
               keeping takes; it’s a price list that charges nothing for the fresh states. And the finite
               set the count needs can be very small. When the record steers nothing and every
               move adds its own price to the ledger, the count runs over one state of the rest of
               the machine, on the record’s own readings, and for a flag that’s a set of two. That’s
               how the machine of Part III, which has no end, still pays for its raise in a merge
               (Theorem 3.19). Applying a finite-state count to a device requires a justified finite
               set of distinguishable states. The abstract theorem does not supply that physical
               premise.

10.3 What I propose

What ties all of those results together is a proposal, and it’s mine: put the relations a
computation carries, and what keeping them takes, into the state and the step. The
items below spell that out, along with one narrower bet inside it, the pointer thesis
about certification. Every one of them reaches past the theorems, and none of them
adds to the physics, which stays in the premises the book names and the results it
cites.
• That the axis belongs in the step (Section 22, Section 23).
• That the usual account of a computation is a shadow of the step, for machines
  whose control never reads their record (Section 12). For a computer with a
  move for each two-counter instruction, under the record layer of Appendix D,
  part of that is proved: the computer is a slice of a certified Thiele-complete
  machine, what’s left with the layer forgotten, and it can’t see the record or the
  ledger (Theorem D.4). That layer’s control reads its trap, so the slice follows the

                  to see computation in general. The small machine’s control does read its own

               • Within the record construction, that certification is the event selected by the

               • That this is a structural account of computation, with a cost model as one part of

11.1 Setting and notation

Start with four piles of tally sheets, one for each pair of settings Alice and Bob can
be given. On each round their answers either agree or disagree. Give agreement a
score of +1 and disagreement a score of −1. In each pile, add the scores and divide
by the number of rounds. That pile’s average is its correlator. It says how strongly
the two answers agree for that pair of settings.
   For example, three agreements and one disagreement give a total of two over
four rounds, hence an average of one half. The four averages make a little square
table: Alice’s setting chooses the row, Bob’s chooses the column. The score I will
study adds three cells of that table and subtracts the bottom-right one. Here are the
names for those quantities.

               11.2 The classical bound
               Write a fixed plan on a card: Alice’s answer to setting zero, her answer to setting one,
               and Bob’s two answers. Each answer is a sign, plus or minus. Before multiplying
               anything, look at Bob’s pair. Either the signs agree or they differ. If they agree,
               adding them gives plus or minus two and subtracting them gives zero. If they differ,
               the sum is zero and the difference is plus or minus two. One of those two quantities
               vanishes; the other cannot exceed two in size. The next calculation uses exactly that
               split.



               Lemma 11.1 (Deterministic strategies). Let a0 , a1 , b0 , b1 ∈ {+1, −1}. Then a0 b0 +
               a0 b1 + a1 b0 − a1 b1 is 2 or −2.

               Proof. Factor: a0 b0 + a0 b1 + a1 b0 − a1 b1 = a0 (b0 + b1 ) + a1 (b0 − b1 ). If b0 = b1
               then b0 + b1 = ±2 and b0 − b1 = 0; if b0 ̸= b1 the other way round. In both cases
               exactly one of the two brackets is ±2 and the other is 0, so the expression is ±2
               times a sign, which is +2 or −2. There are sixteen sign choices, and every one of
               them gives exactly ±2. None gives 0 or ±4.
                  You can also just list the sixteen sign choices and add each one up. Nothing in
               the argument needs real numbers: the signs are whole numbers, so the bound holds
               the same way whether you read the correlators as rationals (what a counter gives)
               or as reals.
                  A fixed card is only the first case. You can shuffle among several cards, perhaps
               using some more often than others. Every card still tells each player an answer using
               that player’s setting alone. The shuffle changes how often each card contributes; it
               does not change the score that a particular card would produce. Since each card’s
               score lies between minus two and two, their average must stay between those same
               ends. The following definition writes out that weighted shuffle.

11.5 The integer check
The machine has whole-number tallies. The three conditions above were written
using averages, which are fractions. You can test the same conditions without
ever dividing: first make sure each denominator is positive, then multiply it away.
Multiplying by a positive number cannot turn a passing inequality into a failing one
or a failing one into a passing one.
   For the first column, the condition starts as one minus two squared averages.
Multiplying by the product of their squared denominators leaves three whole-

               Make sixteen cards, each with four boxes labelled A0 , A1 , B0 , B1 , and put every
               possible arrangement of plus and minus signs on them, one arrangement per card.
               Draw a card before choosing which setting to ask each player about. Alice reads her
               chosen box; Bob reads his. Neither needs the other’s setting. How often each card is
               drawn is the only choice left in this model.
                  Try to get perfect agreement for the first three pairs and perfect disagreement
               for the fourth. Any card that ever gets drawn would need A0 = B0 , A0 = B1
               and A1 = B0 , which makes A1 = B1 too. Changing how often you draw the
               cards cannot fix it. For less extreme requests the question is quantitative: can you
               assign nonnegative weights to the cards, adding to one, that give all four requested
               averages? The following construction supplies those weights exactly when all eight
               CHSH inequalities hold and the correlators are in [−1, 1].

               11.11 Scope of the constructions
               The constructions above pin down the correlators. What follows is where they stop:
               they aren’t a completeness claim for the integer check, they say nothing about full
               behaviours with marginals, and they say nothing about data from a lab.
                   The arcsine boundary. For two settings and two outcomes per side, Tsirelson,
               Landau (1988) and Masanes (2003) give theorems characterizing the correla-
               tors reachable by quantum measurements: | arcsin E00 + arcsin E01 + arcsin E10 −
               arcsin E11 | ≤ π and its sign variants. The characterization holds as an if and only if
               with the completion set and, through Tsirelson’s representation, with the quantum
               set. The point (3/5, 4/5, 4/5, −3/5) is on the boundary: arcsin 53 + arcsin 45 = π2
               by the 3-4-5 triangle, so the sum is π; Tsirelson’s point is on it too. The necessary
               direction holds in any dimension, for any expectation on an algebra of observables
               in which Alice’s commute with Bob’s.

Tsirelson’s 1980 paper gives the bound 2 2 for quantum correlations, and his
theorem says that quantum two-outcome correlators are inner products of real unit
vectors. Landau (1988) and Masanes (2003, arXiv quant-ph/0309137) each give a
condition that, by their proofs, characterizes the quantum correlators exactly for two
settings and two outcomes. Wehner (2006, arXiv quant-ph/0510076) gets Tsirelson
bounds, CHSH among them, from a semidefinite program on the 4 × 4 Gram matrix
of those vectors. Navascués, Pironio and Acín (2007, arXiv quant-ph/0607119;
2008, arXiv 0803.4290) built the hierarchy of moment-matrix tests; their 2008
paper applies a Schur complement to the level-1 matrix of this scenario, recovers
the arcsine condition, and discusses the 1+AB level. I went back to each of those
papers for these points, and that is what I found in them. The results are theirs.
Two of them are proved again here, in finite dimension: Tsirelson’s representation
and the arcsine characterization; the rest of their proofs are not redone. Echenim
and Mhalla give a detailed derivation of Tsirelson’s bound (arXiv 2306.12535), and
exact rational sum-of-squares certificates
                                   √       go back at least to Peyrl and Parrilo (2008).

an integer certificate computed from outcome counts, and the argument from “the
counts pass” to PSD of the pinned matrix and on to S2 ≤ 8. I did not find that chain
elsewhere, and I make no claim beyond it. On the host machine, the same chain
is earned: a raised flag from a clean start stands on a passing check of the packed
counts, and the check passes exactly when the pinned matrix is positive semidefinite
and every pair was sampled (Appendix G, Theorems G.4 and G.5). The converse
direction of the integer check, integers from PSD, is proved there too, and there too
I make no claim beyond the chain.
